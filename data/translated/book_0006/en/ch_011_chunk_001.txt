2 - THE FLOWER OF UTAH  

This isn’t the place to retell the hardships and suffering the Mormon immigrants endured before reaching their final refuge. From the banks of the Mississippi to the western slopes of the Rocky Mountains, they pushed forward with a determination rarely seen in history. Wild men and wild beasts, hunger, thirst, exhaustion, and disease—every obstacle that nature could throw in their way—had been beaten back by their Anglo-Saxon grit. Yet the long journey and its accumulated dangers had shaken even the bravest among them. Every one of them dropped to their knees in heartfelt prayer when they looked down on the wide valley of Utah shining in the sunlight, and heard their leader declare that this was the promised land, and that these untouched acres would be theirs forever.  

Young quickly proved himself to be not only a determined leader but also a skilled organizer. Maps and charts were drawn showing the layout of the future city. Farms were marked out according to each person’s rank. Tradesmen took up their trades, and craftsmen returned to their work. Streets and squares sprang up in the town as if by magic. Out in the country, there was draining, fencing, planting, and clearing, until by the next summer the whole land was golden with wheat. Everything prospered in the strange new settlement. Above all rose the great temple they were building in the city’s center. From dawn until dusk, the sound of hammers and saws never ceased at the monument the settlers were raising to the One who had brought them safely through so many dangers.  

The two survivors, John Ferrier and the little girl who had shared his fate and had become his adopted daughter, traveled with the Mormons to the end of their great pilgrimage. Little Lucy Ferrier rode comfortably enough in Elder Stangerson’s wagon, sharing the space with the Mormon’s three wives and his bold, headstrong twelve-year-old son. Having recovered, with a child’s resilience, from the shock of her mother’s death, she soon became a favorite among the women and made peace with this new life in her moving, canvas-covered home. Meanwhile, Ferrier, restored to health, proved himself a valuable guide and tireless hunter. He quickly earned the respect of his new companions, and when they reached their destination, it was unanimously decided that he would receive a tract of land as large and fertile as any settler’s—except for Young himself and the four principal Elders: Stangerson, Kemball, Johnston, and Drebber.  

On his new land, John Ferrier built a solid log house, which over the years grew with additions into a spacious villa. He was practical, sharp in business, and skilled with his hands. His strong constitution allowed him to work from morning to night improving and cultivating his property. As a result, his farm and everything he owned flourished. In three years he was better off than his neighbors, in six he was well-to-do, in nine he was rich, and in twelve there were fewer than half a dozen men in all of Salt Lake City who could compare with him. From the great inland sea to the distant Wahsatch Mountains, no name was better known than John Ferrier’s.  

There was only one way in which he offended the feelings of his fellow believers: no persuasion or argument could convince him to take multiple wives like his companions. He never explained his refusal, but simply stuck to it, firm and unbending. Some accused him of being lukewarm in his adopted faith; others said he was too greedy or too stingy to support a large household. Still others whispered about an old love story and a fair-haired girl who had faded away on the Atlantic coast. Whatever the reason, Ferrier remained single. In every other way he followed the religion of the settlement and was known as a faithful, upright man.  

Lucy Ferrier grew up in the log house, helping her adopted father in all his work. The sharp mountain air and the scent of pine trees took the place of nurse and mother for her. Year after year she grew taller and stronger, her cheeks rosier, her step lighter. Many a traveler on the road that ran past Ferrier’s farm felt old memories stir when they saw her slender figure moving through the wheat fields, or met her riding her father’s mustang with the easy grace of a true child of the West. So the bud became a flower, and the year that made her father the richest farmer also made her one of the most beautiful examples of American girlhood on the whole Pacific slope.  

It wasn’t her father who first realized that the child had become a woman—fathers rarely do. That change is too gradual, too subtle to be marked by dates. Least of all does the girl herself know it until a voice’s tone or a hand’s touch makes her heart tremble, and she suddenly feels, with a mix of pride and fear, that something new and deeper has awakened inside her. Most people can remember the moment when that realization came, the small event that marked the beginning of a new life. For Lucy Ferrier, the occasion was serious enough on its own, even apart from the great effect it would later have on her fate and on the fate of others.  

It was a warm June morning, and the Latter Day Saints were as busy as the bees whose hive they had chosen for their symbol. The same hum of work rose from the fields and from the streets. Along the dusty main roads stretched lines of heavily loaded mules heading west, for the gold fever had broken out in California, and the Overland Route passed through the City of the Elect. There were also herds of sheep and cattle coming in from distant pastures, and trains of weary immigrants—men and horses equally worn out by their endless journey. Through this busy, colorful crowd rode Lucy Ferrier, guiding her horse with practiced skill. Her fair face was flushed from the ride, and her long chestnut hair streamed behind her. She was on an errand for her father in the City, and she galloped in as she had many times before, fearless and focused only on her task. The travel-stained adventurers turned to stare in amazement, and even the usually stoic Indians, riding in with their furs, softened their expressions as they marveled at the beauty of the pale-faced girl.
