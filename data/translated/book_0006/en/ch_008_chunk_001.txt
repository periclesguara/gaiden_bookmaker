7 - LIGHT IN THE DARKNESS  

The news Lestrade brought us was so serious and so unexpected that the three of us were completely stunned. Gregson jumped out of his chair, spilling the rest of his whiskey and water. I just stared silently at Sherlock Holmes, whose lips were tight and eyebrows drawn low over his eyes.  

“Stangerson too!” he muttered. “The plot thickens.”  

“It was thick enough already,” grumbled Lestrade, taking a seat. “Feels like I’ve walked into a war council.”  

“Are—are you sure about this information?” Gregson stammered.  

“I just came from his room,” said Lestrade. “I was the first to find out what had happened.”  

“We’ve been hearing Gregson’s version of things,” Holmes said. “Would you mind telling us what you’ve seen and done?”  

“No problem,” Lestrade replied, sitting down. “I’ll admit I was convinced Stangerson was involved in Drebber’s death. This new development proves I was completely wrong. Focused on that one idea, I set out to find what had happened to the secretary. They were seen together at Euston Station around half past eight on the evening of the third. At two in the morning, Drebber was found in Brixton Road. The question I faced was what Stangerson had been doing between 8:30 and the time of the murder—and what became of him afterward. I telegraphed Liverpool with his description, warning them to watch the American ships. Then I started calling on all the hotels and lodging houses near Euston. I figured that if Drebber and his companion got separated, Stangerson would naturally find a place nearby for the night and return to the station the next morning.”  

“They probably agreed on a meeting place beforehand,” Holmes remarked.  

“Exactly. I spent all last evening making inquiries without any luck. This morning I started early, and by eight o’clock I reached Halliday’s Private Hotel in Little George Street. When I asked if a Mr. Stangerson was staying there, they immediately said yes.  

“‘You must be the gentleman he’s been expecting,’ they told me. ‘He’s been waiting for someone for two days.’  

“‘Where is he now?’ I asked.  

“‘He’s upstairs in bed. He asked to be called at nine.’  

“‘I’ll go up and see him right away,’ I said.  

“I thought my sudden appearance might shake him up and make him say something revealing. The Boots offered to show me the room. It was on the second floor at the end of a small corridor. He pointed out the door and was about to go back downstairs when I saw something that made me feel sick, even after twenty years on the force. A thin red ribbon of blood was curling out from under the door, winding across the passage, and pooling along the skirting on the other side. I shouted, bringing the Boots back. He nearly fainted when he saw it. The door was locked from the inside, but we forced it open. The window was open, and next to it, huddled on the floor, lay the body of a man in his nightclothes. He was dead, and had been for some time—his limbs were stiff and cold. When we turned him over, the Boots immediately recognized him as the same gentleman who had rented the room under the name Joseph Stangerson. The cause of death was a deep stab wound on the left side, straight to the heart. And now comes the strangest part. What do you think was written above the body?”  

Even before Holmes answered, I felt a chill run through me—a sense of horror coming.  

“The word RACHE, written in blood,” he said.  

“That’s right,” said Lestrade, his voice full of awe. We all sat silent for a while.  

There was something so deliberate and yet so baffling about this unknown killer’s actions that it made his crimes even more horrifying. My nerves, which had held steady in battle, tingled as I thought about it.  

“The man was seen,” Lestrade went on. “A milk boy, on his way to the dairy, happened to go down the lane behind the hotel. He noticed that a ladder, which usually lay on the ground, was propped against one of the second-floor windows, which was wide open. After passing, he looked back and saw a man coming down the ladder. The man moved so calmly and openly that the boy assumed he was a carpenter or handyman working at the hotel. He didn’t pay much attention, except to think it was early for someone to start work. He remembers the man as tall, with a reddish face, wearing a long brownish coat. He must have stayed in the room for some time after the murder, because we found blood-stained water in the basin where he’d washed his hands, and marks on the sheets where he’d wiped his knife.”  

I glanced at Holmes when I heard the description of the murderer—it matched his own exactly. But his face showed no sign of triumph or satisfaction.  

“Did you find anything in the room that might give a clue to the murderer?” he asked.  

“Nothing. Stangerson had Drebber’s purse in his pocket, but that was normal—he handled all the payments. There were about eighty pounds in it, untouched. So whatever the motive behind these strange crimes, robbery definitely isn’t one of them. There were no papers or notes in the victim’s pockets, except for a single telegram dated from Cleveland about a month ago. It said, ‘J. H. is in Europe.’ There was no name signed to it.”  

“And nothing else?” Holmes asked.  

“Nothing significant. The novel he’d fallen asleep reading was on the bed, and his pipe was on a chair beside him. There was a glass of water on the table, and on the window ledge, a small ointment box with two pills inside.”
