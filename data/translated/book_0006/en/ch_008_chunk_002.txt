Sherlock Holmes jumped up from his chair with a cry of excitement.  

“The last link!” he shouted in triumph. “My case is complete.”  

The two detectives stared at him in shock.  

“I now have in my hands,” my companion said confidently, “all the threads that made up this tangled affair. There are still details to fill in, of course, but I’m certain of all the main facts—from the moment Drebber left Stangerson at the station to the discovery of Stangerson’s body—as if I’d seen them myself. I’ll prove what I know. Could you hand me those pills?”  

“I have them,” said Lestrade, pulling out a small white box. “I took them, along with the purse and the telegram, planning to put them safely at the Police Station. It was pure chance that I grabbed the pills at all, since I didn’t think they were important.”  

“Give them here,” said Holmes. “Now, Doctor,” he said, turning to me, “are those ordinary pills?”  

They definitely weren’t. They were pearly gray, small, round, and almost transparent when held to the light. “From how light and clear they are, I’d guess they dissolve easily in water,” I said.  

“Exactly,” replied Holmes. “Now, would you mind going down and bringing up that poor little terrier that’s been sick so long—the one the landlady wanted you to put out of its misery yesterday?”  

I went downstairs and carried the dog up in my arms. Its heavy breathing and dull eyes showed it was near death. Its snow-white muzzle proved it had already lived past a dog’s usual lifespan. I laid it on a cushion by the fire.  

“I’ll cut one of these pills in half,” said Holmes, pulling out his penknife and doing exactly that. “We’ll put one half back in the box for later. The other half I’ll drop into this wine glass with a teaspoon of water. You can see the Doctor was right—it dissolves instantly.”  

“This may be interesting,” said Lestrade, sounding offended, as if he thought we were making fun of him. “But I don’t see what this has to do with the death of Mr. Joseph Stangerson.”  

“Patience, my friend, patience! You’ll see soon enough that it has everything to do with it. I’ll add a little milk to make it drinkable, and when I offer it to the dog, you’ll see he laps it up right away.”  

As he spoke, he poured the glass into a saucer and set it before the terrier, who quickly licked it dry. Holmes’s serious manner had convinced us all, so we sat silently watching the dog, waiting for something dramatic to happen. But nothing did. The dog kept lying on the cushion, still breathing heavily but no better or worse for the dose.  

Holmes took out his watch. As the minutes passed with no result, his face showed growing frustration and disappointment. He bit his lip, drummed his fingers on the table, and looked every bit the picture of impatience. He was so upset that I felt genuinely sorry for him, while the two detectives smirked, clearly pleased to see him fail.  

“It can’t be coincidence!” he suddenly cried, leaping up and pacing the room. “It’s impossible! The very pills I suspected in Drebber’s case are found after Stangerson’s death—and yet they’re harmless? What can it mean? My whole chain of reasoning can’t be wrong. It’s impossible! And yet this poor dog is fine. Ah, I see it! I see it!”  

With a shout of joy, he rushed to the box, cut the second pill in half, dissolved it, added milk, and gave it to the terrier. The poor creature’s tongue had barely touched the mixture when it shuddered violently, every limb convulsing, and then lay stiff and lifeless, as if struck by lightning.  

Sherlock Holmes took a deep breath and wiped the sweat from his forehead. “I should have had more faith,” he said. “I ought to know by now that when a fact seems to contradict a long line of reasoning, it usually just means it can be interpreted another way. Of the two pills in that box, one was deadly poison and the other completely harmless. I should have realized that before I even saw the box.”  

That last statement was so astonishing I could hardly believe he was serious. But the dead dog proved that his guess had been right. The fog in my mind began to lift, and I started to get a vague sense of the truth.  

“All this seems strange to you,” Holmes continued, “because at the start of the investigation, you didn’t recognize the importance of the one real clue that was right in front of you. I was lucky enough to spot it, and everything since has confirmed my first theory—it all follows logically from it. So the things that confused you and made the case seem more complicated actually helped me see the truth more clearly.  

“It’s a mistake to confuse something strange with something mysterious. The most ordinary crime is often the hardest to solve because it lacks unique features to draw conclusions from. This murder would have been far harder to unravel if the body had just been found lying in the street, without those bizarre and dramatic details that made it stand out. Those strange details, instead of making the case harder, actually made it easier.”  

Mr. Gregson, who had been listening impatiently, couldn’t hold back any longer. “Look here, Mr. Sherlock Holmes,” he said, “we all admit you’re a clever man and that you have your own way of working. But we need more than theories and lectures now. It’s time to catch the man. I built my case, and it turns out I was wrong. Young Charpentier couldn’t have been involved in this second affair. Lestrade went after his man, Stangerson, and he was wrong too. You’ve been dropping hints here and there, acting like you know more than we do, but now we have a right to ask you straight out—how much do you actually know about this case? Can you name the man who did it?”
