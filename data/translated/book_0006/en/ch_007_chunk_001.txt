6 - TOBIAS GREGSON SHOWS WHAT HE CAN DO  

The newspapers the next day were full of what they called the “Brixton Mystery.” Each paper had a long piece about the case, and some even ran editorials. There was some information in them that was new to me. I still have a scrapbook full of clippings and excerpts about the case. Here’s a summary of a few of them:  

The *Daily Telegraph* said that, in the history of crime, few tragedies had stranger details. The German name of the victim, the lack of any apparent motive, and the sinister message on the wall all suggested that the crime had been committed by political refugees or revolutionaries. The Socialists had many branches in America, and the victim had probably broken some unwritten rule of theirs and been hunted down. After casually mentioning the Vehmgericht, aqua tofana, Carbonari, the Marchioness de Brinvilliers, Darwin’s theory, Malthus’s principles, and the Ratcliff Highway murders, the article ended by warning the Government and calling for stricter monitoring of foreigners in England.  

The *Standard* commented that lawless acts like this usually happened under a Liberal government. They were caused by the unrest of the masses and the weakening of authority. The victim was an American gentleman who had been living in London for several weeks. He had stayed at the boarding house of Madame Charpentier in Torquay Terrace, Camberwell. He was traveling with his private secretary, Mr. Joseph Stangerson. The two had said goodbye to their landlady on Tuesday the 4th and left for Euston Station, saying they were taking the Liverpool express. They were last seen together on the platform. Nothing more was known until Mr. Drebber’s body was discovered, as reported, in an empty house on Brixton Road, many miles from Euston. How he got there or how he died were still complete mysteries. Nothing was known about Stangerson’s whereabouts. The paper was glad to report that Mr. Lestrade and Mr. Gregson of Scotland Yard were both working on the case, and it confidently predicted that these well-known detectives would soon shed light on it.  

The *Daily News* said there was no doubt the crime was political. The despotism and hatred of Liberalism among the Continental governments had driven many men to England who might have been good citizens if they hadn’t been embittered by what they had suffered. Among such men there existed a strict code of honour, and any violation was punished by death. Every effort, the paper said, should be made to find the secretary, Stangerson, and to learn more about the victim’s habits. A major step forward had been made by discovering the address of the boarding house where he had stayed—a result entirely due to the sharpness and energy of Mr. Gregson of Scotland Yard.  

Sherlock Holmes and I read these articles together at breakfast, and they seemed to amuse him greatly.  

“I told you,” he said, “that no matter what happens, Lestrade and Gregson will come out looking good.”  

“That depends on how it turns out,” I said.  

“Oh, not at all. If the man is caught, it’ll be *because* of their efforts; if he escapes, it’ll be *in spite* of their efforts. Either way, they win. Whatever they do, they’ll have admirers. ‘Un sot trouve toujours un plus sot qui l’admire.’”  

“What on earth is this?” I cried, because just then we heard a rush of footsteps in the hallway and on the stairs, along with our landlady’s exasperated protests.  

“It’s the Baker Street division of the detective police,” said Holmes seriously. As he spoke, half a dozen of the dirtiest, most ragged street kids I’d ever seen burst into the room.  

“Attention!” Holmes barked, and the six grubby little rascals lined up like a row of disreputable statues. “From now on, Wiggins alone will come up to report. The rest of you wait in the street. Have you found it, Wiggins?”  

“No, sir, we haven’t,” said one of the boys.  

“I didn’t really expect you to. Keep at it until you do. Here’s your pay.” He handed each of them a shilling.  

“Now off you go, and bring me a better report next time.”  

He waved his hand, and they scurried downstairs like rats. A moment later we heard their high-pitched voices outside.  

“You can get more work out of one of those little devils than out of a dozen policemen,” Holmes remarked. “The moment an official-looking person appears, people clam up. But these kids go everywhere and hear everything. They’re sharp as needles—what they need is organization.”  

“Are you using them for this Brixton case?” I asked.  

“Yes. There’s one point I want to clear up. It’s just a matter of time. Ah, we’re about to hear some news now, and serious news at that! Here comes Gregson down the road, looking absolutely delighted. He’s heading here, I can tell. Yes, he’s stopping. There he is!”  

There was a loud ring at the bell, and a few seconds later the fair-haired detective bounded up the stairs three at a time and burst into our sitting room.  

“My dear fellow,” he cried, shaking Holmes’s unresponsive hand, “congratulate me! I’ve made the whole thing as clear as day.”  

I thought I saw a flicker of concern cross Holmes’s face.  

“Do you mean you’re on the right track?” he asked.  

“The right track? My dear sir, we’ve got the man locked up.”  

“And his name?”  

“Arthur Charpentier, sub-lieutenant in Her Majesty’s navy,” Gregson declared proudly, rubbing his hands and puffing out his chest.  

Sherlock Holmes sighed with relief and smiled.  

“Have a seat and try one of these cigars,” he said. “We’re eager to hear how you managed it. Would you like some whiskey and water?”  

“I wouldn’t mind,” said the detective. “The strain I’ve been under these last couple of days has worn me out—not so much physically, you understand, but mentally. You’ll appreciate that, Mr. Sherlock Holmes, since we’re both brain-workers.”
