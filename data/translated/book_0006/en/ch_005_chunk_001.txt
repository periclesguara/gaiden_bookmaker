4 - WHAT JOHN RANCE HAD TO TELL  

It was one o’clock when we left No. 3, Lauriston Gardens. Sherlock Holmes led me to the nearest telegraph office, where he sent a long telegram. Then he hailed a cab and told the driver to take us to the address Lestrade had given.  

“There’s nothing like first-hand evidence,” he said. “To be honest, I’ve already made up my mind about the case, but we might as well gather every bit of information we can.”  

“You surprise me, Holmes,” I said. “Surely you’re not as certain as you pretend to be about all those details you mentioned.”  

“There’s no room for error,” he replied. “The very first thing I noticed when I arrived was that a cab had made two ruts with its wheels close to the curb. We haven’t had rain for a week until last night, so those deep wheel marks must have been made during the night. I also saw the horse’s hoofprints—one was much sharper than the other three, meaning that hoof had a new shoe. Since the cab was there after the rain began and wasn’t seen there this morning—I have Gregson’s word for that—it must have been there during the night. Therefore, it brought those two men to the house.”  

“That seems simple enough,” I said. “But what about the other man’s height?”  

“Well, in nine cases out of ten, you can tell a man’s height from the length of his stride. It’s an easy calculation, though I won’t bore you with the numbers. I had this fellow’s stride both in the clay outside and in the dust inside. Then I had a way to check it. When a man writes on a wall, his instinct is to write at eye level. That writing was just over six feet from the ground. It was child’s play.”  

“And his age?” I asked.  

“Well, if a man can stride four and a half feet without effort, he’s not exactly old and feeble. That was the width of a puddle on the garden path that he clearly stepped over. The man in patent-leather boots went around it, while Square-toes jumped across. There’s no mystery about it. I’m just applying to everyday life the same principles of observation and deduction I described in that article. Is there anything else that puzzles you?”  

“The fingernails and the Trichinopoly,” I said.  

“The writing on the wall was done with a man’s forefinger dipped in blood. My magnifying glass showed that the plaster was slightly scratched, which wouldn’t have happened if the man’s nails had been trimmed. I also collected some ash from the floor. It was dark and flaky—the kind only produced by a Trichinopoly cigar. I’ve made a special study of cigar ashes—in fact, I’ve written a monograph on the subject. I flatter myself that I can recognize at a glance the ash of any known brand of cigar or tobacco. It’s details like these that set a skilled detective apart from the Gregson and Lestrade type.”  

“And the flushed face?” I asked.  

“Ah, that was a bolder guess, though I’m sure I was right. Don’t ask me about that yet—not at this stage of the case.”  

I rubbed my forehead. “My head’s spinning,” I said. “The more I think about it, the more confusing it gets. How did those two men—if there were two—get into an empty house? What happened to the cab driver who brought them? How could one man force another to take poison? Where did the blood come from? What was the murderer’s motive, since robbery wasn’t involved? How did the woman’s ring end up there? And above all, why did the second man write the German word RACHE before leaving? I can’t see any way to make all these facts fit together.”  

My companion smiled approvingly.  

“You’ve summed up the difficulties clearly and well,” he said. “There’s still much we don’t know, though I’ve already made up my mind about the main facts. As for poor Lestrade’s discovery, it was just a trick meant to send the police down the wrong path by suggesting Socialism and secret societies. It wasn’t done by a German. The A, if you noticed, was shaped a bit like a German letter, but a real German always prints in Latin characters. So we can safely say it wasn’t written by one, but by a clumsy imitator who overdid it. It was simply a ruse to mislead the investigation. I’m not going to tell you much more about the case, Doctor. You know how a magician loses all credit once he explains his trick. If I show you too much of my method, you might end up thinking I’m just an ordinary man after all.”  

“I’ll never think that,” I said. “You’ve brought detection as close to an exact science as it can ever get in this world.”  

He flushed with pleasure at my words and the sincerity of my tone. I had already noticed that he was as sensitive to praise about his craft as any girl might be about her beauty.  

“I’ll tell you one more thing,” he said. “Patent-leathers and Square-toes arrived in the same cab. They walked down the path together, friendly enough—probably arm in arm. Inside, they paced the room—or rather, Patent-leathers stood still while Square-toes walked up and down. I could read all that in the dust. And I could see that as he walked, he grew more and more agitated. That’s shown by the increasing length of his strides. He was talking the whole time, working himself up into a fury. Then the tragedy happened. That’s all I know for sure; the rest is just theory and guesswork. Still, we have a solid starting point. We’d better hurry—I want to get to Halle’s concert to hear Norman Neruda this afternoon.”  

This conversation took place while our cab wound its way through a long stretch of grimy streets and dreary back alleys. In the most dismal of them, our driver suddenly stopped. “That’s Audley Court in there,” he said, pointing to a narrow gap between dull brick walls. “I’ll be here waiting when you come back.”
