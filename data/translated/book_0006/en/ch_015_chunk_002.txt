It took me quite a while to find out where my two gentlemen were living. But I kept asking around until I finally tracked them down. They were staying at a boarding house in Camberwell, on the other side of the river. Once I’d located them, I knew I had them at my mercy. I’d grown a beard, so there was no chance they’d recognize me. I planned to shadow them and follow them until I saw my opportunity. I was determined they wouldn’t escape me again.  

Even so, they almost did. Wherever they went in London, I was always right behind them. Sometimes I followed in my cab, sometimes on foot—but the cab was better, since they couldn’t easily get away. Because I could only earn money early in the morning or late at night, I started falling behind with my employer. I didn’t care, though, as long as I could get my hands on the men I wanted.  

They were clever, though. They must have suspected they were being followed, because they never went out alone and never after dark. For two weeks straight, I drove behind them every day and never once saw them separate. Drebber was drunk half the time, but Stangerson was always alert. I kept watch day and night, but never saw even the faintest chance. Still, I didn’t lose heart. Something told me the time was close. My only fear was that the thing in my chest might burst too soon and leave my work unfinished.  

At last, one evening I was driving up and down Torquay Terrace—the street where they were boarding—when I saw a cab pull up to their door. Soon after, some luggage was brought out, and then Drebber and Stangerson followed and drove off. I urged my horse on and kept them in sight, uneasy that they might be changing lodgings. At Euston Station they got out. I left a boy to hold my horse and followed them onto the platform. I heard them ask for the Liverpool train, and the guard told them one had just left and there wouldn’t be another for several hours. Stangerson seemed annoyed at that, but Drebber looked more pleased than not. I was close enough in the crowd to hear every word between them. Drebber said he had some private business to attend to and would rejoin Stangerson soon if he waited. His companion argued, reminding him they had agreed to stay together. Drebber replied that the matter was delicate and he had to go alone. I couldn’t catch Stangerson’s exact answer, but Drebber swore at him and reminded him that he was only a paid servant and shouldn’t presume to give orders. At that, the secretary gave up and simply arranged that if Drebber missed the last train, he should meet him at Halliday’s Private Hotel. Drebber said he’d be back on the platform before eleven, then left the station.  

The moment I’d waited for so long had finally come. I had my enemies within my reach. Together they could protect each other, but alone each was at my mercy. Still, I didn’t act hastily. My plan was already made. Revenge isn’t satisfying unless the culprit understands who’s striking him and why punishment has come. I’d worked it out so I could make the man who wronged me realize his old crime had caught up with him.  

A few days earlier, a gentleman who’d been inspecting houses on Brixton Road had dropped the key to one of them in my cab. It was claimed that same evening and returned, but in the meantime I’d made a mold of it and had a copy made. With that, I had access to at least one place in this vast city where I could be sure of not being disturbed. The problem now was how to get Drebber to that house.  

He walked down the street and stopped in one or two liquor shops, spending nearly half an hour in the last one. When he came out, he was clearly drunk. A hansom cab stood just ahead of me, and he hailed it. I followed so closely that the nose of my horse was within a yard of his driver the whole way. We rattled across Waterloo Bridge and through miles of streets until, to my surprise, we ended up back at the same Terrace where he’d been boarding. I couldn’t imagine why he’d returned there, but I pulled up my cab about a hundred yards from the house. He went inside, and his hansom drove away. The next moment the door flew open and two men appeared—one was Drebber, and the other was a young man I’d never seen before. The stranger had Drebber by the collar, and when they reached the top of the steps, he shoved and kicked him so hard that he went halfway across the road. ‘You hound!’ he shouted, shaking his stick at him. ‘I’ll teach you to insult an honest girl!’ He was so furious that I think he would have beaten Drebber with his stick if the scoundrel hadn’t staggered away down the street as fast as he could. He ran to the corner and, spotting my cab, hailed me and jumped in. ‘Drive me to Halliday’s Private Hotel,’ he said.  

Once he was safely inside my cab, my heart leapt with joy so violently I was afraid my aneurysm might burst right then. I drove slowly, thinking over what to do. I could take him out into the countryside and have my final meeting with him in some lonely lane. I had almost decided on that when he made the choice for me. The craving for drink hit him again, and he told me to stop outside a gin palace. He went in, telling me to wait. He stayed there until closing time, and when he came out, he was so drunk that I knew the game was entirely in my hands.
