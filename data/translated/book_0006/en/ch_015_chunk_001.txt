### 6 – A CONTINUATION OF THE REMINISCENCES OF JOHN WATSON, M.D.

Our prisoner’s furious struggle didn’t seem to show any real hostility toward us. Once he realized he couldn’t win, he smiled politely and hoped he hadn’t hurt anyone during the fight.  
“I guess you’re taking me to the police station,” he said to Sherlock Holmes. “My cab’s outside. If you untie my legs, I’ll walk down to it. I’m not as easy to lift as I used to be.”

Gregson and Lestrade exchanged looks, thinking this was a bold request. But Holmes immediately agreed and loosened the towel we had tied around the man’s ankles. The prisoner stood up and stretched his legs, testing his freedom. As I watched him, I thought I had rarely seen a more powerfully built man. His dark, sunburned face showed a look of determination and energy that was as intimidating as his physical strength.

“If there’s ever an opening for chief of police, you’re the man for it,” he said, looking at Holmes with open admiration. “The way you tracked me down was something else.”

“You’d better come with me,” Holmes said to the two detectives.

“I can drive,” said Lestrade.

“Good. Gregson can ride inside with me. You too, Doctor — you’ve been involved in the case, so you might as well stay with us.”

I agreed gladly, and we all went downstairs together. The prisoner made no attempt to escape. He calmly stepped into his own cab, and we followed him. Lestrade climbed onto the driver’s seat, flicked the reins, and in just a few minutes we reached our destination. We were shown into a small room where a police inspector recorded our prisoner’s name and the names of the men he was accused of murdering. The inspector was a pale, emotionless man who went about his work in a dull, mechanical way.  

“The prisoner will appear before the magistrates later this week,” he said. “In the meantime, Mr. Jefferson Hope, do you have anything you wish to say? I must warn you that anything you say will be written down and may be used against you.”

“I’ve got a lot to say,” the prisoner replied slowly. “I want to tell you gentlemen the whole story.”

“Wouldn’t it be better to save that for your trial?” asked the inspector.

“I might never be tried,” he said. “Don’t look so startled. I’m not thinking about suicide. Are you a doctor?” He turned his intense dark eyes on me as he asked.

“Yes, I am,” I replied.

“Then put your hand here,” he said with a faint smile, raising his manacled wrists toward his chest.

I did as he asked and immediately felt a strange, violent throbbing inside. The walls of his chest seemed to tremble like a building shaking under the force of a huge engine. In the silence of the room, I could hear a dull humming and buzzing sound coming from the same place.

“Why,” I exclaimed, “you have an aortic aneurysm!”

“That’s what they call it,” he said calmly. “I went to a doctor last week, and he told me it would burst within a few days. It’s been getting worse for years. I got it from exposure and starvation up in the Salt Lake Mountains. My work’s done now, and I don’t care how soon I die, but I’d like to leave a record of what happened. I don’t want to be remembered as just a common murderer.”

The inspector and the two detectives quickly discussed whether to let him tell his story.

“Doctor, do you think there’s immediate danger?” the inspector asked.

“Most certainly,” I said.

“In that case, it’s clearly our duty, in the interest of justice, to take his statement,” said the inspector. “You’re free to tell your story, but I warn you again — it will be written down.”

“I’ll sit down, if you don’t mind,” the prisoner said, doing so. “This aneurysm makes me tire easily, and that fight we had half an hour ago didn’t help. I’m standing at death’s door, and I’m not about to lie to you. Every word I say is the truth, and what you do with it doesn’t matter to me.”

With that, Jefferson Hope leaned back in his chair and began his remarkable statement. He spoke calmly and methodically, as if what he described were nothing unusual. I can confirm the accuracy of the following account, since I’ve seen Lestrade’s notebook, where the prisoner’s exact words were written down.

“It doesn’t really matter to you why I hated those men,” he began. “It’s enough that they caused the deaths of two people — a father and a daughter — and for that, they deserved to die. After all the time that’s passed since their crime, there was no chance of convicting them in any court. But I knew they were guilty, and I decided that I would be judge, jury, and executioner all in one. You’d have done the same, if you had any manhood in you, and if you’d been in my place.

“The girl I mentioned was supposed to marry me twenty years ago. She was forced into marrying that same Drebber and died of heartbreak. I took the wedding ring from her dead finger, and I swore that Drebber’s dying eyes would see that ring, and that his last thoughts would be of the crime he was being punished for. I carried that ring with me and followed him and his accomplice across two continents until I caught them. They thought they could wear me down, but they couldn’t. If I die tomorrow — which is likely enough — I’ll die knowing my work in this world is finished, and done well. They’re dead, and by my hand. There’s nothing left for me to hope for or want.

“They were rich, and I was poor, so following them wasn’t easy. When I reached London, I was almost out of money and had to find work to survive. Driving and riding come as naturally to me as walking, so I went to a cab owner’s office and got a job. I had to bring in a set amount each week, and whatever I earned beyond that was mine. There was rarely much extra, but I managed to get by. The hardest part was learning my way around, because I swear this city is the most confusing maze ever built. I kept a map with me, though, and once I’d memorized the main hotels and stations, I managed pretty well.”
