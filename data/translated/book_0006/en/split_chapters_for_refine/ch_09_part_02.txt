4 - A FLIGHT FOR LIFE  

Once more the avenger was thwarted, and once more his burning hatred drove him to continue the chase. But he lacked money. For a time, he had to go back to work, saving every dollar for the journey ahead. At last, when he had enough to keep himself alive, he set out for Europe. He tracked his enemies from city to city, taking whatever menial jobs he could find, but he never quite caught up with them. When he reached St. Petersburg, they had already gone to Paris. When he followed them there, he learned they had just left for Copenhagen. In the Danish capital, he arrived a few days too late again—they had moved on to London, where he finally succeeded in running them to ground.  

As for what happened there, we can do no better than to quote the old hunter’s own account, faithfully recorded in Dr. Watson’s Journal, to which we already owe so much.  
### 6 – A CONTINUATION OF THE REMINISCENCES OF JOHN WATSON, M.D.

Our prisoner’s furious struggle didn’t seem to show any real hostility toward us. Once he realized he couldn’t win, he smiled politely and hoped he hadn’t hurt anyone during the fight.  
“I guess you’re taking me to the police station,” he said to Sherlock Holmes. “My cab’s outside. If you untie my legs, I’ll walk down to it. I’m not as easy to lift as I used to be.”

Gregson and Lestrade exchanged looks, thinking this was a bold request. But Holmes immediately agreed and loosened the towel we had tied around the man’s ankles. The prisoner stood up and stretched his legs, testing his freedom. As I watched him, I thought I had rarely seen a more powerfully built man. His dark, sunburned face showed a look of determination and energy that was as intimidating as his physical strength.

“If there’s ever an opening for chief of police, you’re the man for it,” he said, looking at Holmes with open admiration. “The way you tracked me down was something else.”

“You’d better come with me,” Holmes said to the two detectives.

“I can drive,” said Lestrade.

“Good. Gregson can ride inside with me. You too, Doctor — you’ve been involved in the case, so you might as well stay with us.”

I agreed gladly, and we all went downstairs together. The prisoner made no attempt to escape. He calmly stepped into his own cab, and we followed him. Lestrade climbed onto the driver’s seat, flicked the reins, and in just a few minutes we reached our destination. We were shown into a small room where a police inspector recorded our prisoner’s name and the names of the men he was accused of murdering. The inspector was a pale, emotionless man who went about his work in a dull, mechanical way.  

“The prisoner will appear before the magistrates later this week,” he said. “In the meantime, Mr. Jefferson Hope, do you have anything you wish to say? I must warn you that anything you say will be written down and may be used against you.”

“I’ve got a lot to say,” the prisoner replied slowly. “I want to tell you gentlemen the whole story.”

“Wouldn’t it be better to save that for your trial?” asked the inspector.

“I might never be tried,” he said. “Don’t look so startled. I’m not thinking about suicide. Are you a doctor?” He turned his intense dark eyes on me as he asked.

“Yes, I am,” I replied.

“Then put your hand here,” he said with a faint smile, raising his manacled wrists toward his chest.

I did as he asked and immediately felt a strange, violent throbbing inside. The walls of his chest seemed to tremble like a building shaking under the force of a huge engine. In the silence of the room, I could hear a dull humming and buzzing sound coming from the same place.

“Why,” I exclaimed, “you have an aortic aneurysm!”

“That’s what they call it,” he said calmly. “I went to a doctor last week, and he told me it would burst within a few days. It’s been getting worse for years. I got it from exposure and starvation up in the Salt Lake Mountains. My work’s done now, and I don’t care how soon I die, but I’d like to leave a record of what happened. I don’t want to be remembered as just a common murderer.”

The inspector and the two detectives quickly discussed whether to let him tell his story.

“Doctor, do you think there’s immediate danger?” the inspector asked.

“Most certainly,” I said.

“In that case, it’s clearly our duty, in the interest of justice, to take his statement,” said the inspector. “You’re free to tell your story, but I warn you again — it will be written down.”

“I’ll sit down, if you don’t mind,” the prisoner said, doing so. “This aneurysm makes me tire easily, and that fight we had half an hour ago didn’t help. I’m standing at death’s door, and I’m not about to lie to you. Every word I say is the truth, and what you do with it doesn’t matter to me.”

With that, Jefferson Hope leaned back in his chair and began his remarkable statement. He spoke calmly and methodically, as if what he described were nothing unusual. I can confirm the accuracy of the following account, since I’ve seen Lestrade’s notebook, where the prisoner’s exact words were written down.

“It doesn’t really matter to you why I hated those men,” he began. “It’s enough that they caused the deaths of two people — a father and a daughter — and for that, they deserved to die. After all the time that’s passed since their crime, there was no chance of convicting them in any court. But I knew they were guilty, and I decided that I would be judge, jury, and executioner all in one. You’d have done the same, if you had any manhood in you, and if you’d been in my place.

“The girl I mentioned was supposed to marry me twenty years ago. She was forced into marrying that same Drebber and died of heartbreak. I took the wedding ring from her dead finger, and I swore that Drebber’s dying eyes would see that ring, and that his last thoughts would be of the crime he was being punished for. I carried that ring with me and followed him and his accomplice across two continents until I caught them. They thought they could wear me down, but they couldn’t. If I die tomorrow — which is likely enough — I’ll die knowing my work in this world is finished, and done well. They’re dead, and by my hand. There’s nothing left for me to hope for or want.

“They were rich, and I was poor, so following them wasn’t easy. When I reached London, I was almost out of money and had to find work to survive. Driving and riding come as naturally to me as walking, so I went to a cab owner’s office and got a job. I had to bring in a set amount each week, and whatever I earned beyond that was mine. There was rarely much extra, but I managed to get by. The hardest part was learning my way around, because I swear this city is the most confusing maze ever built. I kept a map with me, though, and once I’d memorized the main hotels and stations, I managed pretty well.”
It took me quite a while to find out where my two gentlemen were living. But I kept asking around until I finally tracked them down. They were staying at a boarding house in Camberwell, on the other side of the river. Once I’d located them, I knew I had them at my mercy. I’d grown a beard, so there was no chance they’d recognize me. I planned to shadow them and follow them until I saw my opportunity. I was determined they wouldn’t escape me again.  

Even so, they almost did. Wherever they went in London, I was always right behind them. Sometimes I followed in my cab, sometimes on foot—but the cab was better, since they couldn’t easily get away. Because I could only earn money early in the morning or late at night, I started falling behind with my employer. I didn’t care, though, as long as I could get my hands on the men I wanted.  

They were clever, though. They must have suspected they were being followed, because they never went out alone and never after dark. For two weeks straight, I drove behind them every day and never once saw them separate. Drebber was drunk half the time, but Stangerson was always alert. I kept watch day and night, but never saw even the faintest chance. Still, I didn’t lose heart. Something told me the time was close. My only fear was that the thing in my chest might burst too soon and leave my work unfinished.  

At last, one evening I was driving up and down Torquay Terrace—the street where they were boarding—when I saw a cab pull up to their door. Soon after, some luggage was brought out, and then Drebber and Stangerson followed and drove off. I urged my horse on and kept them in sight, uneasy that they might be changing lodgings. At Euston Station they got out. I left a boy to hold my horse and followed them onto the platform. I heard them ask for the Liverpool train, and the guard told them one had just left and there wouldn’t be another for several hours. Stangerson seemed annoyed at that, but Drebber looked more pleased than not. I was close enough in the crowd to hear every word between them. Drebber said he had some private business to attend to and would rejoin Stangerson soon if he waited. His companion argued, reminding him they had agreed to stay together. Drebber replied that the matter was delicate and he had to go alone. I couldn’t catch Stangerson’s exact answer, but Drebber swore at him and reminded him that he was only a paid servant and shouldn’t presume to give orders. At that, the secretary gave up and simply arranged that if Drebber missed the last train, he should meet him at Halliday’s Private Hotel. Drebber said he’d be back on the platform before eleven, then left the station.  

The moment I’d waited for so long had finally come. I had my enemies within my reach. Together they could protect each other, but alone each was at my mercy. Still, I didn’t act hastily. My plan was already made. Revenge isn’t satisfying unless the culprit understands who’s striking him and why punishment has come. I’d worked it out so I could make the man who wronged me realize his old crime had caught up with him.  

A few days earlier, a gentleman who’d been inspecting houses on Brixton Road had dropped the key to one of them in my cab. It was claimed that same evening and returned, but in the meantime I’d made a mold of it and had a copy made. With that, I had access to at least one place in this vast city where I could be sure of not being disturbed. The problem now was how to get Drebber to that house.  

He walked down the street and stopped in one or two liquor shops, spending nearly half an hour in the last one. When he came out, he was clearly drunk. A hansom cab stood just ahead of me, and he hailed it. I followed so closely that the nose of my horse was within a yard of his driver the whole way. We rattled across Waterloo Bridge and through miles of streets until, to my surprise, we ended up back at the same Terrace where he’d been boarding. I couldn’t imagine why he’d returned there, but I pulled up my cab about a hundred yards from the house. He went inside, and his hansom drove away. Give me a glass of water, please—my mouth gets dry from all this talking.”  

I handed him the glass, and he drank it down.  

“That’s better,” he said. “Well, I waited for fifteen minutes or more, when suddenly I heard a noise like people struggling inside the house. The next moment the door flew open and two men appeared—one was Drebber, and the other was a young man I’d never seen before. The stranger had Drebber by the collar, and when they reached the top of the steps, he shoved and kicked him so hard that he went halfway across the road. ‘You hound!’ he shouted, shaking his stick at him. ‘I’ll teach you to insult an honest girl!’ He was so furious that I think he would have beaten Drebber with his stick if the scoundrel hadn’t staggered away down the street as fast as he could. He ran to the corner and, spotting my cab, hailed me and jumped in. ‘Drive me to Halliday’s Private Hotel,’ he said.  

Once he was safely inside my cab, my heart leapt with joy so violently I was afraid my aneurysm might burst right then. I drove slowly, thinking over what to do. I could take him out into the countryside and have my final meeting with him in some lonely lane. I had almost decided on that when he made the choice for me. The craving for drink hit him again, and he told me to stop outside a gin palace. He went in, telling me to wait. He stayed there until closing time, and when he came out, he was so drunk that I knew the game was entirely in my hands.  
“Don’t think I meant to kill him in cold blood. It would’ve been strict justice if I had, but I couldn’t bring myself to do it. I’d already decided long ago that he’d have a chance at his life, if he chose to take it. During my wandering years in America, I’d worked many jobs, and once I was janitor and cleaner in the laboratory at York College. One day the professor was giving a lecture on poisons. He showed his students an alkaloid, as he called it, extracted from some South American arrow poison—so powerful that even a tiny grain meant instant death. I noticed the bottle where he kept it, and when everyone left, I took a little for myself.  

I was a decent hand at mixing medicines, so I worked the alkaloid into small, dissolvable pills. Each box held two pills—one harmless, one deadly. I decided that when the time came, my gentlemen would each draw one pill, and I’d swallow the one that was left. It would be just as fatal and a lot quieter than shooting across a handkerchief. From that day on, I always carried those pill boxes with me, waiting for the moment to use them.  

It was closer to one than twelve that night—a wild, bitter night, with hard wind and pouring rain. Miserable as it was outside, I was overjoyed inside—so happy I could’ve shouted from sheer excitement. If any of you have ever longed for something for twenty years and suddenly found it within reach, you’d understand how I felt. I lit a cigar to steady my nerves, but my hands were shaking and my temples throbbed with excitement. As I drove, I could almost see old John Ferrier and sweet Lucy looking at me out of the darkness, smiling at me as clearly as I see you now. They seemed to ride ahead of me all the way—one on each side of the horse—until I stopped at the house on Brixton Road.  

Not a soul was around, and the only sound was the rain dripping. When I looked through the window, I saw Drebber slumped in a drunken sleep. I shook his arm. “Time to get out,” I said.  

“All right, cabby,” he muttered.  

He must’ve thought we’d reached the hotel he’d mentioned, because he got out without a word and followed me down the garden path. I walked beside him to keep him steady, since he was still a bit drunk. When we reached the door, I opened it and led him into the front room. I swear to you, the father and daughter seemed to walk in front of us the whole way.  

“It’s damn dark,” he said, stamping around.  

“We’ll have light soon,” I told him, striking a match and lighting a wax candle I’d brought. “Now, Enoch Drebber,” I said, turning toward him and holding the light to my face, “who am I?”  

He stared at me with bleary eyes for a moment. Then I saw horror flood his face and twist his features—it was clear he recognized me. He staggered back, his face pale, sweat breaking out on his forehead, his teeth chattering. At that sight, I leaned against the door and laughed loud and long. I’d always known revenge would be sweet, but I’d never imagined the deep satisfaction I felt in that moment.  

“You dog!” I said. “I’ve hunted you from Salt Lake City to St. Petersburg, and you always escaped me. But now your running is over. One of us won’t live to see tomorrow’s sunrise.” He shrank back even farther, and I could see he thought I was insane—and for that moment, I was. The pounding in my temples felt like hammer blows, and I think I would’ve had a fit if the blood hadn’t burst from my nose and relieved the pressure.  

“What do you think of Lucy Ferrier now?” I shouted, locking the door and shaking the key in his face. “Punishment took its time, but it’s caught up with you at last.” I saw his cowardly lips tremble. He would’ve begged for his life, but he knew it was useless.  

“Would you murder me?” he stammered.  

“This isn’t murder,” I said. “Who calls it murder to kill a mad dog? What mercy did you show my poor girl when you tore her from her slain father and dragged her into your cursed, shameful harem?”  

“It wasn’t me who killed her father!” he cried.  

“But it was you who broke her innocent heart!” I shouted, thrusting the box toward him. “Let God judge between us. Choose and eat. One holds death, the other life. I’ll take the one you leave. Let’s see if there’s justice on earth, or if it’s all just chance.”  

He cringed and cried for mercy, but I drew my knife and held it to his throat until he obeyed. Then I swallowed the other pill, and we stood facing each other in silence for a minute or more, waiting to see who would live and who would die. I’ll never forget the look on his face when the first pangs hit him and he realized the poison was working. I laughed when I saw it and held Lucy’s wedding ring before his eyes. It only lasted a moment—the alkaloid acts fast. His face twisted in pain, he threw out his hands, staggered, and with a hoarse cry, fell heavily to the floor. I turned him over with my foot and felt his heart. No movement. He was dead.  

Blood was still pouring from my nose, but I hadn’t paid any attention to it. I don’t know what made me think to write on the wall with it. Maybe it was a mischievous idea to throw the police off the track, because I felt oddly cheerful. I remembered a German found dead in New York with “RACHE” written above him, and how the newspapers said secret societies were behind it. I figured what puzzled New Yorkers would puzzle Londoners too, so I dipped my finger in my blood and wrote it on the wall. Then I went back to my cab. No one was around, and the night was still wild. I’d driven some distance when I reached into the pocket where I usually kept Lucy’s ring—and found it gone. I was stunned. It was the only keepsake I had of her. Thinking I might’ve dropped it when I bent over Drebber’s body, I drove back. Leaving my cab in a side street, I went straight up to the house—I’d risk anything rather than lose that ring. But when I got there, I walked right into the arms of a police officer coming out. I only managed to calm his suspicions by pretending to be hopelessly drunk.”
“That’s how Enoch Drebber met his end. After that, all I had left to do was the same for Stangerson, to settle John Ferrier’s debt. I knew he was staying at Halliday’s Private Hotel, so I hung around all day, but he never came out. I think he started to suspect something when Drebber didn’t show up. Stangerson was a careful, suspicious man. If he thought he could avoid me by hiding indoors, he was badly mistaken. I soon figured out which window was his bedroom. Early the next morning, I used some ladders lying in the lane behind the hotel and climbed into his room at dawn.  

I woke him and told him his time had come—to answer for the life he’d taken long ago. I told him how Drebber had died and gave him the same choice of poisoned pills. Instead of taking the chance to save himself, he jumped from his bed and grabbed at my throat. I stabbed him through the heart in self-defense. It would have ended the same way anyway—Providence would never have let his guilty hand choose anything but the poison.

“I don’t have much more to say, and it’s just as well, because I’m about finished. I kept driving my cab for another day or two, planning to save enough to get back to America. I was standing in the yard when a ragged boy came up asking if there was a cabby named Jefferson Hope, saying a gentleman at 221B Baker Street wanted his cab. I went round, suspecting nothing, and the next thing I knew, this young man here had snapped the bracelets on my wrists—neater work than I’ve ever seen. That’s my whole story, gentlemen. You can call me a murderer if you like, but I believe I’m as much an officer of justice as any of you.”

The man’s story had been so gripping, and his manner so intense, that we’d all sat silent and absorbed. Even the professional detectives—jaded as they were by endless crimes—seemed genuinely fascinated. When he finished, we sat for several minutes in silence, broken only by the scratching of Lestrade’s pencil as he completed his shorthand notes.

“There’s just one point I’d like a bit more information about,” Sherlock Holmes said at last. “Who was your accomplice—the one who came for the ring I advertised?”

The prisoner gave my friend a playful wink. “I can tell my own secrets,” he said, “but I don’t get other people into trouble. I saw your ad and thought it might be a trap—or maybe it really was the ring I wanted. My friend offered to go check it out. You’ve got to admit, he handled it neatly.”

“No doubt about that,” Holmes said warmly.

“Now, gentlemen,” the Inspector said seriously, “we must follow the proper legal process. On Thursday the prisoner will appear before the magistrates, and your attendance will be required. Until then, I’ll be responsible for him.” He rang the bell as he spoke, and Jefferson Hope was led away by two warders. My friend and I left the station and took a cab back to Baker Street.
### 7 – THE CONCLUSION

We had all been told to appear before the magistrates on Thursday, but when the day came, our testimony wasn’t needed. A higher Judge had taken over the case, and Jefferson Hope had been called before a court where true justice would be served. The very night after his capture, the aneurysm burst. In the morning, they found him lying on the floor of his cell with a calm smile on his face, as if, in his final moments, he’d been able to look back on a life that had meant something and work well done.

“Gregson and Lestrade will be furious about his death,” Holmes said as we discussed it the next evening. “Where’s their big publicity now?”

“I don’t think they had much to do with catching him,” I replied.

“What you actually do in this world doesn’t matter,” my companion said bitterly. “What matters is what you can make people believe you’ve done. Still,” he added more cheerfully after a pause, “I wouldn’t have missed this investigation for anything. There hasn’t been a better case in my memory. Simple as it was, there were several very instructive points about it.”

“Simple!” I exclaimed.

“Well, really, it can hardly be called anything else,” said Sherlock Holmes, smiling at my surprise. “The proof of how simple it was lies in the fact that, with only a few ordinary deductions, I was able to identify the criminal within three days.”

“That’s true,” I admitted.

“I’ve already explained that what’s unusual often helps rather than hinders an investigation. In solving a problem like this, the key is being able to reason backward. It’s a very useful skill—and actually an easy one—but people rarely practice it. In everyday life, people usually reason forward, so the other method gets neglected. For every fifty who can reason synthetically, only one can reason analytically.”

“I admit,” I said, “I don’t quite follow you.”

“I didn’t really expect you to. Let me try to make it clearer. Most people, if you describe a series of events to them, can tell you what the outcome will be. They can piece those events together and predict what’s likely to happen. But very few people, if you give them a result, can work backward in their minds to figure out the steps that led to it. That’s what I mean by reasoning backward—or analytically.”

“I see,” I said.

“This was a case where we were given the result and had to discover everything else ourselves. Let me show you the steps in my reasoning. Starting from the beginning: I approached the house on foot, as you know, with my mind completely clear. Naturally, I began by examining the road. There, as I’ve already told you, I clearly saw cab marks, which I confirmed by asking around had been made during the night. I determined it was a cab, not a private carriage, because the wheel tracks were narrow. The ordinary London growler is much narrower than a gentleman’s brougham.

“That was my first clue. Then I walked slowly down the garden path, which happened to be made of clay—perfect for capturing footprints. You probably saw just a messy strip of mud, but to my trained eyes, every mark meant something. No branch of detective work is more important—or more neglected—than the art of tracking footprints. Fortunately, I’ve always emphasized it, and practice has made it second nature to me. I saw the heavy boot marks of the constables, but I also saw the tracks of the two men who had first gone through the garden. It was easy to tell they’d come before the others, because in places their prints had been partly erased by later ones. That gave me my second link: there had been two visitors during the night—one tall, as I could tell from his long stride, and the other fashionably dressed, judging by the small, neat prints left by his boots.

“When I entered the house, that last deduction was confirmed. The well-dressed man lay dead before me. The tall one, then, must have done the killing—if it was a killing. There was no wound on the dead man’s body, but the strained look on his face told me he had seen his death coming. Men who die suddenly of heart disease or other natural causes never show such fear. When I smelled his lips, I noticed a faint sour odor and concluded that poison had been forced on him. I reasoned that it had been forced because of the fear and hatred visible on his face. By ruling out every other possibility, I reached that conclusion—no other explanation fit the facts. It wasn’t an unusual idea either; forced poisoning isn’t new in criminal history. Any toxicologist would immediately think of the Dolsky case in Odessa or Leturier in Montpellier.

“Then came the big question—why? Robbery clearly wasn’t the motive; nothing had been taken. So was it politics, or was it a woman? That was the problem before me. From the start, I leaned toward the latter. Political assassins usually do their work quickly and flee. This murder had been done deliberately, and the killer had left his tracks all over the room, showing he’d stayed for some time. It had to be a personal wrong, not a political one, that demanded such careful revenge. When the inscription was found on the wall, I was more convinced than ever. It was obviously a distraction. But when the ring was discovered, that settled it. The murderer had used it to remind his victim of some dead or absent woman. That was when I asked Gregson whether he’d included in his telegram to Cleveland any questions about Mr. Drebber’s past. You’ll remember he said no.”
I then made a careful inspection of the room. It confirmed my estimate of the murderer’s height and gave me extra details about the Trichinopoly cigar and the length of his fingernails. Since there were no signs of a struggle, I had already concluded that the blood covering the floor had come from the murderer’s nose during his excitement. I could see that the blood trail matched his footprints exactly. It’s rare for anyone, unless they’re very full-blooded, to have such a nosebleed from emotion, so I guessed that the criminal was a strong, ruddy-faced man. Events proved my guess right.  

After leaving the house, I did what Gregson had failed to do. I sent a telegram to the head of police in Cleveland, asking only about the circumstances of Enoch Drebber’s marriage. The reply was decisive. It said that Drebber had already sought legal protection from an old romantic rival named Jefferson Hope, and that Hope was currently in Europe. I knew then that I held the key to the mystery, and all that remained was to capture the murderer.  

I had already decided that the man who entered the house with Drebber was the same man who had driven the cab. The marks in the road showed that the horse had wandered off in a way that would have been impossible if anyone had been controlling it. Where else could the driver have been except inside the house? Besides, it’s absurd to think any sane man would commit a deliberate murder right under the eyes of a third person who could easily expose him. Finally, if a man wanted to follow another through London, what better disguise could he choose than that of a cab driver? All these points led me to the undeniable conclusion that Jefferson Hope was working among the cabmen of the city.  

If he had been one, there was no reason to think he had stopped. On the contrary, any sudden change would only draw attention to him. He would probably continue driving for a while, at least. There was no reason to think he was using a false name. Why would he, in a country where no one knew his real one? So I organized my Street Arab detective team and sent them systematically to every cab owner in London until they tracked down the man I wanted. You remember how well they succeeded and how quickly I acted once they did. The murder of Stangerson was completely unexpected, though it probably couldn’t have been prevented in any case. Because of it, as you know, I got hold of the pills whose existence I had already suspected. You see, the whole thing forms a perfect chain of logic, without a single break or flaw.  

“That’s incredible!” I exclaimed. “Your work deserves public recognition. You should publish an account of the case. If you won’t, I’ll do it for you.”  

“You can do whatever you like, Doctor,” he replied. “But look here!” He handed me a newspaper. “Check this out!”  

It was that day’s *Echo*, and the paragraph he pointed to dealt with our case.  

“The public,” it said, “has missed a sensational story because of the sudden death of the man Hope, who was suspected of murdering Mr. Enoch Drebber and Mr. Joseph Stangerson. The full details of the case will probably never be known now, though reliable sources tell us the crime resulted from an old and romantic feud involving both love and Mormonism. It appears that both victims were once members of the Latter Day Saints, and Hope, the deceased suspect, was also from Salt Lake City. If nothing else, the case has at least demonstrated the remarkable efficiency of our detective police force. It should also remind all foreigners that it’s wiser to settle their personal feuds at home rather than bring them onto British soil. It’s widely known that the credit for this clever arrest belongs entirely to the well-known Scotland Yard detectives, Messrs. Lestrade and Gregson. The man was apparently captured in the rooms of a certain Mr. Sherlock Holmes, who, as an amateur, has shown some promise in detective work and, with such mentors, may one day reach some degree of their skill. It is expected that some form of testimonial will be presented to the two officers as proper recognition of their service.”  

“Didn’t I tell you so from the start?” said Sherlock Holmes, laughing. “That’s what we get from our *Study in Scarlet*—a testimonial for them!”  

“Never mind,” I said. “I have all the facts in my journal, and the public will know the truth. For now, you’ll have to be satisfied with the knowledge of your success, like the old Roman miser—  

‘The crowd hisses at me, but I applaud myself  
At home, as I count the coins in my chest.’”
