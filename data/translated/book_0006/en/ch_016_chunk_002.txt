I then made a careful inspection of the room. It confirmed my estimate of the murderer’s height and gave me extra details about the Trichinopoly cigar and the length of his fingernails. Since there were no signs of a struggle, I had already concluded that the blood covering the floor had come from the murderer’s nose during his excitement. I could see that the blood trail matched his footprints exactly. It’s rare for anyone, unless they’re very full-blooded, to have such a nosebleed from emotion, so I guessed that the criminal was a strong, ruddy-faced man. Events proved my guess right.  

After leaving the house, I did what Gregson had failed to do. I sent a telegram to the head of police in Cleveland, asking only about the circumstances of Enoch Drebber’s marriage. The reply was decisive. It said that Drebber had already sought legal protection from an old romantic rival named Jefferson Hope, and that Hope was currently in Europe. I knew then that I held the key to the mystery, and all that remained was to capture the murderer.  

I had already decided that the man who entered the house with Drebber was the same man who had driven the cab. The marks in the road showed that the horse had wandered off in a way that would have been impossible if anyone had been controlling it. Where else could the driver have been except inside the house? Besides, it’s absurd to think any sane man would commit a deliberate murder right under the eyes of a third person who could easily expose him. Finally, if a man wanted to follow another through London, what better disguise could he choose than that of a cab driver? All these points led me to the undeniable conclusion that Jefferson Hope was working among the cabmen of the city.  

If he had been one, there was no reason to think he had stopped. On the contrary, any sudden change would only draw attention to him. He would probably continue driving for a while, at least. There was no reason to think he was using a false name. Why would he, in a country where no one knew his real one? So I organized my Street Arab detective team and sent them systematically to every cab owner in London until they tracked down the man I wanted. You remember how well they succeeded and how quickly I acted once they did. The murder of Stangerson was completely unexpected, though it probably couldn’t have been prevented in any case. Because of it, as you know, I got hold of the pills whose existence I had already suspected. You see, the whole thing forms a perfect chain of logic, without a single break or flaw.  

“That’s incredible!” I exclaimed. “Your work deserves public recognition. You should publish an account of the case. If you won’t, I’ll do it for you.”  

“You can do whatever you like, Doctor,” he replied. “But look here!” He handed me a newspaper. “Check this out!”  

It was that day’s *Echo*, and the paragraph he pointed to dealt with our case.  

“The public,” it said, “has missed a sensational story because of the sudden death of the man Hope, who was suspected of murdering Mr. Enoch Drebber and Mr. Joseph Stangerson. The full details of the case will probably never be known now, though reliable sources tell us the crime resulted from an old and romantic feud involving both love and Mormonism. It appears that both victims were once members of the Latter Day Saints, and Hope, the deceased suspect, was also from Salt Lake City. If nothing else, the case has at least demonstrated the remarkable efficiency of our detective police force. It should also remind all foreigners that it’s wiser to settle their personal feuds at home rather than bring them onto British soil. It’s widely known that the credit for this clever arrest belongs entirely to the well-known Scotland Yard detectives, Messrs. Lestrade and Gregson. The man was apparently captured in the rooms of a certain Mr. Sherlock Holmes, who, as an amateur, has shown some promise in detective work and, with such mentors, may one day reach some degree of their skill. It is expected that some form of testimonial will be presented to the two officers as proper recognition of their service.”  

“Didn’t I tell you so from the start?” said Sherlock Holmes, laughing. “That’s what we get from our *Study in Scarlet*—a testimonial for them!”  

“Never mind,” I said. “I have all the facts in my journal, and the public will know the truth. For now, you’ll have to be satisfied with the knowledge of your success, like the old Roman miser—  

‘The crowd hisses at me, but I applaud myself  
At home, as I count the coins in my chest.’”
