Heber C. Kemball, in one of his sermons, affectionately referred to his hundred wives with this term of endearment.  

Ferrier stayed silent for a while, his brow furrowed in thought.  

“You’ll give us some time,” he said finally. “My daughter is very young—barely old enough to marry.”  

“She has one month to decide,” said Young, standing up. “At the end of that time, she’ll give her answer.”  

He was leaving the room when he turned back, his face flushed and his eyes blazing. “It would be better for you, John Ferrier,” he thundered, “if you and your daughter were lying as bleached skeletons on the Sierra Blanco right now, than if you dared to set your weak wills against the orders of the Holy Four!”  

With a threatening wave of his hand, he turned and left. Ferrier heard his heavy footsteps crunching down the gravel path.  

Ferrier sat with his elbows on his knees, thinking about how to tell his daughter what had happened, when a soft hand touched his. Looking up, he saw her standing beside him. One look at her pale, frightened face told him she had heard everything.  

“I couldn’t help it,” she said, answering his look. “His voice echoed through the whole house. Oh, Father—what are we going to do?”  

“Don’t you worry yourself,” he said, pulling her close and running his rough, broad hand gently over her chestnut hair. “We’ll figure something out. You haven’t lost your feelings for that young man, have you?”  

She only sobbed and squeezed his hand.  

“No, of course not. I’d hate to hear you say otherwise. He’s a fine lad, and he’s a Christian—which is more than I can say for these folks, with all their praying and preaching. There’s a group heading for Nevada tomorrow, and I’ll find a way to send him a message about the trouble we’re in. If I know that young man, he’ll be back here faster than any telegraph.”  

Lucy laughed through her tears at her father’s description.  

“When he comes, he’ll know what’s best to do. But, Father, I’m scared for you. People tell such awful stories about what happens to those who go against the Prophet—something terrible always happens.”  

“But we haven’t gone against him yet,” her father replied. “We’ll worry about that when the time comes. We’ve got a whole month ahead of us. When that’s up, I think we’d better get out of Utah.”  

“Leave Utah?”  

“That’s about right.”  

“But the farm?”  

“We’ll sell what we can for cash and forget the rest. Honestly, Lucy, I’ve thought about leaving before. I don’t like bowing down to any man the way these people do to their so-called prophet. I’m a free-born American, and this kind of thing doesn’t sit right with me. I’m too old to change now. If he starts snooping around this farm, he might find himself facing a load of buckshot coming the other way.”  

“But they won’t let us leave,” his daughter protested.  

“Wait till Jefferson gets here—we’ll handle it then. In the meantime, don’t worry, sweetheart, and don’t cry yourself red-eyed, or he’ll blame me when he sees you. There’s nothing to be afraid of, no danger at all.”  

John Ferrier spoke these comforting words with great confidence, but Lucy couldn’t help noticing that he took unusual care locking the doors that night—and that he carefully cleaned and loaded the old rusty shotgun hanging on his bedroom wall.
