6 - TOBIAS GREGSON SHOWS WHAT HE CAN DO  

The newspapers the next day were full of what they called the “Brixton Mystery.” Each paper carried a lengthy article on the case, and some even published editorials. There was information in them that was new to me. I still have a scrapbook filled with clippings and excerpts about the affair. Here is a summary of a few of them:  

The *Daily Telegraph* declared that, in the history of crime, few tragedies had stranger details. The German name of the victim, the absence of any apparent motive, and the sinister message on the wall all suggested that the crime had been committed by political refugees or revolutionaries. The Socialists had many branches in America, and the victim had probably broken some unwritten rule of theirs and been hunted down. After casually mentioning the Vehmgericht, aqua tofana, the Carbonari, the Marchioness de Brinvilliers, Darwin’s theory, Malthus’s principles, and the Ratcliff Highway murders, the article concluded by warning the Government and calling for stricter surveillance of foreigners in England.  

The *Standard* commented that lawless acts of this kind usually occurred under a Liberal government. They were caused by the unrest of the masses and the weakening of authority. The victim was an American gentleman who had been living in London for several weeks. He had lodged at the boarding house of Madame Charpentier in Torquay Terrace, Camberwell. He was traveling with his private secretary, Mr. Joseph Stangerson. The two had taken leave of their landlady on Tuesday the 4th and left for Euston Station, saying they were taking the Liverpool express. They were last seen together on the platform. Nothing more was known until Mr. Drebber’s body was discovered, as reported, in an empty house on Brixton Road, many miles from Euston. How he came there or how he met his death remained complete mysteries. Nothing was known of Stangerson’s whereabouts. The paper was pleased to report that Mr. Lestrade and Mr. Gregson of Scotland Yard were both engaged on the case and confidently predicted that these well-known detectives would soon shed light upon it.  

The *Daily News* asserted that there was no doubt the crime was political. The despotism and hatred of Liberalism among the Continental governments had driven many men to England who might have been good citizens had they not been embittered by what they had suffered. Among such men there existed a strict code of honour, and any violation was punished by death. Every effort, the paper said, should be made to find the secretary, Stangerson, and to learn more about the victim’s habits. A major step forward had been made by discovering the address of the boarding house where he had stayed—a result entirely due to the sharpness and energy of Mr. Gregson of Scotland Yard.  

Sherlock Holmes and I read these articles together at breakfast, and they seemed to amuse him greatly.  

“I told you,” he said, “that no matter what happens, Lestrade and Gregson will come out looking good.”  

“That depends on how it turns out,” I said.  

“Oh, not at all. If the man is caught, it will be *because* of their efforts; if he escapes, it will be *in spite* of their efforts. Either way, they win. Whatever they do, they’ll have admirers. ‘Un sot trouve toujours un plus sot qui l’admire.’”  

“What on earth is this?” I cried, for just then we heard a rush of footsteps in the hallway and on the stairs, accompanied by our landlady’s exasperated protests.  

“It’s the Baker Street division of the detective police,” said Holmes gravely. As he spoke, half a dozen of the dirtiest, most ragged street boys I had ever seen burst into the room.  

“Attention!” Holmes barked, and the six grubby little rascals lined up like a row of disreputable statues. “From now on, Wiggins alone will come up to report. The rest of you wait in the street. Have you found it, Wiggins?”  

“No, sir, we haven’t,” said one of the boys.  

“I didn’t really expect you to. Keep at it until you do. Here’s your pay.” He handed each of them a shilling.  

“Now off you go, and bring me a better report next time.”  

He waved his hand, and they scurried downstairs like rats. A moment later we heard their high-pitched voices outside.  

“You can get more work out of one of those little devils than out of a dozen policemen,” Holmes remarked. “The moment an official-looking person appears, people clam up. But these kids go everywhere and hear everything. They’re sharp as needles—what they need is organization.”  

“Are you using them for this Brixton case?” I asked.  

“Yes. There’s one point I want to clear up. It’s just a matter of time. Ah, we’re about to hear some news now, and serious news at that! Here comes Gregson down the road, looking absolutely delighted. He’s heading here, I can tell. Yes, he’s stopping. There he is!”  

There was a loud ring at the bell, and a few seconds later the fair-haired detective bounded up the stairs three at a time and burst into our sitting room.  

“My dear fellow,” he cried, shaking Holmes’s unresponsive hand, “congratulate me! I’ve made the whole thing as clear as day.”  

I thought I saw a flicker of concern cross Holmes’s face.  

“Do you mean you’re on the right track?” he asked.  

“The right track? My dear sir, we’ve got the man locked up.”  

“And his name?”  

“Arthur Charpentier, sub-lieutenant in Her Majesty’s navy,” Gregson declared proudly, rubbing his hands and puffing out his chest.  

Sherlock Holmes sighed with relief and smiled.  

“Have a seat and try one of these cigars,” he said. “We’re eager to hear how you managed it. Would you like some whiskey and water?”  

“I wouldn’t mind,” said the detective. “The strain I’ve been under these last couple of days has worn me out—not so much physically, you understand, but mentally. You’ll appreciate that, Mr. Sherlock Holmes, since we’re both brain-workers.”  

“You give me too much credit,” said Holmes seriously. “Let’s hear how you reached this very satisfying result.”  

The detective sat down in the armchair and puffed contentedly on his cigar. Then he suddenly slapped his thigh, overcome with amusement.  

“The funny part is,” he exclaimed, “that fool Lestrade, who thinks he’s so clever, has gone completely off on the wrong track. He’s chasing the secretary, Stangerson, who had no more to do with the crime than a newborn baby. I’ve no doubt he’s caught him by now.”  

The thought amused Gregson so much that he laughed until he nearly choked.  

“And how did you find your clue?” asked Holmes.  

“Ah, I’ll tell you everything. Of course, Doctor Watson, this is strictly between us. The first problem we faced was finding out about this American’s background. Some people would have waited for replies to their advertisements or for someone to come forward with information. That’s not how Tobias Gregson works. You remember the hat beside the dead man?”  

“Yes,” said Holmes. “By John Underwood and Sons, 129 Camberwell Road.”  

Gregson looked quite deflated.  

“I didn’t realize you noticed that,” he said. “Have you been there?”  

“No.”  

“Ha!” said Gregson, sounding relieved. “You should never ignore a lead, no matter how small it seems.”  

“To a great mind, nothing is small,” Holmes said sententiously.  

“Well, I went to Underwood and asked if he had sold a hat of that size and description. He checked his records and found it immediately. He had sent the hat to a Mr. Drebber, staying at Charpentier’s Boarding Establishment, Torquay Terrace. That’s how I got his address.”  

“Smart—very smart,” murmured Sherlock Holmes.  

“I next called on Madame Charpentier,” the detective continued. “I found her very pale and distressed. Her daughter was in the room too—an exceptionally beautiful girl, by the way. Her eyes were red, and her lips trembled as I spoke to her. I noticed that right away. I began to suspect something. You know the feeling, Mr. Sherlock Holmes, when you realize you’re on the right scent—it’s like a thrill through your nerves. ‘Have you heard about the mysterious death of your former boarder, Mr. Enoch J. Drebber of Cleveland?’ I asked.
