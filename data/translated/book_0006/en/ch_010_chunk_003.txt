At the head of the column rode about twenty stern, hard-faced men in dark homespun clothes, carrying rifles. When they reached the base of the bluff, they stopped and held a short meeting.  

“The wells are to the right, brothers,” said one—a clean-shaven man with gray hair and a firm mouth.  

“To the right of the Sierra Blanco—then we’ll reach the Rio Grande,” said another.  

“Don’t worry about water,” shouted a third. “The One who drew it from the rocks won’t abandon His chosen people now.”  

“Amen! Amen!” the whole group responded.  

They were about to move on when one of the youngest, sharp-eyed riders gave a shout and pointed toward the rocky crag above them. From its peak fluttered a small strip of pink, standing out clearly against the gray stone. At once, the men pulled up their horses and unslung their guns. More riders galloped forward to support the vanguard. The word “Redskins” was on everyone’s lips.  

“There can’t be many Indians around here,” said the older man who seemed to be their leader. “We’ve passed the Pawnees, and there aren’t any other tribes until we cross the great mountains.”  

“Shall I go ahead and check, Brother Stangerson?” asked one.  

“And I,” “and I,” shouted a dozen others.  

“Leave your horses here, and we’ll wait for you,” the Elder replied. In moments, the young men dismounted, tied up their horses, and began climbing the steep slope toward the object that had caught their attention. They moved quickly and quietly, confident and skilled like seasoned scouts. The men below watched them dart from rock to rock until their shapes stood out against the sky. The young man who had first raised the alarm led the way. Suddenly, his companions saw him throw up his hands in shock. When they joined him, they too were struck speechless by what they saw.  

On the small plateau crowning the barren hill lay a single huge boulder. Against it rested a tall, bearded man with a rugged face, so thin he looked half-starved. His calm face and steady breathing showed he was deeply asleep. Beside him lay a little girl, her round white arms wrapped around his brown, sinewy neck, her golden head resting on his chest. Her rosy lips were slightly open, showing small white teeth, and a sweet smile played across her face. Her plump white legs, ending in white socks and neat shoes with shiny buckles, made a strange contrast to the man’s long, shriveled limbs. On the rock ledge above them perched three solemn buzzards, which screeched harshly at the sight of the newcomers and flapped away in frustration.  

The cries of the birds woke the sleepers, who looked around in confusion. The man staggered to his feet and stared down at the plain that had been empty when he fell asleep, but was now filled with a vast crowd of people and animals. His face showed disbelief, and he rubbed his eyes with his bony hand. “This must be what they call delirium,” he muttered. The child clung to his coat and said nothing, her wide eyes full of wonder.  

The rescuers quickly convinced the two castaways that what they saw was real. One man lifted the little girl onto his shoulder, while two others supported her gaunt companion and helped him toward the wagons.  

“My name’s John Ferrier,” the man said. “Me and that little one are all that’s left of twenty-one people. The rest died of thirst and hunger down south.”  

“Is she your child?” someone asked.  

“I guess she is now,” he said defiantly. “She’s mine because I saved her. No man will take her from me. She’s Lucy Ferrier from this day on. But who are you?” he added, eyeing his strong, sunburned rescuers with curiosity. “There’s a mighty lot of you.”  

“Close to ten thousand,” said one of the young men. “We are the persecuted children of God—the chosen of the Angel Merona.”  

“Never heard of him,” said Ferrier. “Seems he’s chosen quite a crowd.”  

“Don’t mock what’s sacred,” the other said sternly. “We believe in the holy writings engraved in Egyptian letters on plates of beaten gold, given to the prophet Joseph Smith at Palmyra. We came from Nauvoo, Illinois, where we built our temple. We’re seeking refuge from the violent and the godless—even if it’s in the heart of the desert.”  

The name “Nauvoo” clearly stirred memories for John Ferrier. “I see,” he said. “You’re the Mormons.”  

“We are the Mormons,” they answered together.  

“And where are you headed?”  

“We don’t know. God’s hand is guiding us through our Prophet. You must come before him. He’ll decide what to do with you.”  

By now they had reached the base of the hill and were surrounded by crowds of pilgrims—pale, gentle-faced women, strong laughing children, and serious, watchful men. Cries of surprise and pity rose when they saw how young one stranger was and how worn-out the other looked. Their escort didn’t stop but pushed on, followed by a growing crowd of Mormons, until they reached a wagon that stood out for its size and bright decoration. Six horses pulled it, while the others had only two or four. Beside the driver sat a man no older than thirty, but his broad head and determined expression marked him as a leader. He was reading a brown-backed book, but when the crowd approached, he set it aside and listened carefully to the account of what had happened. Then he turned to the two castaways.
