### 3 – JOHN FERRIER TALKS WITH THE PROPHET

Three weeks had passed since Jefferson Hope and his companions had left Salt Lake City. John Ferrier’s heart ached whenever he thought about the young man’s return and the coming loss of his adopted daughter. Still, her bright and happy face comforted him more than any argument could. Deep down, he had always sworn that nothing would ever make him let his daughter marry a Mormon. To him, such a union wasn’t a real marriage at all—it was a shame and a disgrace. Whatever he thought about Mormon beliefs, on that point he would never bend. Yet he had to keep silent, because voicing an unorthodox opinion in those days in the Land of the Saints was dangerous.

Yes, dangerous—so dangerous that even the most devout whispered their private thoughts in fear, afraid that a single careless word might be twisted and bring swift punishment. The once-persecuted had become persecutors themselves, and of the most terrifying kind. Not the Inquisition of Seville, nor the German Vehmgericht, nor the secret societies of Italy had ever created a more dreadful system than the one that cast its shadow over Utah.

Its secrecy and mystery made it even more terrifying. It seemed all-knowing and all-powerful, yet no one ever saw or heard it directly. A man who defied the Church simply disappeared. No one knew where he went or what became of him. His wife and children waited at home, but no father ever returned to tell what had happened before those hidden judges. A careless word or reckless act could mean complete destruction, yet no one knew what form that destruction would take. It was no wonder that people lived in constant fear, unable even in the wilderness to whisper the doubts that tormented them.

At first, this shadowy power struck only at those who had joined the Mormon faith and later tried to abandon or distort it. But soon its reach grew wider. There were not enough adult women, and polygamy without women to marry was an empty doctrine. Strange rumors began to spread—rumors of murdered travelers and looted camps in places where no Indians had ever been seen. New women appeared in the Elders’ households—women who wept and wasted away, their faces marked with unending horror. Late-night travelers in the mountains spoke of bands of masked, armed men moving silently through the darkness. These tales multiplied, confirmed again and again, until they formed a clear identity. Even today, in the lonely ranches of the West, the name “Danite Band” or “Avenging Angels” is spoken with dread.

The more people learned about the organization behind these horrors, the greater their fear grew. No one knew who belonged to it. The names of those who committed bloodshed and violence in the name of religion were kept utterly secret. The very friend you confided in about your doubts of the Prophet might be one of those who came at night with fire and sword to exact vengeance. So everyone feared his neighbor, and no one dared speak of what lay closest to his heart.

One bright morning, John Ferrier was about to head to his wheat fields when he heard the latch click. Looking out the window, he saw a stout, sandy-haired, middle-aged man walking up the path. His heart jumped—this was none other than Brigham Young himself. Alarmed—for he knew such a visit rarely meant good news—Ferrier hurried to the door to greet the Mormon leader. But Brigham Young accepted his greeting coldly and followed him into the sitting room with a stern expression.

“Brother Ferrier,” he said, sitting down and studying the farmer keenly from under his pale lashes, “the true believers have been good friends to you. We rescued you when you were starving in the desert. We shared our food, led you safely to the Chosen Valley, gave you a fine piece of land, and let you grow rich under our protection. Isn’t that true?”

“It is,” John Ferrier replied.

“In return, we asked only one thing: that you embrace the true faith and live by its customs. You promised to do this, yet if what people say is true, you’ve failed.”

“How have I failed?” Ferrier protested, spreading his hands. “Haven’t I given to the common fund? Haven’t I attended the Temple? Haven’t I—?”

“Where are your wives?” Brigham Young interrupted, glancing around. “Call them in so I may greet them.”

“It’s true I haven’t married,” Ferrier admitted. “But women were few, and many others had stronger claims. I wasn’t alone—I had my daughter to care for me.”

“It’s about that daughter that I’ve come to speak,” said the Mormon leader. “She’s grown into the flower of Utah and has caught the eye of many important men.”

John Ferrier groaned inwardly.

“There are stories about her that I’d rather not believe—rumors that she’s been sealed to a Gentile. That must be idle gossip. What is the thirteenth rule of the sainted Joseph Smith’s code? ‘Let every maiden of the true faith marry one of the elect; for if she weds a Gentile, she commits a grievous sin.’ Knowing this, it’s impossible that you, a professed believer, would allow your daughter to break that law.”

Ferrier said nothing. He fidgeted nervously with his riding whip.

“On this point your faith will be tested,” Brigham Young continued. “The Sacred Council of Four has decided so. The girl is young, and we don’t wish to bind her to old age, nor deprive her of all choice. We Elders already have many wives, but our sons must also be provided for. Stangerson has a son, and Drebber has a son. Either would gladly welcome your daughter into his home. Let her choose between them. They are young, wealthy, and true believers. What do you say to that?”
