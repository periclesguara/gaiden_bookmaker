For several months, Jefferson Hope stayed hidden in the mountains, living a wild, lonely life while feeding the fierce desire for revenge that consumed him. Stories spread through the City about a strange figure seen roaming the outskirts and haunting the remote mountain passes. Once, a bullet whistled through Stangerson’s window and flattened itself against the wall less than a foot from him. On another occasion, as Drebber walked beneath a cliff, a huge boulder crashed down, and he escaped death only by throwing himself flat on the ground.  

The two young Mormons quickly realized who was behind these attacks. They led repeated expeditions into the mountains, hoping to capture or kill their enemy, but they always failed. After that, they took precautions—never going out alone or after dark and keeping guards around their houses. Eventually, since nothing more was seen or heard of their pursuer, they relaxed their vigilance, believing time had cooled his fury.  

In fact, time had only made it worse. Hope’s mind was tough and unbending, and revenge had taken such complete control of it that no other feeling could find a place there. Still, he was a practical man. He soon realized that even his strong body couldn’t endure the constant strain he was putting on it. Exposure and lack of proper food were wearing him down. If he died like an animal in the mountains, what would become of his revenge? Yet that would surely happen if he stayed. He knew that dying there would only serve his enemies, so he reluctantly went back to the old Nevada mines to recover his health and earn enough money to continue his mission without starving.  

He had planned to be gone for no more than a year, but unforeseen events kept him at the mines for nearly five. When that time ended, his memory of his wrongs and his hunger for vengeance were just as sharp as on the night he had stood beside John Ferrier’s grave. Disguised and using a false name, he returned to Salt Lake City, caring little what happened to him as long as he could achieve what he saw as justice. There he found bad news waiting. A few months earlier, there had been a split among the Chosen People—some of the younger members of the Church had rebelled against the Elders’ authority. As a result, several dissenters had left Utah and become Gentiles. Among them were Drebber and Stangerson, and no one knew where they had gone. Rumor said Drebber had turned much of his property into cash and left a wealthy man, while Stangerson was relatively poor. But there was no clue at all to their location.  

Most people, no matter how vengeful, would have given up faced with such a dead end. But Jefferson Hope didn’t hesitate for a moment. With the little money he had, supplemented by whatever work he could find, he traveled from town to town across the United States searching for his enemies. Years passed. His black hair turned gray, but still he roamed on—a human bloodhound, his mind fixed entirely on the single purpose that had taken over his life.  

At last, his persistence paid off. It was only a glimpse of a face in a window, but that one glance told him that Cleveland, Ohio, held the men he was hunting. He went back to his shabby lodgings with his plan of vengeance carefully laid out. But fate intervened again. Drebber happened to look out his window, recognized the wanderer in the street, and saw murder in his eyes. He hurried to a justice of the peace with Stangerson, who was now his private secretary, and claimed they were in danger from the jealousy and hatred of an old rival. That evening, Jefferson Hope was arrested. Unable to find anyone to post bail, he was kept in custody for several weeks. When he was finally released, he discovered that Drebber’s house was empty—both men had fled to Europe.  

Once more the avenger was thwarted, and once more his burning hatred drove him to continue the chase. But he lacked money. For a time, he had to go back to work, saving every dollar for the journey ahead. At last, when he had enough to keep himself alive, he set out for Europe. He tracked his enemies from city to city, taking whatever menial jobs he could find, but he never quite caught up with them. When he reached St. Petersburg, they had already gone to Paris. When he followed them there, he learned they had just left for Copenhagen. In the Danish capital, he arrived a few days too late again—they had moved on to London, where he finally succeeded in running them to ground.  

As for what happened there, we can do no better than to quote the old hunter’s own account, faithfully recorded in Dr. Watson’s Journal, to which we already owe so much.
