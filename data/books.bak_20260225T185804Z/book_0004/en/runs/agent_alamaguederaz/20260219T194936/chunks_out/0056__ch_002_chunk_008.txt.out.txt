He knew his rule would end the moment he had fulfilled Amalric’s purpose. He also knew that as long as he kept oppressing his own kingdom, the Nemedian would allow him to stay on the throne, because Amalric wanted to crush Aquilonia into complete submission, destroy its last trace of independence, and then seize it for himself. He planned to rebuild it in his own way with his immense wealth and use its people and resources to take the crown of Nemedia from Tarascus. Amalric’s ultimate ambition was to sit on an emperor’s throne, and Valerius knew it. Valerius didn’t know whether Tarascus suspected this, but he did know that the king of Nemedia approved of his ruthless actions. Tarascus hated Aquilonia with a hatred born from old wars. He wanted nothing more than the destruction of the western kingdom.  

And Valerius intended to ruin the land so completely that not even Amalric’s wealth could ever rebuild it. He hated the baron just as much as he hated the Aquilonians, and he only hoped to live long enough to see the day when Aquilonia lay in total ruin, and Tarascus and Amalric were locked in a hopeless civil war that would destroy Nemedia as well.  

He believed that conquering the still-defiant provinces of Gunderland, Poitain, and the Bossonian Marches would mark the end of his reign. By then, he would have served Amalric’s purpose and could be discarded. So he delayed the conquest of those provinces, limiting his actions to pointless raids and skirmishes, meeting Amalric’s demands for progress with all kinds of convincing excuses and delays.  

His life became a blur of feasts and wild debauchery. He filled his palace with the most beautiful girls in the kingdom, whether they came willingly or not. He cursed the gods and sprawled drunkenly on the banquet hall floor wearing the golden crown, staining his royal purple robes with spilled wine. In fits of bloodlust, he decorated the gallows in the market square with hanging corpses, fed the headsmen’s axes, and sent his Nemedian horsemen thundering across the land to plunder and burn. Driven to madness, the country was in constant turmoil, with desperate revolts savagely crushed. Valerius plundered, raped, looted, and destroyed until even Amalric protested, warning him that he would bankrupt the kingdom beyond recovery—unaware that this was exactly what Valerius intended.  

But while people in both Aquilonia and Nemedia spoke of the king’s madness, in Nemedia they also talked much about Xaltotun, the masked one. Yet few ever saw him in the streets of Belverus. People said he spent much of his time in the hills, holding strange meetings with the surviving remnants of an ancient race—dark, silent people who claimed descent from a forgotten kingdom. There were whispers of drums beating far up in the dreaming hills, of fires glowing in the night, and strange chants carried on the wind—chants and rituals forgotten for centuries, remembered only as meaningless words muttered beside mountain hearths in villages whose people looked oddly different from those in the valleys.  

No one knew the reason for these gatherings, unless it was Orastes, who often accompanied the Pythonian and whose face had grown increasingly haunted.  

But in the height of spring, a sudden whisper swept across the dying kingdom and stirred the land to life. It came like a soft wind rising from the south, awakening men sunk in despair. Yet no one could say exactly how it began. Some spoke of a strange, grim old woman who came down from the mountains, her hair streaming in the wind, followed by a great gray wolf like a loyal dog. Others whispered of the priests of Asura, who slipped like shadows from Gunderland to Poitain and into the forest villages of the Bossonians.  

However the word spread, rebellion flared like wildfire along the borders. Outlying Nemedian garrisons were stormed and slaughtered, foraging parties were wiped out; the west was in arms again. But this uprising had a different spirit—a fierce determination and inspired fury instead of the desperate hopelessness that had driven earlier revolts. It wasn’t just the common folk; barons were fortifying their castles and defying the provincial governors. Bands of Bossonians were seen moving along the edges of the marches—sturdy, determined men in brigandines and steel caps, longbows in hand. From the stagnant decay of ruin, the realm suddenly pulsed with life—dangerous, defiant life. So Amalric urgently summoned Tarascus, who came with an army.  

In the royal palace of Tarantia, the two kings and Amalric met to discuss the uprising. They had not called for Xaltotun, who was buried in his mysterious studies in the Nemedian hills. Since that bloody day in the valley of the Valkia, they had not sought his magical help. He had withdrawn from them, speaking little, seemingly indifferent to their schemes.  

They hadn’t summoned Orastes either, but he came anyway, pale as sea foam before a storm. He stood in the gold-domed chamber where the kings held council, and they stared in astonishment at his haggard face, seeing a fear they had never imagined Orastes could feel.  

“You look exhausted, Orastes,” said Amalric. “Sit on this couch, and I’ll have a slave bring you wine. You’ve ridden hard—”  

Orastes waved the offer away.  

“I’ve killed three horses on the road from Belverus. I can’t drink wine, I can’t rest, until I’ve said what I must say.”  

He paced back and forth as if some inner fire wouldn’t let him stand still, then stopped before his bewildered companions.  

“When we used the Heart of Ahriman to bring a dead man back to life,” Orastes said abruptly, “we didn’t think about the consequences of disturbing the black dust of the past. The fault is mine—the sin is mine. We thought only of our own ambitions, forgetting what ambitions that man might have had himself. And we have unleashed a demon upon the earth, a fiend beyond human understanding. I have delved deep into evil, but there’s a limit to how far I—or any man of my time and race—can go. My ancestors were pure men, untouched by demonic corruption; it’s only I who have sunk into darkness, and even my sin has its limits. But behind Xaltotun lie a thousand centuries of black magic and devilry, an ancient heritage of evil. He is beyond our comprehension not only because he is a sorcerer himself, but because he is the descendant of a race of sorcerers.”
