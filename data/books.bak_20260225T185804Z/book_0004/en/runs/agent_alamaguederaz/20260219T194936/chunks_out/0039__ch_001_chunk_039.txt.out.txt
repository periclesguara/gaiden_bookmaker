Cursing, the king slashed left and right with his broadsword, stabbing and tearing with his dagger. Blood-soaked fangs gleamed in the moonlight, filthy claws reached for him, but he hacked his way through to his stallion, grabbed the reins, and leaped into the saddle. His sword rose and fell, a cold arc in the moonlight, spraying blood as it split twisted heads and hacked through staggering bodies. The stallion reared, biting and kicking. They broke through and thundered down the road. For a short while, gray, hideous shadows flickered on either side. Then they fell behind, and Conan, reaching a wooded ridge, saw a vast stretch of bare slopes rising and rolling out before him.

13 - "A GHOST OUT OF THE PAST"  
"A Ghost Out of the Past"

Shortly after sunrise, Conan crossed the Argossean border. He had seen no sign of Beloso. Either the captain had escaped while the king lay unconscious, or he had fallen victim to the grim man-eaters of the Zingaran forest. But Conan had seen nothing to suggest the latter. The fact that he had lain undisturbed for so long suggested that the monsters had been busy chasing the captain instead. And if the man still lived, Conan was sure he was somewhere ahead on the same road. Unless Beloso had planned to enter Argos, he would never have taken the eastward road at all.

The helmeted border guards didn’t question the Cimmerian. A lone wandering mercenary needed no passport or safe-conduct, especially when his plain armor showed he served no lord. Through low grassy hills where streams murmured and oak groves cast shifting light and shadow across the turf, he rode on, following the long road that rose and dipped over valleys and ridges fading into the blue distance. It was an ancient road, this highway from Poitain to the sea.

Argos was at peace; heavy ox-carts rumbled along the road, and men with bare, sun-browned, muscular arms worked in orchards and fields that smiled beneath the roadside trees. Old men sitting on benches before inns under spreading oaks called out greetings to the traveler.

From the field workers, from the talkative old men in the inns where he quenched his thirst with great leather mugs of foaming ale, and from the sharp-eyed, silk-clad merchants he met on the road, Conan sought news of Beloso.

The stories conflicted, but Conan learned this much: a lean, wiry Zingaran with the dangerous black eyes and mustache typical of his people was somewhere ahead on the road, apparently heading for Messantia. It made sense; all the seaports of Argos were cosmopolitan, unlike the inland provinces, and Messantia was the most diverse of them all. Ships from every maritime nation filled its harbor, and refugees and fugitives from many lands gathered there. The laws were loose; Messantia thrived on sea trade, and its citizens found it profitable to turn a blind eye when dealing with sailors. Not only legitimate trade flowed into Messantia—smugglers and pirates did business there too. Conan knew all this well, for in the old days, when he had been a Barachan pirate, he had sailed by night into Messantia’s harbor to unload strange cargoes. Most of the pirates of the Barachan Isles—small islands off Zingara’s southwestern coast—were Argossean sailors, and as long as they preyed only on foreign shipping, the Argossean authorities were not overly strict in enforcing sea laws.

But Conan’s ventures had gone beyond those of the Barachans. He had also sailed with the Zingaran buccaneers, and even with the wild black corsairs who swept up from the far south to raid the northern coasts. That put him outside the protection of any law. If he were recognized in any Argossean port, it would cost him his head. Yet he rode toward Messantia without hesitation, stopping only to rest his stallion and snatch a few moments of sleep for himself.

He entered the city without question, blending into the crowds that constantly flowed in and out of the great commercial hub. Messantia had no walls. The sea and its ships guarded the great southern trading city.

It was evening when Conan rode slowly through the streets that sloped down toward the waterfront. At the ends of those streets he saw the wharves, the masts, and the sails of ships. He smelled salt water for the first time in years, heard the hum of ropes and the creak of spars in the breeze that whipped up whitecaps beyond the headlands. Once again, the pull of distant wandering stirred in his heart.

But he didn’t ride on to the docks. He turned aside and guided his horse up a steep flight of wide, worn stone steps to a broad street where ornate white mansions overlooked the waterfront and harbor below. Here lived the men who had grown rich from the hard-earned wealth of the seas—some old sea captains who had found treasure far away, but mostly traders and merchants who had never set foot on a bare deck or heard the roar of a storm or sea battle.

Conan turned his horse through a certain gold-ornamented gate and rode into a courtyard where a fountain tinkled and pigeons fluttered from marble ledges to marble paving. A page in a brightly patterned silk tunic and hose came forward curiously. The merchants of Messantia dealt with many rough and strange men, but most of them smelled of the sea. It was unusual for a mercenary soldier to ride so boldly into the courtyard of a wealthy merchant.

“The merchant Publio lives here?” Conan’s tone made it more a statement than a question, and something in his voice made the page remove his feathered cap as he bowed and answered, “Yes, my captain, he does.”

Conan dismounted, and the page called a servant, who came running to take the stallion’s reins.

“Your master is inside?” Conan asked, pulling off his gauntlets and brushing the road dust from his cloak and armor.

“Yes, my captain. Whom shall I announce?”

“I’ll announce myself,” Conan grunted. “I know the way well enough. Stay here.”

Obeying the firm command, the page stood still, watching as Conan climbed a short flight of marble steps, wondering what connection his master could possibly have with this giant warrior who looked every bit the northern barbarian.
