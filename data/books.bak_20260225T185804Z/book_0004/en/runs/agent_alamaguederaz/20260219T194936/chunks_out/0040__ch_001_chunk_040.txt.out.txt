The servants stopped what they were doing and stared with their mouths open as Conan crossed a wide, cool balcony that overlooked the courtyard and stepped into a broad hallway swept by the sea breeze. Halfway down, he heard the sound of a quill scratching and turned into a large room with wide windows that looked out over the harbor.

Publio sat at a carved teakwood desk, writing on fine parchment with a golden quill. He was a short man with a large head and quick, dark eyes. His blue robe was made of the finest watered silk, trimmed with gold cloth, and a heavy gold chain hung from his thick white neck.

When the Cimmerian entered, the merchant looked up, annoyed. Then he froze mid-gesture. His mouth fell open; he stared as if seeing a ghost from the past. Disbelief and fear flickered in his wide eyes.

“Well,” said Conan, “have you no greeting for me, Publio?”

Publio wet his lips. “Conan!” he whispered in shock. “Mitra! Conan! Amra!”

“Who else?” The Cimmerian unclasped his cloak and tossed it, along with his gauntlets, onto the desk. “Well, man?” he said irritably. “Can’t you at least offer me a cup of wine? My throat’s dry with road dust.”

“Yes, wine!” echoed Publio automatically. His hand reached for a gong, then jerked back as if from a hot coal, and he shuddered.

Conan watched him with a trace of grim amusement in his eyes as the merchant got up and quickly shut the door, first craning his neck up and down the hallway to make sure no slave was nearby. Then, returning, he took a gold vessel of wine from a nearby table and was about to pour into a slender goblet when Conan impatiently took the vessel from him, lifted it with both hands, and drank deeply with satisfaction.

“Yes, it’s Conan, all right,” muttered Publio. “Man, are you insane?”

“By Crom, Publio,” said Conan, lowering the vessel but keeping it in his hands, “you live in finer quarters than before. Only an Argossean merchant could turn a stinking little waterfront shop that reeked of rotten fish and cheap wine into this kind of wealth.”

“The old days are gone,” muttered Publio, pulling his robe around him with a slight involuntary shiver. “I’ve cast off the past like an old cloak.”

“Well,” said Conan, “you can’t cast me off like an old cloak. I don’t want much from you, but what I do want, I’ll have. You can’t refuse me. We did too much business together in the old days. Do you think I don’t know that this fine mansion was built on my sweat and blood? How many cargoes from my ships passed through your shop?”

“All merchants of Messantia have dealt with sea raiders at one time or another,” mumbled Publio nervously.

“But not with the black corsairs,” said Conan grimly.

“For Mitra’s sake, be quiet!” gasped Publio, sweat breaking out on his forehead. His fingers twitched at the gold embroidery of his robe.

“I just wanted to remind you,” said Conan. “Don’t be so scared. You took plenty of risks back when you were fighting for your life and fortune in that filthy little shop by the docks, hand in hand with every smuggler, pirate, and buccaneer from here to the Barachan Isles. Prosperity must have made you soft.”

“I’m respectable now,” began Publio.

“Meaning you’re rich as hell,” snorted Conan. “Why? How did you get rich so much faster than your rivals? Was it because you did such great business in ivory, ostrich feathers, copper, hides, pearls, and gold ornaments from the coast of Kush? And how did you get them so cheap while other merchants were paying their weight in silver to the Stygians? I’ll tell you, in case you’ve forgotten: you bought them from me, for far less than they were worth, and I took them from the tribes of the Black Coast and from Stygian ships—me and the black corsairs.”

“In Mitra’s name, stop!” begged Publio. “I haven’t forgotten. But what are you doing here? I’m the only man in Argos who knew that the king of Aquilonia was once Conan the buccaneer, back in the old days. But word has come south that Aquilonia has fallen and the king is dead.”

“My enemies have killed me a hundred times with rumors,” grunted Conan. “Yet here I sit, drinking Kyros wine.” And he did just that.

Lowering the vessel, now nearly empty, he said, “It’s a small thing I ask, Publio. I know you keep track of everything that happens in Messantia. I want to know if a Zingaran named Beloso—or he might be using another name—is in this city. He’s tall, lean, and dark like most of his people, and he’s likely trying to sell a very rare jewel.”

Publio shook his head. “I haven’t heard of such a man. But thousands come and go in Messantia. If he’s here, my agents will find him.”

“Good. Send them to look for him. In the meantime, have my horse cared for and bring me food here in this room.”

Publio agreed quickly, and Conan drained the wine vessel, tossed it carelessly into a corner, and strode to a nearby window, unconsciously expanding his chest as he breathed in the salt air. He looked down at the winding waterfront streets, swept his gaze over the ships in the harbor with appreciation, then lifted his eyes beyond the bay, far into the blue haze where sea met sky. His thoughts drifted beyond that horizon, to the golden southern seas under blazing suns, where there were no laws and life burned hot. Some stray scent of spice or palm brought back vivid memories of strange coasts where mangroves grew and drums thundered, of ships locked in battle and decks running red, of smoke, fire, and the cries of slaughter. Lost in thought, he barely noticed when Publio slipped quietly out of the room.
