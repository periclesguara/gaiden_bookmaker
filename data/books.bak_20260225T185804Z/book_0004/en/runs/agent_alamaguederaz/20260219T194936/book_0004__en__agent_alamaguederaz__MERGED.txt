THE HOUR OF THE DRAGON  

By Robert E. Howard  

O Sleeper, Wake Up!  

The long candles flickered, sending black shadows wavering along the walls, and the velvet tapestries rippled. Yet there was no wind in the chamber. Four men stood around the ebony table on which rested a green sarcophagus that gleamed like carved jade. In the raised right hand of each man, a strange black candle burned with an eerie greenish light. Outside, night reigned, and a lost wind moaned among the dark trees.  

Inside the chamber, silence hung heavy, broken only by the wavering of the shadows, while four pairs of eyes, burning with intensity, were fixed on the long green coffin, across which cryptic hieroglyphs seemed to writhe as if alive in the unsteady light. The man standing at the foot of the sarcophagus leaned over it and moved his candle as though writing with a pen, tracing a mystic symbol in the air. Then he set the candle down in its black-gold holder at the foot of the case, and, mumbling some formula unintelligible to his companions, he thrust a broad white hand into his fur-trimmed robe. When he drew it out again, it was as if he held a ball of living fire in his palm.  

The other three gasped sharply, and the dark, powerful man who stood at the head of the sarcophagus whispered, “The Heart of Ahriman!” The one holding the jewel lifted a quick hand for silence. Somewhere, a dog began to howl mournfully, and a stealthy step padded outside the barred and bolted door. But none of the men looked away from the mummy case, over which the man in the ermine-trimmed robe was now moving the blazing jewel while muttering an incantation that was already ancient when Atlantis sank. The glare of the gem dazzled their eyes so that they could not be sure of what they saw; but with a splintering crash, the carved lid of the sarcophagus burst outward as if from some irresistible pressure from within, and the four men, bending eagerly forward, saw the occupant—a shriveled, withered, wizened shape, with dried brown limbs like dead wood showing through rotting bandages.  

“Bring that thing back?” muttered the small dark man who stood on the right, with a short, mocking laugh. “It’s ready to crumble at a touch. We’re fools—”  

“Quiet!” hissed the large man who held the jewel, his tone sharp with command. Sweat stood on his broad white forehead, and his eyes were wide. He leaned forward and, without touching the thing with his hand, laid the blazing jewel on the mummy’s chest. Then he drew back and watched with fierce intensity, his lips moving in a soundless invocation.  

It was as if a globe of living fire flickered and burned on the dead, withered chest. The watchers sucked in their breath through clenched teeth. For as they watched, a horrifying transformation began. The withered shape in the sarcophagus was expanding, growing, lengthening. The bandages split and fell away into brown dust. The shriveled limbs swelled and straightened. Their dark hue began to fade.  

“By Mitra!” whispered the tall, yellow-haired man on the left. “He wasn’t a Stygian. That part, at least, was true.”  

Again, a trembling finger warned for silence. The hound outside had stopped howling. It whimpered, as if in a nightmare, and then that sound too faded into silence, during which the yellow-haired man clearly heard the heavy door strain, as if something outside pressed hard against it. He half turned, his hand on his sword, but the man in the ermine robe hissed urgently, “Stay! Don’t break the chain! And for your life, don’t go to the door!”  

The yellow-haired man shrugged and turned back—then froze, staring. In the jade sarcophagus lay a living man: tall, strong, naked, with pale skin and dark hair and beard. He lay motionless, his eyes wide open, blank and unknowing as a newborn’s. On his chest, the great jewel glowed and sparkled.  

The man in ermine reeled as if from the release of unbearable tension.  

“Ishtar!” he gasped. “It’s Xaltotun—and he lives! Valerius! Tarascus! Amalric! Do you see? Do you see? You doubted me—but I have not failed! We’ve stood at the open gates of hell tonight, and the shapes of darkness gathered close around us—yes, they followed him to the very door—but we have brought the great magician back to life.”  

“And damned our souls to eternal torment, no doubt,” muttered the small dark man, Tarascus.  

The yellow-haired man, Valerius, laughed harshly.  

“What torment could be worse than life itself? We’re all damned from birth anyway. Besides, who wouldn’t sell his wretched soul for a throne?”  

“There’s no intelligence in his eyes, Orastes,” said the large man.  

“He’s been dead a long time,” answered Orastes. “He’s like one newly awakened. His mind is empty after the long sleep—no, he was dead, not sleeping. We’ve brought his spirit back across the voids and gulfs of night and oblivion. I’ll speak to him.”  

He bent over the foot of the sarcophagus, fixing his gaze on the wide, dark eyes of the man within, and said slowly, “Awake, Xaltotun!”  

The man’s lips moved mechanically. “Xaltotun,” he repeated in a groping whisper.  

“You are Xaltotun!” exclaimed Orastes, like a hypnotist driving home his command. “You are Xaltotun of Python, in Acheron.”  

A dim flame flickered in the dark eyes.  

“I was Xaltotun,” he whispered. “I am dead.”  

“You are Xaltotun!” cried Orastes. “You are not dead! You live!”  

“I am Xaltotun,” came the eerie whisper. “But I am dead. In my house in Khemi, in Stygia, there I died.”  

“And the priests who poisoned you mummified your body with their dark arts, keeping all your organs intact!” exclaimed Orastes. “But now you live again! The Heart of Ahriman has restored your life, drawn your spirit back from space and eternity.”  

“The Heart of Ahriman!” The flame of memory grew stronger. “The barbarians stole it from me!”

“He remembers,” muttered Orastes. “Lift him out of the case.”

The others obeyed hesitantly, as if they were afraid to touch the man they had brought back to life, and they didn’t feel any easier when they felt firm, muscular flesh, pulsing with blood and life, under their fingers. But they lifted him onto the table, and Orastes dressed him in a strange dark velvet robe decorated with gold stars and crescent moons, then fastened a gold cloth band around his temples to hold back the black wavy hair that fell to his shoulders. He let them do as they wished, saying nothing, not even when they placed him in a carved, throne-like chair with a tall ebony back, wide silver arms, and feet shaped like golden claws. He sat there motionless, and slowly intelligence returned to his dark eyes, making them deep, strange, and luminous. It was as if long-buried witchlights were rising slowly through midnight pools of darkness.

Orastes cast a cautious glance at his companions, who stood staring in grim fascination at their strange guest. Their iron nerves had endured an ordeal that might have driven weaker men insane. He knew he was dealing with no cowards, but with men whose courage matched their lawless ambition and capacity for evil. He turned his attention back to the figure in the black chair. And at last, the man spoke.

“I remember,” he said in a strong, resonant voice, speaking Nemedian with a strange, ancient accent. “I am Xaltotun, who was high priest of Set in Python, which was in Acheron. The Heart of Ahriman—I dreamed I had found it again—where is it?”

Orastes placed it in his hand, and Xaltotun drew a deep breath as he gazed into the depths of the terrible jewel burning in his grasp.

“They stole it from me long ago,” he said. “It is the red heart of the night, powerful to save or to damn. It came from far away, and from long ago. While I held it, no one could stand against me. But it was stolen from me, and Acheron fell, and I fled into exile in dark Stygia. I remember much, but much I have forgotten. I have been in a far land, across misty voids and gulfs and unlit oceans. What year is this?”

Orastes answered him. “It is the waning of the Year of the Lion, three thousand years after the fall of Acheron.”

“Three thousand years!” murmured the other. “So long? Who are you?”

“I am Orastes, once a priest of Mitra. This man is Amalric, baron of Tor, in Nemedia; this one is Tarascus, younger brother of the king of Nemedia; and this tall man is Valerius, rightful heir to the throne of Aquilonia.”

“Why have you given me life?” demanded Xaltotun. “What do you want from me?”

The man was now fully alive and aware, his sharp eyes reflecting the activity of a clear, unclouded mind. There was no hesitation or uncertainty in his manner. He went straight to the point, as one who knows that no one gives something for nothing. Orastes met him with equal frankness.

“We have opened the gates of hell tonight to free your soul and return it to your body because we need your help. We want to place Tarascus on the throne of Nemedia, and to win for Valerius the crown of Aquilonia. With your sorcery, you can help us.”

Xaltotun’s mind was twisted and full of unexpected turns.

“You must be deeply skilled in the arts yourself, Orastes, to have been able to restore my life. How is it that a priest of Mitra knows of the Heart of Ahriman and the incantations of Skelos?”

“I am no longer a priest of Mitra,” answered Orastes. “I was cast out of my order because of my studies in black magic. If not for Amalric there, I might have been burned as a sorcerer.

“But that left me free to pursue my studies. I traveled in Zamora, in Vendhya, in Stygia, and through the haunted jungles of Khitai. I read the iron-bound books of Skelos and spoke with unseen beings in deep wells and faceless shapes in black, stinking jungles. I caught a glimpse of your sarcophagus in the demon-haunted crypts beneath the black, giant-walled temple of Set in the wilds of Stygia, and I learned the arts that could bring life back to your shriveled corpse. From decaying manuscripts I learned of the Heart of Ahriman. Then for a year I searched for its hiding place, and at last I found it.”

“Then why bother to bring me back to life?” demanded Xaltotun, his piercing gaze fixed on the priest. “Why didn’t you use the Heart to increase your own power?”

“Because no man today knows the secrets of the Heart,” answered Orastes. “Not even in legend do the arts survive that could unlock its full powers. I knew it could restore life; of its deeper secrets I am ignorant. I only used it to bring you back to life. It is your knowledge we seek. As for the Heart, only you know its terrible secrets.”

Xaltotun shook his head, staring broodingly into the flaming depths.

“My necromantic knowledge is greater than the combined knowledge of all other men,” he said, “yet even I do not know the full power of the jewel. I did not invoke it in the old days; I guarded it lest it be used against me. At last it was stolen, and in the hands of a feathered shaman of the barbarians it defeated all my mighty sorcery. Then it vanished, and I was poisoned by the jealous priests of Stygia before I could learn where it had been hidden.”

“It was hidden in a cavern beneath the temple of Mitra, in Tarantia,” said Orastes. “By secret means I discovered this, after I had located your remains in Set’s underground temple in Stygia.

“Zamorian thieves, partly protected by spells I learned from sources better left unnamed, stole your mummy-case from under the very claws of those who guarded it in the dark, and by camel caravan, ship, and ox-wagon it finally came to this city.

“Those same thieves—or rather, those of them who survived their dreadful quest—stole the Heart of Ahriman from its haunted cavern beneath the temple of Mitra, and even then, all the skill of men and the spells of sorcerers nearly failed. One of them lived long enough to reach me and place the jewel in my hands before he died, raving and gibbering about what he had seen in that cursed crypt. The thieves of Zamora are the most faithful men alive to their trust. Even with my enchantments, only they could have stolen the Heart from where it had lain in demon-guarded darkness since the fall of Acheron, three thousand years ago.”

Xaltotun lifted his lion-like head and stared far into the distance, as if he were peering back through the lost centuries.  

“Three thousand years!” he muttered. “Set! Tell me what has happened in the world.”  

“The barbarians who destroyed Acheron founded new kingdoms,” replied Orastes. “Where the empire once stretched, there now stand realms called Aquilonia, Nemedia, and Argos, named after the tribes that built them. The older kingdoms of Ophir, Corinthia, and western Koth, which had been subject to the kings of Acheron, regained their independence when the empire fell.”  

“And what became of the people of Acheron?” demanded Xaltotun. “When I fled into Stygia, Python was in ruins, and all the great purple-towered cities of Acheron were fouled with blood and crushed beneath the sandals of the barbarians.”  

“In the hills, small groups of people still claim descent from Acheron,” answered Orastes. “But for the rest, the tide of my barbarian ancestors swept over them and wiped them out. They—my ancestors—had suffered greatly under the kings of Acheron.”  

A grim, terrible smile twisted the Pythonian’s lips.  

“Yes! Many a barbarian, both man and woman, died screaming on the altar beneath this hand. I have seen their heads piled high to form a pyramid in the great square of Python when the kings returned from the west with their spoils and naked captives.”  

“Yes. And when the day of reckoning came, the sword showed no mercy. So Acheron ceased to exist, and purple-towered Python became only a memory of forgotten days. But the younger kingdoms rose from the ruins of the empire and grew strong. And now we have brought you back to help us rule these kingdoms, which, though less strange and wondrous than Acheron of old, are still rich and powerful—well worth fighting for. Look!” Orastes unrolled before the stranger a map skillfully drawn on vellum.  

Xaltotun studied it, then shook his head in confusion.  

“The very shape of the land has changed. It’s like something familiar seen in a dream, strangely distorted.”  

“Nevertheless,” said Orastes, tracing with his finger, “here is Belverus, the capital of Nemedia, where we are now. Here run the borders of Nemedia. To the south and southeast are Ophir and Corinthia, to the east Brythunia, and to the west Aquilonia.”  

“It’s the map of a world I don’t know,” said Xaltotun softly, but Orastes did not miss the dark, fiery gleam of hatred that flickered in his eyes.  

“It’s a map you will help us change,” said Orastes. “Our first goal is to place Tarascus on the throne of Nemedia. We want to do this without conflict, in such a way that no suspicion will fall on Tarascus. We don’t want the land torn apart by civil war, but to save all our strength for the conquest of Aquilonia.  

“If King Nimed and his sons were to die naturally—say, in a plague—Tarascus would ascend the throne as the next heir, peacefully and without opposition.”  

Xaltotun nodded silently, and Orastes went on.  

“The other task will be harder. We cannot put Valerius on the Aquilonian throne without war, and that kingdom is a formidable enemy. Its people are a hardy, warlike race, toughened by constant battles with the Picts, Zingarians, and Cimmerians. For five hundred years Aquilonia and Nemedia have fought on and off, and the advantage has always gone to the Aquilonians.  

“Their current king is the most famous warrior among the western nations. He’s a foreigner, an adventurer who seized the crown by force during a time of civil unrest, strangling King Namedides with his own hands right on the throne. His name is Conan, and no man can stand against him in battle.  

“Valerius is now the rightful heir to the throne. He was driven into exile by his royal kinsman Namedides and has been away from his homeland for years, but he is of the old royal blood, and many of the barons would secretly welcome the overthrow of Conan, who is a commoner with no royal or even noble lineage. But the common people are loyal to him, as are the nobles of the outer provinces. Still, if his armies were defeated in the battle that must come, and Conan himself slain, I think it would not be hard to place Valerius on the throne. Indeed, with Conan dead, the only center of power would vanish. He is not part of a dynasty—just a lone adventurer.”  

“I wish I could see this king,” mused Xaltotun, glancing toward a silvery mirror that formed one of the wall panels. The mirror cast no reflection, but Xaltotun’s expression showed he understood its purpose, and Orastes nodded with the pride of a craftsman recognized by a master of his art.  

“I’ll try to show him to you,” he said. Sitting before the mirror, he gazed deeply into its surface, where a dim shadow began to take form.  

It was eerie, but those watching knew it was only the reflection of Orastes’ thought, made visible in the mirror as a wizard’s thoughts appear in a magic crystal. It floated hazily, then suddenly sharpened into vivid clarity—a tall man, broad-shouldered and deep-chested, with a thick, corded neck and heavily muscled limbs. He wore silk and velvet, with the royal lions of Aquilonia embroidered in gold on his rich tunic, and the crown of Aquilonia gleamed on his square-cut black hair. But the great sword at his side seemed more natural to him than the royal finery. His brow was low and wide, his eyes a volcanic blue that burned with inner fire. His dark, scarred, almost sinister face was that of a warrior, and his velvet garments could not hide the hard, dangerous lines of his body.  

“That man is no Hyborian!” exclaimed Xaltotun.  

“No; he’s a Cimmerian, one of those wild tribesmen who live in the gray hills of the north.”  

“I fought his ancestors long ago,” muttered Xaltotun. “Not even the kings of Acheron could conquer them.”  

“They’re still a terror to the nations of the south,” said Orastes. “He’s a true son of that savage race, and so far, he’s proved himself unbeatable.”

Xaltotun didn’t answer; he sat staring down at the pool of living fire that shimmered in his hand. Outside, the hound howled again, long and shuddering.

2 - A BLACK WIND BLOWS  
A Black Wind Blows

The year of the dragon began with war, plague, and unrest. The black plague stalked through the streets of Belverus, striking down the merchant in his stall, the serf in his hovel, the knight at his banquet table. The healers’ arts were useless against it. People said it had come from hell as punishment for pride and lust. It was as swift and deadly as a viper’s strike. The victim’s body turned purple, then black, and within minutes he collapsed, dying, the stench of his own decay in his nostrils even before death tore his soul from his rotting flesh. A hot, roaring wind blew endlessly from the south, and the crops withered in the fields, the cattle sank and died where they stood.

People cried out to Mitra and cursed the king, for throughout the kingdom whispers spread that the king secretly indulged in vile practices and foul debaucheries in the darkness of his palace. Then, in that same palace, death stalked grinning, surrounded by the monstrous vapors of the plague. In one night the king died with his three sons, and the drums that thundered their funeral dirge drowned out the grim, ominous bells that rang from the carts lumbering through the streets, collecting the rotting dead.

That night, just before dawn, the hot wind that had blown for weeks stopped rustling through the silken window curtains. Out of the north rose a great wind that roared among the towers, with cataclysmic thunder, blinding lightning, and driving rain. But the dawn came clean, green, and clear; the scorched earth covered itself in grass, the thirsty crops sprang up again, and the plague was gone—its foul miasma swept from the land by the mighty wind.

People said the gods were satisfied because the evil king and his sons were dead, and when his young brother Tarascus was crowned in the great coronation hall, the crowds cheered until the towers shook, hailing the monarch upon whom the gods smiled.

Such a wave of joy and celebration sweeping the land often signals a war of conquest. So no one was surprised when it was announced that King Tarascus had declared void the truce made by the late king with their western neighbors and was gathering his armies to invade Aquilonia. His reason was straightforward; his motives, loudly proclaimed, gave his actions the glow of a holy cause. He took up the cause of Valerius, the “rightful heir to the throne.” He declared that he came not as an enemy of Aquilonia, but as a friend, to free its people from the tyranny of a usurper and a foreigner.

If there were cynical smiles in some quarters, and whispers about the king’s good friend Amalric, whose vast personal fortune seemed to be flowing into the rather empty royal treasury, they were ignored in the general wave of fervor and Tarascus’s popularity. If any shrewd minds suspected that Amalric was the real ruler of Nemedia behind the scenes, they were careful not to say so aloud. And the war moved forward with enthusiasm.

The king and his allies marched westward at the head of fifty thousand men—knights in shining armor with pennons streaming above their helmets, pikemen in steel caps and brigandines, crossbowmen in leather jerkins. They crossed the border, captured a frontier castle, burned three mountain villages, and then, in the valley of the Valkia, ten miles west of the border, they met the forces of Conan, king of Aquilonia—forty-five thousand knights, archers, and men-at-arms, the flower of Aquilonian strength and chivalry. Only the knights of Poitain, under Prospero, had not yet arrived, for they had far to ride from the southwestern corner of the kingdom. Tarascus had struck without warning. His invasion had followed immediately after his proclamation, without a formal declaration of war.

The two armies faced each other across a wide, shallow valley, with rugged cliffs and a shallow stream winding through reeds and willows down the middle. The camp followers of both sides came to the stream for water, shouting insults and throwing stones across at one another. The last rays of the sun gleamed on the golden banner of Nemedia with its scarlet dragon, fluttering in the breeze above King Tarascus’s pavilion on a rise near the eastern cliffs. But the shadow of the western cliffs fell like a vast purple shroud across the tents and the army of Aquilonia, and upon the black banner with its golden lion that flew above King Conan’s pavilion.

All night the fires blazed along the valley, and the wind carried the sound of trumpets, the clash of arms, and the sharp challenges of sentries pacing their horses along both sides of the willow-lined stream.

It was in the darkness before dawn that King Conan stirred on his couch, which was nothing more than a pile of silks and furs thrown on a dais, and awoke. He sat up suddenly, crying out sharply and grabbing for his sword. Pallantides, his commander, rushing in at the cry, saw his king sitting upright, hand on his hilt, sweat dripping from his strangely pale face.

“Your Majesty!” exclaimed Pallantides. “Is something wrong?”

“What of the camp?” demanded Conan. “Are the guards posted?”

“Five hundred horsemen patrol the stream, your Majesty,” answered the general. “The Nemedians haven’t tried to move against us in the night. They wait for dawn, as we do.”

“By Crom,” muttered Conan. “I woke with the feeling that doom was creeping on me in the dark.”

He stared up at the great golden lamp that shed a soft glow over the velvet hangings and carpets of the great tent. They were alone; not even a slave or page slept on the carpeted floor. But Conan’s eyes blazed as they always did in the face of great danger, and the sword trembled in his hand. Pallantides watched him uneasily. Conan seemed to be listening.

“Listen!” hissed the king. “Did you hear that? A stealthy step!”  

“Seven knights guard your tent, Your Majesty,” said Pallantides. “No one could come near without being challenged.”  

“Not outside,” growled Conan. “It sounded like it came from inside the tent.”  

Pallantides quickly looked around, startled. The velvet hangings blended with the shadows in the corners, but if anyone else had been in the pavilion, the general would have seen them. Again, he shook his head.  

“There’s no one here, sire. You’re sleeping in the middle of your army.”  

“I’ve seen death strike a king in the middle of thousands,” muttered Conan. “Something that walks on invisible feet and can’t be seen—”  

“Maybe you were dreaming, Your Majesty,” said Pallantides, a bit uneasy.  

“So I was,” grunted Conan. “And it was a devilish dream, too. I walked again all the long, weary roads I traveled before I became king.”  

He fell silent, and Pallantides stared at him without speaking. The king was a mystery to the general, as he was to most of his civilized subjects. Pallantides knew that Conan had walked many strange paths in his wild, eventful life, and had been many things before fate placed him on Aquilonia’s throne.  

“I saw again the battlefield where I was born,” said Conan, resting his chin moodily on his massive fist. “I saw myself in a panther-skin loincloth, throwing my spear at mountain beasts. I was a mercenary swordsman again, a hetman of the kozaki who live along the Zaporoska River, a corsair raiding the coasts of Kush, a pirate of the Barachan Isles, a chief of the Himelian hillmen. I’ve been all these things, and I dreamed of them all; all the forms I’ve taken passed before me like an endless procession, and their feet beat out a funeral rhythm in the echoing dust.  

“But through all my dreams moved strange, veiled figures and ghostly shadows, and a distant voice mocked me. And near the end, I seemed to see myself lying on this dais in my tent, and a shape bent over me, cloaked and hooded. I couldn’t move, and then the hood fell back and a rotting skull grinned down at me. That’s when I woke up.”  

“That’s an evil dream, Your Majesty,” said Pallantides, suppressing a shiver. “But nothing more.”  

Conan shook his head, more uncertain than dismissive. He came from a barbarian race, and the superstitions and instincts of his heritage lay close beneath the surface of his mind.  

“I’ve dreamed many dark dreams,” he said, “and most meant nothing. But by Crom, this one wasn’t like the others! I wish this battle were already fought and won, for I’ve had a grim feeling ever since King Nimed died of the black plague. Why did it stop when he died?”  

“Men say he sinned—”  

“Men are fools, as always,” grunted Conan. “If the plague struck everyone who sinned, then by Crom there wouldn’t be enough left alive to count! Why would the gods—who the priests claim are just—kill five hundred peasants, merchants, and nobles before killing the king, if the whole plague was meant for him? Were the gods striking blindly, like swordsmen in a fog? By Mitra, if I swung my sword that wildly, Aquilonia would have had a new king long ago.  

“No! The black plague isn’t a natural disease. It hides in Stygian tombs and is only brought forth by sorcerers. I was a swordsman in Prince Almuric’s army when we invaded Stygia, and of his thirty thousand men, fifteen thousand died by Stygian arrows, and the rest by the black plague that swept over us like a wind from the south. I was the only one who survived.”  

“Yet only five hundred died in Nemedia,” argued Pallantides.  

“Whoever summoned it knew how to stop it at will,” answered Conan. “So I know it was planned and evil. Someone called it forth, someone dismissed it when the job was done—when Tarascus was safe on the throne and being hailed as the savior of the people from the wrath of the gods. By Crom, I sense a dark, cunning mind behind all this. What about this stranger people say advises Tarascus?”  

“He wears a veil,” answered Pallantides. “They say he’s a foreigner—a stranger from Stygia.”  

“A stranger from Stygia!” repeated Conan, scowling. “A stranger from hell, more like!—Ha! What’s that?”  

“The trumpets of the Nemedians!” exclaimed Pallantides. “And listen—our own sound right after theirs! Dawn is breaking, and the captains are gathering the armies for battle! Mitra be with them, for many won’t see the sun set behind the cliffs.”  

“Send my squires to me!” exclaimed Conan, rising quickly and throwing off his velvet night-robe; he seemed to have forgotten his dark thoughts at the prospect of action. “Go to the captains and make sure everything is ready. I’ll join you as soon as I’ve put on my armor.”  

Many of Conan’s habits were strange to the civilized people he ruled, and one of them was his insistence on sleeping alone in his chamber or tent. Pallantides hurried from the pavilion, his armor clanking—the same armor he had put on at midnight after a few hours of sleep. He cast a quick glance over the camp, which was now alive with movement, armor clinking and men moving dimly in the uncertain light among the long rows of tents. Stars still glimmered faintly in the western sky, but long pink streaks stretched along the eastern horizon, and against them the dragon banner of Nemedia unfurled its billowing silk folds.  

Pallantides turned toward a smaller tent nearby, where the royal squires slept. They were already tumbling out, roused by the trumpets. And as Pallantides called to them to hurry, he froze in shock at a deep, fierce shout and the sound of a heavy blow from inside the king’s tent, followed by the sickening crash of a falling body. Then came a low laugh that turned the general’s blood to ice.  

Echoing the cry, Pallantides spun around and rushed back into the pavilion. He cried out again when he saw Conan’s massive body stretched out on the carpet. The king’s great two-handed sword lay near his hand, and a shattered tent pole showed where his strike had landed. Pallantides drew his sword and glared around the tent, but nothing met his eyes. Except for the king and himself, it was empty—just as it had been when he left it.

“Your Majesty!” Pallantides dropped to one knee beside the fallen giant.  

Conan’s eyes were open; they blazed with full awareness and recognition. His lips twisted, but no sound came out. He seemed unable to move.  

Voices sounded outside. Pallantides rose quickly and stepped to the door. The royal squires and one of the knights guarding the tent stood there.  

“We heard a sound inside,” said the knight apologetically. “Is everything all right with the king?”  

Pallantides looked at him sharply.  

“No one has entered or left the pavilion tonight?”  

“No one except you, my lord,” answered the knight, and Pallantides could see he was telling the truth.  

“The king stumbled and dropped his sword,” said Pallantides shortly. “Return to your post.”  

As the knight turned away, the general subtly motioned to the five royal squires, and when they had followed him inside, he pulled the flap closed. They went pale at the sight of the king lying on the carpet, but Pallantides’ quick gesture stopped their cries.  

The general bent over him again, and again Conan tried to speak. The veins in his temples and the cords in his neck bulged with the effort, and he lifted his head off the ground. At last a voice came, mumbling and barely understandable.  

“The thing—the thing in the corner!”  

Pallantides lifted his head and looked around fearfully. He saw only the pale faces of the squires in the lamplight, and the velvet shadows clinging to the walls of the pavilion. That was all.  

“There’s nothing here, Your Majesty,” he said.  

“It was there, in the corner,” muttered the king, tossing his lion-maned head from side to side as he struggled to rise. “A man—or at least he looked like a man—wrapped in rags like a mummy’s bandages, with a rotting cloak around him and a hood. All I could see were his eyes as he crouched there in the shadows. I thought he was a shadow himself until I saw his eyes. They were like black jewels.  

“I lunged at him and swung my sword, but I missed completely—how, only Crom knows—and shattered that pole instead. He grabbed my wrist as I stumbled off balance, and his fingers burned like hot iron. All my strength left me, and the floor rose up and hit me like a club. Then he was gone, and I was down, and—curse him!—I can’t move! I’m paralyzed!”  

Pallantides lifted the giant’s hand, and his skin crawled. On the king’s wrist were blue marks from long, thin fingers. What kind of hand could grip hard enough to leave its print on that thick wrist? Pallantides remembered the low laugh he had heard as he rushed into the tent, and cold sweat broke out on his skin. It hadn’t been Conan who laughed.  

“This is something evil!” whispered a trembling squire. “Men say the children of darkness fight for Tarascus!”  

“Be silent!” ordered Pallantides sternly.  

Outside, dawn was dimming the stars. A light wind came down from the peaks, carrying the fanfare of a thousand trumpets. At the sound, a convulsive shudder ran through the king’s massive body. Again the veins in his temples bulged as he struggled to break the invisible chains that held him down.  

“Put my armor on me and tie me into my saddle,” he whispered. “I’ll lead the charge yet!”  

Pallantides shook his head, and a squire tugged at his skirt.  

“My lord, we’re lost if the army finds out the king has been struck down! Only he could have led us to victory today.”  

“Help me lift him onto the dais,” said the general.  

They obeyed, laying the helpless giant on the furs and covering him with a silken cloak. Pallantides turned to the five squires and studied their pale faces for a long moment before speaking.  

“Our lips must stay sealed forever about what happens in this tent,” he said at last. “The kingdom of Aquilonia depends on it. One of you go fetch the officer Valannus, captain of the Pellian spearmen.”  

The chosen squire bowed and hurried from the tent, and Pallantides stood staring down at the stricken king while outside the trumpets blared, drums thundered, and the roar of the masses rose with the growing dawn. Soon the squire returned with the officer Pallantides had named—a tall, broad, powerful man, built much like the king. Like Conan, he had thick black hair, but his eyes were gray and his features were different.  

“The king has been struck by a strange illness,” said Pallantides briefly. “A great honor is yours: you are to wear his armor and ride at the head of the army today. No one must know it isn’t the king who rides.”  

“It’s an honor a man would gladly give his life for,” stammered the captain, overwhelmed by the command. “Mitra grant that I don’t fail in this mighty trust!”  

And while the fallen king stared with burning eyes that reflected the bitter rage and humiliation eating at his heart, the squires stripped Valannus of his mail shirt, helmet, and leg armor, and dressed him in Conan’s black plate armor, with the visored salade and the dark plumes nodding over the wyvern crest. Over all they placed the silken surcoat with the royal lion embroidered in gold on the chest, and they girded him with a broad gold-buckled belt that held a jewel-hilted broadsword in a cloth-of-gold scabbard. While they worked, trumpets blared outside, weapons clanged, and across the river rose a deep roar as squadron after squadron moved into position.  

Fully armed, Valannus dropped to one knee and bowed his plumes before the figure lying on the dais.  

“Lord King, may Mitra grant that I don’t dishonor the armor I wear today!”  

“Bring me Tarascus’ head and I’ll make you a baron!” In the heat of his anguish, Conan’s thin layer of civilization had fallen away. His eyes blazed, his teeth ground together in fury and bloodlust, as savage as any tribesman from the Cimmerian hills.  

The Aquilonian army was drawn up—long, serried lines of pikemen and horsemen in gleaming steel—when a giant figure in black armor emerged from the royal pavilion. As he swung into the saddle of the black stallion held by four squires, a roar that shook the mountains rose from the host. They lifted their blades and thundered their acclaim for their warrior king—knights in gold-chased armor, pikemen in mail coats and helmets, archers in leather jerkins with their longbows in their left hands.

The army on the far side of the valley was on the move, trotting down the long, gentle slope toward the river; their steel armor gleamed through the morning mist swirling around their horses’ hooves.  

The Aquilonian army advanced at an easy pace to meet them. The steady rhythm of the armored horses’ hooves made the ground shake. Banners unfurled their long silken folds in the morning wind; lances swayed like a forest of bristling spears, dipping and rising, their pennons fluttering around them.  

Ten men-at-arms, grim and silent veterans who knew how to keep their mouths shut, guarded the royal pavilion. One squire stood inside the tent, peering out through a slit in the doorway. Except for the few who were in on the secret, no one in the vast army knew that it wasn’t Conan riding the great stallion at the head of the host.  

The Aquilonian army had taken up the usual formation: the strongest part was the center, made up entirely of heavily armored knights; the wings were formed of smaller groups of cavalry, mostly mounted men-at-arms, supported by pikemen and archers. The archers were Bossonians from the western borders, sturdy men of medium height, wearing leather jackets and iron helmets.  

The Nemedian army advanced in a similar formation, and the two forces moved toward the river, their wings ahead of their centers. In the middle of the Aquilonian host, the great lion banner streamed its billowing black folds above the steel-clad figure on the black stallion.  

But on his dais inside the royal pavilion, Conan groaned in torment and cursed with strange heathen oaths.  

“The armies are closing in,” said the squire, watching from the doorway. “Listen to the trumpets! Ah! The rising sun flashes off the lances and helmets until I can hardly see. It turns the river red—aye, it will be truly red before this day is over!  

“The enemy has reached the river. Now arrows fly between the armies like stinging clouds that block the sun. Ha! Well shot, bowmen! The Bossonians have the upper hand! Hear them shout!”  

Faintly, through the blare of trumpets and the clash of steel, the deep, fierce roar of the Bossonians reached the king’s ears as they drew and loosed their arrows in perfect rhythm.  

“Their archers are trying to keep ours busy while their knights ride into the river,” said the squire. “The banks aren’t steep; they slope gently to the water’s edge. The knights are coming on—they crash through the willows. By Mitra, the clothyard arrows find every gap in their armor! Horses and men go down, thrashing and struggling in the water. It’s not deep, and the current isn’t strong, but men are drowning there, dragged under by their armor and trampled by panicked horses. Now the Aquilonian knights advance. They ride into the river and clash with the Nemedian knights. The water swirls around their horses’ bellies, and the clang of sword on sword is deafening.”  

“Crom!” Conan gasped in agony. Life was slowly returning to his veins, but he still couldn’t lift his massive body from the dais.  

“The wings are closing in,” said the squire. “Pikemen and swordsmen are fighting hand to hand in the stream, and behind them the archers keep shooting.  

“By Mitra, the Nemedian crossbowmen are being badly harassed, and the Bossonians are arching their arrows high to drop among the rear ranks. The enemy’s center gains no ground, and their wings are being pushed back from the river again.”  

“Crom, Ymir, and Mitra!” Conan raged. “Gods and devils, if only I could reach the battle—even if it meant dying at the first blow!”  

* * * * *  

Outside, through the long, hot day, the battle raged and thundered. The valley shook with charge and countercharge, with the hiss of arrows and the crash of splintering shields and lances. But the Aquilonian forces held firm. Once they were driven back from the bank, but a counterattack, led by the black banner flying over the black stallion, regained the lost ground. Like an iron wall, they held the right bank of the stream, and at last the squire brought Conan the news that the Nemedians were retreating from the river.  

“Their wings are in chaos!” he shouted. “Their knights are reeling back from the sword fight. But wait—what’s this? Your banner is moving—the center is crossing the river! By Mitra, Valannus is leading the army across!”  

“Fool!” groaned Conan. “It could be a trap. He should hold his ground; by dawn Prospero will be here with the Poitanian reinforcements.”  

“The knights are riding into a storm of arrows!” cried the squire. “But they don’t falter! They charge on—they’ve crossed! They’re attacking up the slope! Pallantides has thrown the wings across the river to support them! It’s all he can do. The lion banner dips and wavers above the chaos.  

“The Nemedian knights are making a stand. They’re breaking! They’re falling back! Their left wing is in full flight, and our pikemen are cutting them down as they run! I see Valannus, fighting like a madman. He’s lost in the fury of battle. The men no longer look to Pallantides—they follow Valannus, thinking he’s you, Conan, since he rides with his visor closed.  

“But look! There’s method in his madness! He’s swinging wide of the Nemedian front with five thousand knights, the best of the army. The main Nemedian host is in confusion—and look! Their flank is protected by cliffs, but there’s a pass left unguarded! It’s like a great crack in the wall that opens again behind the Nemedian lines. By Mitra, Valannus sees it and seizes the chance! He’s driven their wing before him, and now he’s leading his knights toward that pass. They swing wide of the main battle; they cut through a line of spearmen—they charge into the pass!”  

“An ambush!” cried Conan, struggling to rise.  

“No!” shouted the squire triumphantly. “The whole Nemedian army is in plain sight! They’ve forgotten the pass! They never expected to be pushed back that far. Oh, fool, fool Tarascus, to make such a blunder! Ah, I see lances and pennons pouring out from the far end of the pass, beyond the Nemedian lines. They’ll strike those ranks from behind and crush them. Mitra, what is this?”

He stumbled as the tent walls swayed like a drunkard. Far off, above the thunder of battle, rose a deep, booming roar that sounded horribly foreboding.  

“The cliffs are reeling!” screamed the squire. “Oh gods, what’s happening? The river’s bursting from its bed, and the peaks are collapsing! The ground’s shaking, and horses and armored riders are being thrown down! The cliffs! The cliffs are falling!”  

As he spoke, a grinding rumble came, followed by a thunderous crash, and the earth quaked. Over the roar of battle came screams of wild terror.  

“The cliffs have fallen!” cried the pale-faced squire. “They’ve crashed down into the gorge and crushed every living thing in it! I saw the lion banner wave for an instant through the dust and falling rocks, and then it vanished! Ha, the Nemedians are shouting in triumph! And well they might, for the fall of the cliffs has wiped out five thousand of our bravest knights—listen!”  

To Conan’s ears came a great wave of sound, rising higher and higher in panic: “The king is dead! The king is dead! Run! Run! The king is dead!”  

“Liars!” gasped Conan. “Dogs! Cowards! By Crom, if only I could stand—if I could crawl to the river with my sword in my teeth! What, boy, are they running?”  

“Yes!” sobbed the squire. “They’re spurring for the river; they’re broken, swept away like foam before a storm. I see Pallantides trying to stop them—he’s down, and the horses trample him! They’re rushing into the river—knights, archers, pikemen—all mixed together in one mad flood of ruin. The Nemedians are right behind them, cutting them down like wheat.”  

“But they’ll make a stand on this side of the river!” shouted the king. With an effort that made sweat stream down his temples, he pushed himself up on his elbows.  

“No!” cried the squire. “They can’t! They’re broken—routed! Oh gods, that I should live to see this day!”  

Then he remembered his duty and shouted to the men-at-arms who stood grimly watching their comrades flee. “Get a horse, quickly, and help me lift the king onto it. We can’t stay here.”  

But before they could obey, the first wave of the storm hit them. Knights, spearmen, and archers ran among the tents, tripping over ropes and baggage, mixed with Nemedian riders who struck down everyone they saw. Tent ropes were slashed, fires sprang up everywhere, and looting had already begun. The grim guards around Conan’s tent died where they stood, fighting to the end, and over their torn bodies thundered the hooves of the conquerors.  

But the squire had pulled the tent flap shut, and in the chaos of slaughter no one realized the pavilion still held someone inside. The flight and pursuit swept past and roared away up the valley, and soon the squire looked out to see a group of men approaching the royal tent with clear intent.  

“Here comes the king of Nemedia with four companions and his squire,” he said. “He’ll accept your surrender, my lord—”  

“Surrender, my ass!” growled the king.  

He had forced himself up to a sitting position. He swung his legs painfully off the dais and struggled to his feet, swaying like a drunk. The squire ran to help him, but Conan shoved him aside.  

“Give me that bow!” he snarled, pointing to a longbow and quiver hanging from a tent pole.  

“But your Majesty!” cried the squire in distress. “The battle’s lost! It would be fitting for a king to yield with the dignity of royal blood!”  

“I’ve got no royal blood,” Conan growled. “I’m a barbarian, the son of a blacksmith.”  

Snatching the bow and an arrow, he staggered toward the tent’s opening. His appearance was so fearsome—bare except for short leather breeches and a sleeveless shirt open to show his massive, hairy chest, his huge limbs, and his blue eyes blazing under his tangled black hair—that the squire shrank back, more afraid of his king than of the whole Nemedian army.  

Reeling on wide, unsteady legs, Conan tore open the tent flap and lurched out under the canopy. The king of Nemedia and his men had dismounted and stopped short, staring in shock at the sight before them.  

“Here I am, you jackals!” roared the Cimmerian. “I’m the king! Death to you, dog-brothers!”  

He drew the arrow to the head and let it fly, and it buried itself in the chest of the knight standing beside Tarascus. Conan hurled the bow at the king of Nemedia.  

“Curse my shaky hand! Come in and take me if you dare!”  

Reeling backward on unsteady legs, he fell with his shoulders against a tent pole, and propped upright, he lifted his great sword with both hands.  

“By Mitra, it is the king!” swore Tarascus. He glanced quickly around and laughed. “That other one was a jackal in his armor! In with you, dogs, and take his head!”  

The three soldiers—men-at-arms wearing the royal guard’s emblem—charged the king, and one struck down the squire with a mace. The other two met worse fates. As the first rushed in, sword raised, Conan met him with a sweeping blow that sliced through chain mail like cloth and cut the Nemedian’s arm and shoulder clean off. The man’s body fell backward across his companion’s legs. The second stumbled, and before he could recover, Conan’s great sword ran him through.  

Conan wrenched his blade free with a ragged gasp and staggered back against the tent pole. His huge limbs trembled, his chest heaved, and sweat streamed down his face and neck. But his eyes blazed with savage triumph as he panted, “Why do you stand so far off, dog of Belverus? I can’t reach you—come in and die!”  

Tarascus hesitated, glanced at the last man-at-arms and his squire—a tall, grim man in black armor—and took a step forward. He was much smaller and weaker than the giant Cimmerian, but he wore full armor and was known across the western lands as a master swordsman. But his squire caught his arm.

“No, your Majesty, don’t throw your life away. I’ll call the archers to shoot this barbarian, the same way we shoot lions.”

Neither of them had noticed that a chariot had come up while the fight was happening, and now it stopped before them. But Conan saw it over their shoulders, and a strange chill crept down his spine. There was something vaguely unnatural about the black horses pulling the chariot, but it was the person inside who caught the king’s full attention.

He was tall, powerfully built, dressed in a long, plain silk robe. He wore a Shemitish headdress, and its lower folds hid his face except for his dark, magnetic eyes. The hands gripping the reins, holding the rearing horses back on their haunches, were pale but strong. Conan glared at the stranger, every primal instinct on edge. He could feel an aura of danger and power radiating from the veiled figure, a threat as real as the silent ripple of tall grass that shows where a serpent moves.

“Hail, Xaltotun!” exclaimed Tarascus. “Here is the king of Aquilonia! He didn’t die in the landslide as we thought.”

“I know,” replied the other, not bothering to explain how he knew. “What do you plan to do now?”

“I’ll call the archers to kill him,” said the Nemedian. “As long as he lives, he’s a threat to us.”

“Even a dog can be useful,” said Xaltotun. “Take him alive.”

Conan laughed harshly. “Come and try!” he challenged. “If not for my treacherous legs, I’d chop you out of that chariot like a woodsman felling a tree. But you’ll never take me alive, damn you!”

“He speaks the truth, I fear,” said Tarascus. “He’s a barbarian, with the mindless fury of a wounded tiger. Let me call the archers.”

“Watch and learn wisdom,” said Xaltotun.

His hand slipped into his robe and came out holding something shiny—a gleaming sphere. He suddenly threw it at Conan. The Cimmerian contemptuously struck it aside with his sword—but the instant it touched, there was a sharp explosion, a burst of blinding white light, and Conan fell unconscious to the ground.

“He’s dead?” Tarascus’ tone was more statement than question.

“No. Only unconscious. He’ll wake in a few hours. Order your men to tie his arms and legs and lift him into my chariot.”

Tarascus gestured, and his men obeyed, grunting as they lifted the limp king into the chariot. Xaltotun threw a velvet cloak over Conan’s body, completely hiding him from view. He took up the reins.

“I’m going to Belverus,” he said. “Tell Amalric I’ll come if he needs me. But with Conan gone and his army shattered, lance and sword should be enough to finish the conquest. Prospero can’t have more than ten thousand men, and he’ll likely retreat to Tarantia when he hears the news. Say nothing to Amalric, Valerius, or anyone else about our capture. Let them believe Conan died in the rockslide.”

He stared at the man-at-arms for a long moment, until the soldier shifted uneasily under his gaze.

“What’s that around your waist?” Xaltotun demanded.

“My belt, my lord!” stammered the bewildered guardsman.

“You lie!” Xaltotun’s laugh was as merciless as a blade’s edge. “It’s a poisonous serpent! What a fool you are, to wear a snake around your waist!”

The man’s eyes bulged as he looked down—and to his horror, he saw the buckle of his belt rise up. It was a snake’s head! He saw its evil eyes, its dripping fangs, heard the hiss, and felt the disgusting movement of it around his body. He screamed in terror and struck at it with his bare hand, felt the fangs sink into his flesh—and then he stiffened and fell heavily to the ground. Tarascus looked down at him without expression. He saw only the leather belt and the buckle, its pointed tongue stuck in the man’s palm. Xaltotun turned his hypnotic gaze on Tarascus’ squire, and the man went pale and began to tremble, but the king intervened. “No, we can trust him.”

The sorcerer tightened the reins and turned the horses.

“Make sure this remains secret. If I’m needed, let Altaro, Orastes’ servant, summon me as I taught him. I’ll be in your palace at Belverus.”

Tarascus raised his hand in salute, but his expression was grim as he watched the departing sorcerer.

“Why would he spare the Cimmerian?” whispered the frightened squire.

“I’m wondering that myself,” grunted Tarascus.

Behind the rumbling chariot, the distant roar of battle and pursuit faded. The setting sun painted the cliffs in scarlet fire, and the chariot rolled into the vast blue shadows rising from the east.

4 – “FROM WHAT HELL HAVE YOU CRAWLED?”

Conan knew nothing of that long ride in Xaltotun’s chariot. He lay like a dead man while the bronze wheels clattered over mountain stones, swished through the tall grass of fertile valleys, and finally, descending from the rugged heights, rumbled steadily along the broad white road winding through the rich meadows toward the walls of Belverus.

Just before dawn, a faint spark of life stirred in him. He heard murmuring voices, the groan of heavy hinges. Through a slit in the cloak covering him, he saw, dimly lit by torchlight, the great black arch of a gateway and the bearded faces of soldiers, the torches glinting off their spearheads and helmets.

“How went the battle, my lord?” asked an eager voice in the Nemedian tongue.

“Very well,” came the curt reply. “The king of Aquilonia lies dead, and his army is broken.”

A burst of excited voices rose, then was drowned out by the whir of the chariot wheels on the stone. Sparks flew from under the rims as Xaltotun drove his horses through the arch. But Conan heard one of the guards mutter, “From beyond the border to Belverus between sunset and dawn! And the horses barely sweating! By Mitra, they—” Then silence swallowed the voices, leaving only the clatter of hooves and wheels echoing down the shadowed street.

What he had heard registered in Conan’s mind but meant nothing to him. He was like a mindless machine that could hear and see but not comprehend. Sights and sounds drifted around him without meaning. He sank again into a heavy stupor and was only vaguely aware when the chariot stopped in a deep, high-walled courtyard, and many hands lifted him out, carrying him up a winding stone staircase and down a long, dim hallway. Whispers, soft footsteps, and random noises swirled around him, distant and disconnected.

But when he finally awoke, it was sudden and perfectly clear. His mind was sharp, and he remembered everything about the battle in the mountains and what had happened afterward. He also had a good idea of where he was.

He lay on a velvet couch, still dressed as he had been the day before, but his limbs were bound with chains that even he could not break. The room around him was richly but gloomily decorated, the walls covered in black velvet tapestries, the floor in thick purple carpets. There was no visible door or window, and a single gold lamp, intricately carved and hanging from the ornate ceiling, cast a strange, reddish light over everything.

In that light, the figure sitting before him in a silver, throne-like chair looked unreal and dreamlike, its outline blurred by a sheer silk robe. Yet the face was sharply clear—unnaturally so in that uncertain glow. It was as if a strange aura surrounded the man’s head, throwing his bearded face into sharp relief, making it the only solid, undeniable reality in that eerie, ghostly room.

It was a striking face, with strong, classical features. But there was something unsettling about its calmness, a hint of knowledge beyond human understanding, a confidence deeper than any mortal certainty. And something about it tugged at Conan’s memory. He knew he had never seen this man before, yet the features reminded him of something—or someone. It was like coming face to face with a figure from a nightmare that had haunted his dreams.

“Who are you?” demanded the king fiercely, forcing himself upright despite the chains.

“Men call me Xaltotun,” came the reply, in a deep, golden voice.

“What place is this?” Conan asked next.

“A chamber in the palace of King Tarascus, in Belverus.”

Conan wasn’t surprised. Belverus, the capital, was also the largest Nemedian city near the border.

“And where’s Tarascus?”

“With the army.”

“Well,” Conan growled, “if you plan to kill me, why don’t you just do it and be done with it?”

“I didn’t save you from the king’s archers just to murder you in Belverus,” said Xaltotun.

“What in hell did you do to me?” Conan demanded.

“I shattered your consciousness,” Xaltotun replied. “How, you wouldn’t understand. Call it black magic if you like.”

Conan had already guessed as much, but his mind was turning over something else.

“I think I see why you spared me,” he rumbled. “Amalric wants to keep me alive as a counter to Valerius, in case the impossible happens and he becomes king of Aquilonia. Everyone knows the baron of Tor is behind this plan to put Valerius on my throne. And if I know Amalric, he doesn’t mean for Valerius to be anything more than a puppet—just like Tarascus is now.”

“Amalric doesn’t know you’ve been captured,” said Xaltotun. “Neither does Valerius. Both believe you died at Valkia.”

Conan’s eyes narrowed as he stared silently at the man.

“I knew there was a mind behind all this,” he muttered, “but I thought it was Amalric’s. So Amalric, Tarascus, and Valerius are just puppets dancing on your strings? Who are you really?”

“What does it matter? If I told you, you wouldn’t believe me. What if I said I could put you back on the throne of Aquilonia?”

Conan’s eyes burned into him like a wolf’s.

“What’s your price?”

“Obedience to me.”

“Go to hell with your offer!” Conan snarled. “I’m no puppet. I won my crown with my sword. And it’s beyond your power to buy and sell the throne of Aquilonia as you please. The kingdom isn’t conquered; one battle doesn’t decide a war.”

“You fight against more than swords,” said Xaltotun. “Was it a mortal’s blade that struck you down in your tent before the battle? No—it was a creature of darkness, a wanderer from the outer void, whose fingers burned with the frozen cold of the black gulfs, freezing the blood in your veins and the marrow in your bones. A cold so intense it seared your flesh like white-hot iron!

“Was it mere chance that made the man wearing your armor lead his knights into the pass? Chance that brought the cliffs crashing down on them?”

Conan glared at him without speaking, a chill creeping down his spine. His barbarian legends were full of wizards and sorcerers, and anyone could see this was no ordinary man. Conan sensed something strange about him—an aura that didn’t belong to this world, a feeling of immense and sinister age. But his stubborn spirit refused to yield.

“The fall of the cliffs was chance,” he said roughly. “The charge into the pass was what any man would have done.”

“Not so. You would never have led that charge. You would have suspected a trap. You wouldn’t have crossed the river at all until you were sure the Nemedian retreat was real. No hypnotic suggestion could have clouded your mind, even in the madness of battle, to make you reckless and rush blindly into the snare—as it did the lesser man who pretended to be you.”

“Then if all this was planned,” Conan said skeptically, “if it was all a scheme to trap my army, why didn’t your ‘creature of darkness’ just kill me in my tent?”

“Because I wanted you alive. It took no sorcery to foresee that Pallantides would send another man out in your armor. I wanted you alive and unharmed. You may still have a place in my plans. There’s a power in you greater than the cunning of my allies. You’re a dangerous enemy—but you might make a worthy servant.”

Conan spat fiercely at the word, and Xaltotun, ignoring his anger, took a crystal globe from a nearby table and set it before him. He didn’t support it or place it on anything—it hung motionless in midair, as solid as if it rested on an iron stand. Conan snorted at this display of sorcery, but he couldn’t help being impressed.  

“Do you want to know what’s happening in Aquilonia?” Xaltotun asked.  

Conan didn’t answer, but the sudden stiffness in his posture betrayed his interest.  

Xaltotun stared into the cloudy depths of the globe and spoke. “It’s now the evening after the battle of Valkia. Last night, the main body of the army camped near Valkia, while squadrons of knights chased the fleeing Aquilonians. At dawn, the host broke camp and pushed west through the mountains. Prospero, with ten thousand Poitanians, was miles from the battlefield when he met the fleeing survivors in the early morning. He had marched all night, hoping to reach the field before the battle began. Unable to rally the shattered remnants of the army, he fell back toward Tarantia. Riding hard, changing his tired horses for fresh ones seized from the countryside, he’s now approaching Tarantia.  

“I see his weary knights, their armor gray with dust, their banners drooping as they push their exhausted horses across the plain. I also see the streets of Tarantia. The city is in chaos. Somehow, word has reached the people of the defeat and the death of King Conan. The mob is mad with fear, crying that the king is dead and that there’s no one left to lead them against the Nemedians. Giant shadows rush toward Aquilonia from the east, and the sky is black with vultures.”  

Conan cursed deeply.  

“What are these but words? The poorest beggar in the street could predict as much. If you say you saw all that in your glass ball, then you’re a liar as well as a scoundrel—and there’s no doubt about the last part! Prospero will hold Tarantia, and the barons will rally to him. Count Trocero of Poitain commands the kingdom in my absence, and he’ll drive those Nemedian dogs howling back to their kennels. What are fifty thousand Nemedians? Aquilonia will swallow them whole. They’ll never see Belverus again. It wasn’t Aquilonia that was conquered at Valkia—it was only Conan.”  

“Aquilonia is doomed,” Xaltotun replied calmly. “Lance, axe, and torch will conquer her; and if they fail, powers from the darkness of ages will march against her. As the cliffs fell at Valkia, so will walled cities and mountains fall if the need arises, and rivers will roar from their channels to drown entire provinces.  

“Better that steel and bowstring prevail without further help from the arts, for the constant use of great spells sometimes sets forces in motion that could shake the universe.”  

“From what hell did you crawl, you night-born dog?” muttered Conan, staring at the man. The Cimmerian shivered involuntarily; he sensed something impossibly ancient, impossibly evil.  

Xaltotun lifted his head, as if listening to whispers across the void. He seemed to forget his prisoner. Then he shook his head impatiently and glanced at Conan with cold detachment.  

“What? Why, if I told you, you wouldn’t believe me. But I’m tired of talking to you—it’s less exhausting to destroy a walled city than to shape my thoughts into words a brainless barbarian can understand.”  

“If my hands were free,” said Conan, “I’d soon make a brainless corpse out of you.”  

“I don’t doubt it—if I were foolish enough to give you the chance,” Xaltotun replied, clapping his hands.  

His manner had changed; there was impatience in his tone and a certain nervousness in his movements, though Conan didn’t think this unease had anything to do with him.  

“Think about what I’ve told you, barbarian,” said Xaltotun. “You’ll have plenty of time. I haven’t yet decided what to do with you—it depends on events yet to come. But remember this: if I decide to use you in my plans, it will be better for you to submit without resistance than to face my wrath.”  

Conan spat a curse at him just as the hangings that covered a door swung aside and four huge black men entered. Each wore only a silk loincloth held by a belt from which hung a large key.  

Xaltotun gestured impatiently toward the king and turned away, as if dismissing the matter entirely from his mind. His fingers twitched strangely. From a carved green jade box he took a handful of shimmering black powder and dropped it into a brazier that stood on a golden tripod beside him. The crystal globe, which he seemed to have forgotten, suddenly fell to the floor, as if its invisible support had been withdrawn.  

Then the black men lifted Conan—so weighed down with chains that he couldn’t walk—and carried him from the chamber. A glance back, before the heavy, gold-bound teak door closed, showed Xaltotun leaning back in his throne-like chair, arms folded, while a thin wisp of smoke curled up from the brazier. Conan’s scalp prickled. In Stygia, that ancient and evil kingdom far to the south, he had seen such black dust before. It was the pollen of the black lotus, which brings deathlike sleep and monstrous dreams; and he knew that only the grim wizards of the Black Ring, the very depths of evil, willingly sought the scarlet nightmares of the black lotus to renew their necromantic powers.  

The Black Ring was a myth and a lie to most people in the western world, but Conan knew of its ghastly reality and its grim followers who practiced their vile sorcery in the black vaults of Stygia and beneath the shadowed domes of cursed Sabatea.  

He glanced back at the mysterious, gold-bound door, shuddering at what it concealed.  

Whether it was day or night, the king couldn’t tell. The palace of King Tarascus seemed a shadowy, dark place that shunned natural light. The spirit of darkness and shadow hung over it, and Conan felt that spirit was embodied in the stranger Xaltotun. The black men carried the king along a winding corridor so dimly lit that they moved like black ghosts bearing a dead man, and down a stone stairway that seemed to spiral endlessly. A torch in one man’s hand cast huge, twisted shadows along the wall; it was like the descent into hell of a corpse carried by dusky demons.

At last they reached the bottom of the stairs and then walked down a long, straight hallway. On one side was a blank wall broken here and there by arched doorways, each with a staircase leading upward behind it. On the other side was another wall lined with heavy barred doors spaced evenly a few feet apart.

Stopping in front of one of these doors, one of the black men took the key hanging from his belt and turned it in the lock. Then, pushing open the grille, they entered with their prisoner. They found themselves in a small dungeon with thick stone walls, floor, and ceiling. In the opposite wall was another barred door. Conan couldn’t tell what lay beyond it, but he doubted it was another hallway. The flickering torchlight shining through the bars hinted at a shadowy, open space beyond, echoing with depth.

In one corner of the dungeon, near the door they had entered through, a cluster of rusty chains hung from a large iron ring set into the stone. A skeleton dangled from these chains. Conan stared at it with grim curiosity, noticing the condition of the bare bones—most were splintered and broken. The skull, which had fallen from the spine, was crushed as if by a savage blow of tremendous force.

One of the black men, not the one who had opened the door, calmly removed the chains from the ring using his key on the heavy lock and dragged the pile of rusted metal and shattered bones to one side. Then they fastened Conan’s chains to that same ring, and the third man turned his key in the lock of the farther door, grunting once he was sure it was properly secured.

Then they looked at Conan in silence—tall, dark giants with narrow eyes, the torchlight glinting off their glossy skin.

The one holding the key to the nearer door spoke in a guttural tone: “This your palace now, white dog-king! Only master and we know. All palace sleep. We keep secret. You live and die here, maybe. Like him!” He kicked the shattered skull contemptuously, sending it clattering across the stone floor.

Conan didn’t bother to respond to the insult, and the man, angered by his prisoner’s silence, muttered a curse, bent down, and spat full in the king’s face. It was a fatal mistake. Conan sat on the floor, chained at the waist, ankles, and wrists to the ring in the wall. He couldn’t stand or move more than a yard away from the wall. But there was enough slack in the chains on his wrists, and before the man could pull his head back out of reach, Conan gathered the slack in his powerful hand and struck him on the head. The man dropped like a slaughtered ox, and his companions stared in shock at his body lying still, his scalp split open and blood seeping from his nose and ears.

They made no move to retaliate, nor did they accept Conan’s taunting invitation to come within reach of the bloody chain in his hand. Grunting in their ape-like language, they eventually lifted the unconscious man and carried him out like a sack of grain, his arms and legs dangling. They used his key to lock the door behind them but didn’t remove it from the gold chain attached to his belt. They took the torch with them, and as they moved down the corridor, the darkness crept in behind them like a living thing. Their soft, padded footsteps faded away along with the glow of their torch, leaving darkness and silence unbroken.

Conan lay still, enduring the weight of his chains and the hopelessness of his situation with the stoic patience of the wild lands that had shaped him. He didn’t move, because the clinking of his chains when he shifted sounded unnaturally loud in the deep silence. His instincts, born from countless generations of wilderness-dwellers, told him not to reveal his position while helpless. It wasn’t a conscious decision—he didn’t stay quiet because he reasoned that unseen dangers might be lurking in the dark. Xaltotun had told him he wouldn’t be harmed, and Conan believed it was in the man’s interest to keep him alive, at least for now. But the instincts of the wild were still there—the same instincts that had made him, as a child, lie silent and hidden while beasts prowled nearby.

Even his sharp eyes couldn’t pierce the solid darkness. But after a while—he couldn’t tell how long—a faint glow appeared, a slanting gray beam by which he could just make out the bars of the door beside him and even the skeleton hanging from the other grille. This puzzled him until he realized the reason. He was deep underground, in the pits beneath the palace, yet for some reason a shaft had been built from somewhere above. Outside, the moon had risen high enough for its light to slant dimly down the shaft. He realized he could use that light to tell the passing of days and nights. Perhaps the sun would also shine down the shaft, though it might be closed during the day. Maybe it was a cruel trick, allowing a prisoner only a glimpse of daylight or moonlight.

His gaze fell on the broken bones in the far corner, faintly glimmering. He didn’t waste thought on who the poor soul had been or why he had been doomed, but he did wonder at the shattered state of the bones. They hadn’t been broken on a rack. Then, as he looked closer, another grim detail became clear—the shinbones were split lengthwise. There was only one reason for that: they had been broken open to get at the marrow. But what creature other than a human breaks bones for their marrow? Perhaps those remains were silent proof of a horrible act of cannibalism, some wretch driven mad by starvation. Conan wondered if his own bones would one day hang there in rusty chains. He fought down the wild panic of a trapped beast.

The Cimmerian didn’t curse, scream, or weep as a civilized man might have. But the turmoil in his heart was no less fierce. His great muscles trembled with the intensity of his emotions. Somewhere far to the west, the Nemedian army was cutting and burning its way through the heart of his kingdom. The small Poitanian force couldn’t stand against them. Prospero might be able to hold Tarantia for weeks or even months, but eventually, without reinforcements, he would have to surrender to superior numbers. Surely the barons would rally to him against the invaders. But meanwhile, Conan himself lay helpless in a dark cell while others led his soldiers and fought for his crown. The king ground his powerful teeth in furious rage.

Then he froze when he heard a quiet step outside the far door. Straining his eyes, he made out a bent, shadowy figure beyond the grille. There was a faint rasp of metal on metal, and he heard the click of tumblers, as if a key had turned in the lock. Then the figure moved silently out of sight. Some guard, he thought, testing the lock. After a while, he heard the same sound again, fainter and farther away, followed by the soft creak of a door opening, and then the quick retreat of softly shod feet fading into the distance. Then silence returned.

Conan listened for what felt like a long time, though it couldn’t have been, since the moon still shone down the hidden shaft. He heard nothing more. At last, he shifted his position, and his chains rattled. Then he caught another sound—a lighter footstep, a soft tread outside the nearer door, the one through which he had entered the cell. A moment later, a slender figure appeared faintly outlined in the gray light.

“King Conan!” a soft voice called urgently. “Oh, my lord, are you there?”

“Where else?” he answered cautiously, turning his head to stare at the figure.

It was a girl, gripping the bars with her slender fingers. The dim light behind her outlined her graceful body through the thin silk wrapped around her hips and glimmered faintly on jeweled breastplates. Her dark eyes shone in the shadows, and her pale limbs gleamed softly, like polished stone. Her hair was a dark, rippling mass that caught the faint light with a burnished glow.

“The keys to your chains and the far door!” she whispered, and a slim white hand slipped through the bars, dropping three small objects with a metallic clink onto the stones beside him.

“What trick is this?” he demanded. “You speak the Nemedian tongue, and I have no friends in Nemedia. What devilry is your master planning now? Did he send you here to mock me?”

“It’s no trick!” The girl was trembling violently. Her bracelets and breastplates clinked against the bars she held. “I swear by Mitra! I stole the keys from the black jailers. They guard the pits, and each carries a key that opens only one set of locks. I got them drunk. The one whose head you broke was carried off to a healer, and I couldn’t get his key. But I stole the others. Oh, please, don’t waste time! Beyond these dungeons lie the pits that open into hell itself.”

Somewhat impressed, Conan tried the keys, expecting only failure and mocking laughter. But he was jolted to find that one actually worked, freeing him from his chains—it fit not only the lock that fastened them to the ring, but also the locks on his limbs. A few seconds later, he stood upright, exulting fiercely in his partial freedom. With a quick stride, he reached the grille, his fingers closing around a bar and the slender wrist pressed against it, trapping the girl, who lifted her face bravely to meet his fierce gaze.

“Who are you, girl?” he demanded. “Why are you doing this?”

“I am only Zenobia,” she breathed, her voice catching as if in fear, “just a girl from the king’s harem.”

“Unless this is some cursed trick,” Conan muttered, “I can’t see why you’d bring me these keys.”

She bowed her dark head, then lifted it again to meet his suspicious eyes. Tears sparkled like jewels on her long dark lashes.

“I am only a girl from the king’s harem,” she said, with a kind of proud humility. “He has never looked at me, and probably never will. I am less than one of the dogs that gnaw bones in his banquet hall.

“But I am no painted toy; I am flesh and blood. I breathe, hate, fear, rejoice, and love. And I have loved you, King Conan, ever since I saw you riding at the head of your knights through the streets of Belverus when you visited King Nimed, years ago. My heart longed to leap from my chest and fall in the dust beneath your horse’s hooves.”

Color flooded her face as she spoke, but her dark eyes did not waver. Conan didn’t answer right away; wild, passionate, and untamed as he was, even he felt a kind of awe at the raw honesty of a woman’s soul laid bare before him.

She bent her head and pressed her red lips to the fingers that held her wrist. Then she suddenly lifted her head, as if remembering the danger of their situation, and fear flared in her dark eyes.

“Hurry!” she whispered urgently. “It’s past midnight. You must go.”

“But won’t they flay you alive for stealing these keys?”

“They’ll never know. If the black men remember in the morning who gave them the wine, they won’t dare admit the keys were stolen while they were drunk. The key I couldn’t get is the one that opens this door. You must find your way to freedom through the pits. What horrors lie beyond that door, I can’t even guess. But greater danger waits for you if you stay here.

“King Tarascus has returned—”

“What? Tarascus?”

“Yes! He’s returned, in great secrecy, and not long ago he went down into the pits and came back pale and shaking, like a man who’s faced a terrible risk. I heard him whisper to his squire, Arideus, that despite Xaltotun, you must die.”

“What about Xaltotun?” murmured Conan.

She shuddered.

“Don’t speak his name!” she whispered. “Demons are often called by the sound of their names. The slaves say he lies in his chamber behind a bolted door, dreaming the dreams of the black lotus. I think even Tarascus secretly fears him, or he would have killed you openly. But Tarascus was in the pits tonight, and what he did there, only Mitra knows.”

“I wonder if that was Tarascus who fumbled at my cell door a while ago,” Conan muttered.

“Here—a dagger!” she whispered, pushing something through the bars. His eager fingers closed around the familiar shape. “Go quickly through that door, turn left, and follow the line of cells until you reach a stone stairway. For your life’s sake, don’t stray from the path of the cells! Climb the stairs and open the door at the top—one of the keys will fit it. If Mitra wills it, I’ll be waiting for you there.” Then she was gone, her light slippered footsteps fading into the darkness.

Conan shrugged and turned toward the farther grille. It might be some devilish trap set by Tarascus, but charging headlong into danger was less offensive to Conan’s nature than sitting quietly and waiting for death. He examined the weapon the girl had given him and smiled grimly. Whatever else she might be, that dagger proved she had practical sense. It wasn’t a slim stiletto chosen for its jeweled hilt or golden guard, meant only for dainty murders in a lady’s chamber; it was a straightforward poniard, a warrior’s weapon—broad-bladed, fifteen inches long, tapering to a diamond-sharp point.  

He grunted in satisfaction. The feel of the hilt lifted his spirits and filled him with confidence. Whatever webs of conspiracy surrounded him, whatever deceit and betrayal trapped him, this knife was real. The great muscles of his right arm swelled in anticipation of deadly blows.  

He tried the farther door, fumbling with the keys as he did so. It wasn’t locked. Yet he remembered the black man locking it. That furtive, bent figure hadn’t been a jailer checking the bolts—it had unlocked the door instead. There was something sinister about that unlocked door. But Conan didn’t hesitate. He pushed open the grille and stepped from the dungeon into the outer darkness.  

As he had suspected, the door didn’t open into another corridor. The stone floor stretched beneath his feet, and the line of cells ran away to the right and left behind him, but he couldn’t make out the other limits of the place he had entered. He could see neither the roof nor any other wall. Moonlight filtered into that vast space only through the cell grilles and was almost lost in the darkness. Eyes less sharp than his could hardly have made out the faint gray patches that floated before each cell door.  

Turning left, he moved quickly and silently along the row of dungeons, his bare feet making no sound on the stone. He glanced briefly into each cell as he passed. They were all empty but locked. In some, he caught the glimmer of bare white bones. These pits were relics of a darker age, built long ago when Belverus had been a fortress rather than a city. But clearly, their more recent use had been far greater than anyone in the world suspected.  

Ahead, he soon saw the dim outline of a stairway slanting sharply upward and knew it must be the one he sought. Then he suddenly whirled, crouching in the deep shadows at its base.  

Something was moving behind him—something large and stealthy, padding on feet that were not human. He looked down the long row of cells, before each of which lay a square of dim gray light, little more than a patch of thinner darkness. He saw something moving across those squares. What it was, he couldn’t tell, but it was massive and heavy, yet moved with more than human ease and speed. He glimpsed it as it crossed the gray patches, then lost it as it merged into the shadows between. Its stealthy advance was eerie, appearing and disappearing like a blur in his vision.  

He heard the bars rattle as it tested each door in turn. Now it had reached the cell he had just left, and the door swung open as it pulled. He saw a huge bulky shape faintly outlined in the gray doorway, and then it vanished into the dungeon. Sweat beaded on Conan’s face and hands. Now he understood why Tarascus had come so quietly to his door and fled so quickly afterward. The king had unlocked his door and, somewhere in these hellish pits, opened a cell or cage that held some monstrous creature.  

Now the thing was coming out of the cell again and advancing up the corridor, its misshapen head close to the ground. It no longer bothered with the locked doors. It was following his scent. He saw it more clearly now; the gray light revealed a giant, manlike body, but far bulkier and thicker than any human. It walked on two legs, though hunched forward, and its gray, shaggy coat gleamed with streaks of silver. Its head was a ghastly mockery of a human face, and its long arms nearly touched the floor.  

Conan recognized it at last—understood the meaning of those crushed and broken bones in the dungeon—and knew the creature that haunted the pits. It was a gray ape, one of the monstrous man-eaters from the forests along the mountainous eastern shores of the Sea of Vilayet. Half mythical and completely horrifying, these apes were the goblins of Hyborian legend—real-world ogres, cannibals, and killers of the dark forests.  

He knew it had caught his scent, for it was coming fast now, its barrel-like body rolling forward on short, powerful, bowed legs. He cast a quick glance up the long stairway but knew the creature would be on him before he could reach the distant door. He chose to face it head-on.  

Conan stepped into the nearest patch of moonlight to gain whatever advantage of visibility he could, for the beast, he knew, could see better than he could in the dark. Instantly the brute saw him; its great yellow tusks gleamed in the shadows, but it made no sound. Creatures of night and silence, the gray apes of Vilayet were voiceless. Yet in its dim, hideous features—a monstrous parody of a human face—there showed a ghastly joy.  

Conan stood ready, watching the oncoming monster without a tremor. He knew he had to stake his life on a single thrust; there would be no chance for another, no time to strike and leap away. The first blow had to kill—and kill instantly—if he hoped to survive that terrible grapple. His eyes swept over the short, thick neck, the hairy belly, and the massive chest, swelling in great arches like twin shields. He had to aim for the heart; better to risk the blade being turned by the heavy ribs than to strike where the wound wouldn’t be instantly fatal. Fully aware of the odds, Conan matched his speed, precision, and strength against the brute’s raw power and savagery. He would meet it chest to chest, strike a killing blow, and trust his own toughness to endure the brief but deadly struggle that would follow.

As the ape came charging at him, swinging its huge, deadly arms wide, he dove between them and struck with all his desperate strength. He felt the blade sink to the hilt into the creature’s hairy chest, and instantly, letting go of it, he ducked his head and tightened his whole body into one compact knot of muscle. As he did, he grabbed the closing arms and drove his knee hard into the monster’s belly, bracing himself against the crushing grip.  

For one dizzy moment he felt as if he were being torn apart by an earthquake; then suddenly he was free, sprawling on the floor, while the monster gasped out its life beneath him, its red eyes rolling upward, the knife hilt quivering in its chest. His desperate strike had done its work.  

Conan was panting like a man after a long battle, trembling in every limb. Some of his joints felt as if they had been pulled out of place, and blood dripped from scratches where the monster’s claws had raked him; his muscles and tendons had been brutally strained and twisted. If the beast had lived a second longer, it would surely have torn him apart. But the Cimmerian’s immense strength had held out, for that brief instant, against the dying convulsion of the ape that would have ripped a weaker man to pieces.  

Conan bent down and yanked the knife from the monster’s chest. Then he hurried up the stairs. He couldn’t guess what other horrors might be waiting in the darkness, but he had no wish to meet any more. This kind of close, desperate fighting was exhausting even for the giant Cimmerian. The moonlight was fading from the floor, the shadows closing in, and something close to panic drove him up the stairway. He let out a gusty sigh of relief when he reached the top and felt the third key turn in the lock. He opened the door slightly and craned his neck to look through, half expecting an attack from some human or beastly foe.  

He looked into a bare stone hallway, dimly lit, and a slender, graceful figure stood before the door.  

“Your Majesty!” It was a low, vibrant cry, half relief and half fear. The girl sprang to his side, then hesitated, suddenly shy.  

“You’re bleeding,” she said. “You’ve been hurt!”  

He brushed off her concern with an impatient gesture.  

“Just scratches that wouldn’t hurt a baby. Your little skewer came in handy, though. If not for it, Tarascus’ pet ape would be cracking my bones for the marrow right now. But what now?”  

“Follow me,” she whispered. “I’ll lead you outside the city walls. I have a horse hidden there.”  

She turned to lead the way down the corridor, but he laid a heavy hand on her bare shoulder.  

“Walk beside me,” he said quietly, wrapping his massive arm around her slim waist. “You’ve been straight with me so far, and I’m inclined to believe you; but I’ve survived this long only because I never trusted anyone too much—man or woman. So! If you betray me, you won’t live to enjoy it.”  

She didn’t flinch at the sight of the blood-stained knife or the feel of his hard muscles against her body.  

“Strike me down without mercy if I betray you,” she said. “The very feel of your arm around me, even in threat, is like the fulfillment of a dream.”  

The vaulted corridor ended at a door, which she opened. Outside lay another black man, a giant wearing a turban and silk loincloth, with a curved sword lying on the floor near his hand. He didn’t move.  

“I drugged his wine,” she whispered, stepping carefully around the motionless figure. “He’s the last and outer guard of the pits. No one has ever escaped from them before, and no one has ever wanted to go down there; so only these black men guard them. Only they, of all the servants, knew that it was King Conan whom Xaltotun brought in his chariot as a prisoner. I was watching, sleepless, from an upper window that looked into the courtyard, while the other girls slept; for I knew a battle was being fought—or had been fought—in the west, and I feared for you…  

“I saw the blacks carry you up the stairs, and I recognized you in the torchlight. I slipped into this wing of the palace tonight, just in time to see them take you to the pits. I hadn’t dared come here before nightfall. You must have lain in drugged unconsciousness all day in Xaltotun’s chamber.  

“Oh, we must be careful! Strange things are happening in the palace tonight. The slaves said Xaltotun sleeps as he often does, drugged by the lotus of Stygia, but Tarascus is in the palace. He entered secretly through the postern gate, his cloak dusty as if from long travel, attended only by his squire, the lean, silent Arideus. I don’t understand it, but I’m afraid.”  

They came to the foot of a narrow, winding stair, and climbing it, passed through a narrow panel that she slid aside. When they had gone through, she slipped it back into place, and it became just another part of the ornate wall. They were now in a broader corridor, carpeted and hung with tapestries, where golden light from hanging lamps glowed softly.  

Conan listened intently, but heard no sound anywhere in the palace. He didn’t know what part of the palace he was in, or which direction led to Xaltotun’s chamber. The girl was trembling as she led him along the corridor, stopping finally beside an alcove hidden by satin drapes. Pulling these aside, she motioned for him to step into the niche and whispered, “Wait here! Beyond that door at the end of the corridor, we could meet slaves or eunuchs at any hour. I’ll go see if the way is clear before we try it.”  

At once his instinctive suspicion flared up.  

“Are you leading me into a trap?”  

Tears welled in her dark eyes. She dropped to her knees and seized his strong hand.  

“Oh, my king, don’t doubt me now!” Her voice trembled with desperate urgency. “If you hesitate, we’re lost! Why would I bring you up out of the pits only to betray you now?”  

“All right,” he muttered. “I’ll trust you—though, by Crom, the habits of a lifetime aren’t easy to break. Still, I wouldn’t harm you now, even if you brought all the swordsmen in Nemedia down on me. If not for you, Tarascus’ damned ape would have found me chained and unarmed. Do as you will, girl.”

Kissing his hands, she sprang up lightly and ran down the hallway, disappearing through a heavy double door.  

He looked after her, wondering if he was a fool to trust her; then he shrugged his broad shoulders and pulled the satin curtains together, hiding his refuge. It wasn’t strange that a passionate young woman would risk her life to help him; such things had happened often enough before. Many women had looked on him with favor, both during his wanderings and in his days as a king.  

Still, he didn’t stay motionless in the alcove, waiting for her to return. Following his instincts, he searched the niche for another exit and soon found one—the entrance to a narrow passage hidden behind the tapestries, leading to an ornately carved door barely visible in the dim light filtering in from the outer corridor. As he stared into it, he heard, somewhere beyond that carved door, the sound of another door opening and closing, followed by a low murmur of voices. The sound of one of those voices, familiar to him, made a dark, dangerous expression cross his face. Without hesitation, he slipped down the passage and crouched beside the door like a stalking panther. It wasn’t locked, and with careful precision he pushed it open a crack, showing a reckless disregard for danger that only he could have explained or justified.  

The other side was also covered by tapestries, but through a thin slit in the velvet he could see into a room lit by a candle on an ebony table. Two men were there. One was a scarred, sinister-looking thug in leather breeches and a ragged cloak; the other was Tarascus, king of Nemedia.  

Tarascus looked uneasy. He was slightly pale and kept starting and glancing around, as if expecting and dreading some sound or movement.  

“Go quickly, right now,” he was saying. “He’s deep in a drugged sleep, but I don’t know when he might wake.”  

“Strange to hear words of fear coming from Tarascus,” rumbled the other in a harsh, deep voice.  

The king frowned.  

“I fear no ordinary man, as you well know. But when I saw the cliffs fall at Valkia, I knew that the devil we had brought back to life was no fraud. I fear his powers because I don’t know their full extent. But I do know they’re somehow tied to this cursed thing I stole from him. It brought him back to life, so it must be the source of his sorcery.  

“He had it well hidden, but under my secret orders a slave spied on him and saw him put it in a golden chest and hide the chest. Even then, I wouldn’t have dared steal it if Xaltotun hadn’t been sunk in lotus sleep.  

“I believe it’s the secret of his power. Orastes used it to bring him back to life. With it, he’ll make us all his slaves if we’re not careful. So take it and throw it into the sea as I’ve commanded. Make sure you’re far enough from land that neither tide nor storm can wash it ashore. You’ve been paid.”  

“So I have,” grunted the thug. “And I owe you more than gold, king; I owe you my gratitude. Even thieves can be thankful.”  

“Whatever debt you think you owe me,” said Tarascus, “will be paid when you’ve thrown that thing into the sea.”  

“I’ll ride for Zingara and take a ship from Kordava,” promised the other. “I can’t show my face in Argos because of a murder or two—”  

“I don’t care, as long as it’s done. Here it is; a horse is waiting for you in the courtyard. Go, and go quickly!”  

Something passed between them, something that glowed like living fire. Conan caught only a brief glimpse of it before the thug pulled a slouch hat low over his eyes, wrapped his cloak around his shoulders, and hurried out of the room. As the door closed behind him, Conan moved with the savage fury of unchained bloodlust. He had held himself back as long as he could. The sight of his enemy so close drove his wild blood into a frenzy and swept away all caution and restraint.  

Tarascus was turning toward an inner door when Conan tore aside the hangings and leaped into the room like a blood-crazed panther. Tarascus spun around, but before he could even recognize his attacker, Conan’s dagger slashed into him.  

But the blow wasn’t fatal, as Conan realized instantly. His foot had caught in a fold of the curtain and tripped him mid-leap. The blade sank into Tarascus’s shoulder and tore down along his ribs, and the king of Nemedia screamed.  

The force of the blow and Conan’s lunging body knocked him back against the table, which toppled over, snuffing out the candle. Both men crashed to the floor in the darkness, tangled in the fallen tapestry. Conan stabbed blindly in the dark while Tarascus screamed in panic. As if fear gave him superhuman strength, Tarascus tore free and stumbled away, shouting, “Help! Guards! Arideus! Orastes! Orastes!”  

Conan rose, kicking himself free from the tangled cloth and broken table, cursing bitterly in frustration. He was disoriented and didn’t know the layout of the palace. Tarascus’s screams still echoed in the distance, and a wild uproar was rising in response. The Nemedian had escaped him in the dark, and Conan didn’t know which way he had gone. His reckless strike for vengeance had failed, and now he had only one task left—to save his own life if he could.  

Swearing fiercely, Conan ran back down the passage and into the alcove, glaring out into the lit corridor just as Zenobia came running toward him, her dark eyes wide with terror.  

“Oh, what’s happened?” she cried. “The whole palace is awake! I swear I didn’t betray you—”  

“No, I stirred up this hornet’s nest myself,” he grunted. “I tried to settle a score. What’s the quickest way out of here?”  

She grabbed his wrist and ran swiftly down the corridor. But before they reached the heavy door at the far end, muffled shouts rose from behind it, and the doors began to shake under blows from the other side. Zenobia wrung her hands and whimpered.

“We’re trapped! I locked that door when I came back through it. But they’ll break it down any second. The way to the postern gate is through there.”

Conan spun around. Up the corridor, though still out of sight, he heard a growing uproar that told him his enemies were behind him as well as in front.

“Quick! In here!” the girl cried desperately, running across the corridor and throwing open the door to a chamber.

Conan followed her inside and snapped the golden latch behind them. They stood in an elaborately furnished room, empty except for themselves, and she pulled him toward a window barred with gold, through which he could see trees and shrubs.

“You’re strong,” she gasped. “If you can tear these bars away, you might still get out. The garden is full of guards, but the bushes are thick—you might avoid them. The southern wall is also the city’s outer wall. Once you’re over that, you have a chance to escape. A horse is hidden for you in a thicket beside the road that runs west, a few hundred paces south of the Fountain of Thrallos. You know where that is?”

“Yes! But what about you? I meant to take you with me.”

A rush of joy lit up her beautiful face.

“Then my happiness is complete! But I won’t slow you down. If you try to take me, you’ll fail. No, don’t worry about me. They’ll never suspect that I helped you willingly. Go! What you just said will make my life shine for all the years to come.”

He caught her up in his iron arms, crushed her slender, trembling body against him, and kissed her fiercely on eyes, cheeks, throat, and lips until she lay gasping in his embrace. Wild and stormy as a tempest, even his love was violent.

“I’ll go,” he muttered. “But by Crom, I’ll come back for you one day!”

Turning, he gripped the gold bars and ripped them from their sockets with one mighty wrench, swung a leg over the sill, and climbed down swiftly, clinging to the carvings on the wall. He hit the ground running and vanished like a shadow into the maze of tall rosebushes and spreading trees. One glance back showed Zenobia leaning over the window ledge, her arms stretched out after him in silent farewell and sacrifice.

Guards were running through the garden, all heading toward the palace, where the noise grew louder by the moment—tall men in shining cuirasses and crested bronze helmets. The starlight flashed off their polished armor among the trees, giving away their every move, but the sound of their coming reached far ahead of them. To Conan, raised in the wild, their rush through the shrubbery sounded like a herd of stampeding cattle. Some passed within a few feet of where he lay flat in a thick clump of bushes, never guessing he was there. Focused on the palace, they noticed nothing else. When they had gone shouting past, he rose and slipped through the garden as silently as a panther.

Soon he reached the southern wall and climbed the steps leading to the parapet. The wall was built to keep people out, not in. No sentry was in sight along the battlements. Crouching by an embrasure, he looked back at the great palace rising above the cypresses behind him. Lights blazed from every window, and he could see figures moving back and forth across them like puppets on invisible strings. He grinned grimly, shook his fist in a gesture of farewell and defiance, and lowered himself over the outer edge of the parapet.

A low tree a few yards below caught his weight as he dropped silently into its branches. An instant later he was racing through the shadows with the long, swinging stride of a hillman that devours distance.

Gardens and pleasure villas surrounded the walls of Belverus. Drowsy slaves, sleeping beside their watch pikes, never saw the swift, stealthy figure that climbed walls, crossed alleys shaded by arching trees, and slipped noiselessly through orchards and vineyards. Watchdogs awoke and lifted their deep, booming barks at a gliding shadow—half seen, half sensed—and then it was gone.

In a chamber of the palace, Tarascus writhed and cursed on a blood-stained couch under the quick, skilled hands of Orastes. The palace was full of wide-eyed, trembling servants, but the room where the king lay was empty except for him and the renegade priest.

“Are you sure he’s still asleep?” Tarascus demanded again, clenching his teeth against the sting of the herbal juices Orastes was using to bandage the long, ragged cut in his shoulder and ribs. “Ishtar, Mitra, and Set! That burns like molten pitch from hell!”

“Which you’d be feeling for eternity if not for your luck,” said Orastes. “Whoever swung that knife meant to kill. Yes, I’ve told you Xaltotun still sleeps. Why are you so insistent about that? What’s he got to do with this?”

“You don’t know what’s happened in the palace tonight?” Tarascus searched the priest’s face with burning intensity.

“Nothing. As you know, I’ve been busy for months translating manuscripts for Xaltotun—transcribing ancient texts written in the newer tongues into script he can read. He knew all the languages and writings of his own age, but he hasn’t yet learned all the modern ones, and to save time he has me translate these works for him, to see if any new knowledge has been discovered since his era. I didn’t even know he had returned last night until he sent for me and told me about the battle. Then I went back to my studies, and I didn’t know you had come back until the uproar in the palace drew me out of my cell.”

“Then you don’t know that Xaltotun brought the king of Aquilonia here as a captive?”

Orastes shook his head, not particularly surprised.

“Xaltotun only said that Conan would trouble us no more. I assumed he was dead, but I didn’t ask for details.”

“Xaltotun saved his life when I would have killed him,” snarled Tarascus. “I saw his plan instantly. He means to keep Conan alive to use as a weapon against us—against Amalric, against Valerius, and against me. As long as Conan lives, he’s a threat, a rallying point for Aquilonia, something that could be used to force us into actions we wouldn’t otherwise take. I don’t trust that undead sorcerer from Python. Lately, I’ve begun to fear him.”

I followed him a few hours after he had gone east. I wanted to find out what he planned to do with Conan. I discovered that he had locked him up in the pits. I meant to make sure the barbarian died, no matter what Xaltotun wanted. And I succeeded—

A careful knock came at the door.

“That’s Arideus,” grunted Tarascus. “Let him in.”

The dark, grim-faced squire entered, his eyes blazing with barely contained excitement.

“Well, Arideus?” exclaimed Tarascus. “Have you found the man who attacked me?”

“You didn’t see him, my lord?” asked Arideus, as if confirming something he already suspected. “You didn’t recognize him?”

“No. It happened so fast, and the candle went out—my only thought was that it was some demon unleashed on me by Xaltotun’s sorcery—”

“The Pythonian sleeps in his locked and barred chamber. But I’ve been down in the pits.” Arideus’s thin shoulders twitched with excitement.

“Well, speak, man!” snapped Tarascus impatiently. “What did you find there?”

“An empty cell,” whispered the squire. “And the body of the great ape!”

“What?” Tarascus jerked upright, and blood spurted from his reopened wound.

“Yes! The man-eater is dead—stabbed through the heart—and Conan is gone!”

Tarascus’s face turned ashen as he let Orastes push him flat again while the priest resumed tending to his torn flesh.

“Conan!” he repeated. “Not a crushed corpse—escaped! Mitra! He’s no man, but a devil himself! I thought Xaltotun was behind this wound. Now I see. Gods and demons! It was Conan who stabbed me! Arideus!”

“Search every corner of the palace. He could be creeping through the dark halls right now like a starving tiger. Leave no niche unchecked, and be careful. You’re not hunting a civilized man, but a blood-crazed barbarian with the strength and savagery of a wild beast. Sweep the palace grounds and the city. Surround the walls. If he’s already escaped the city—as he might well have—take a troop of horsemen and chase him. Once he’s past the walls, it’ll be like hunting a wolf through the hills. But hurry, and you might still catch him.”

“This is a matter that calls for more than ordinary human skill,” said Orastes. “Perhaps we should ask Xaltotun’s counsel.”

“No!” shouted Tarascus fiercely. “Let the soldiers hunt Conan down and kill him. Xaltotun can’t blame us for killing a prisoner to stop his escape.”

“Well,” said Orastes, “I’m no Acheronian, but I know a few of the arts, and how to command certain spirits that have taken on physical form. Maybe I can help you with this.”

The Fountain of Thrallos stood in a cluster of oaks beside the road, a mile from the city walls. Its musical trickle reached Conan’s ears through the still starlit night. He drank deeply of its icy water, then hurried south toward a small, dense thicket he saw nearby. Circling it, he spotted a large white horse tied among the bushes. Letting out a deep, gusty sigh, he strode toward it—only to whirl around at the sound of a mocking laugh.

A figure in dull, glinting mail stepped out of the shadows into the starlight. This was no polished palace guard with plumes and shining armor. It was a tall man wearing a morion and gray chain mail—one of the Adventurers, a class of warriors unique to Nemedia: men who had never gained the wealth or rank of knighthood, or who had fallen from it; hard-bitten fighters who lived for war and adventure. They were their own breed, sometimes leading troops, but answerable only to the king himself. Conan knew he could not have been discovered by a more dangerous foe.

A quick glance around the shadows told him the man was alone. He drew a deep breath, his massive chest expanding, his muscles coiling tight as he dug his toes into the turf.

“I was riding for Belverus on Amalric’s business,” said the Adventurer, moving forward cautiously. The starlight gleamed along the length of the great two-handed sword he held bare in his hand. “A horse whinnied to mine from the thicket. I checked it out and thought it strange to find one tethered here. I waited—and look, I’ve caught a rare prize!”

The Adventurers lived by their swords.

“I know you,” muttered the Nemedian. “You’re Conan, king of Aquilonia. I thought I saw you die in the valley of the Valkia, but—”

Conan leapt like a dying tiger. Skilled fighter though the Adventurer was, he never expected the desperate speed that lay in the barbarian’s sinews. He was caught off guard, his heavy sword half raised. Before he could strike or block, the king’s dagger drove into his throat above the gorget, angling down into his heart. With a choking gurgle he staggered and fell, and Conan savagely ripped his blade free as the man collapsed. The white horse snorted and shied at the sight and smell of blood on the weapon.

Standing over his dead foe, dagger dripping, sweat shining on his broad chest, Conan froze like a statue, listening. The woods around him were silent except for the sleepy chirp of startled birds. But from the city, a mile away, came the harsh blare of a trumpet.

He bent quickly over the fallen man. A brief search showed that whatever message the man carried was meant to be delivered by word of mouth. But Conan didn’t waste time. Dawn was only a few hours away. Minutes later, the white horse was galloping west along the pale road, its rider clad in the gray mail of a Nemedian Adventurer.

7 - THE RENDING OF THE VEIL  
The Rending of the Veil

Conan knew his only hope of escape was speed. He didn’t even think about hiding near Belverus until the pursuit passed by; he was sure that Tarascus’s uncanny ally could track him down anywhere. Besides, hiding wasn’t in his nature—he preferred an open fight or an open chase. He knew he had a long head start. He would make them run a brutal race for the border.

Zenobia had chosen wisely when she picked the white horse. Its speed, toughness, and endurance were obvious. The girl knew weapons and horses—and, Conan thought with some satisfaction, she also knew men. He rode westward at a pace that devoured the miles.  

He traveled through a sleeping land, passing villages sheltered by groves and white-walled villas surrounded by wide fields and orchards that grew fewer as he went west. As the villages thinned out, the land became rougher, and the fortresses that loomed from the hills spoke of centuries of border wars. But no one rode down from those castles to challenge or stop him. The lords of those keeps were following Amalric’s banner; the flags that once flew over those towers now waved over the Aquilonian plains.  

When the last clustered village was behind him, Conan left the road, which was beginning to curve northwest toward the distant mountain passes. Staying on the road would mean passing border towers still manned by soldiers who would not let him go without question. He knew there would be no patrols riding the borderlands on either side, as there were in normal times, but those towers remained, and by dawn there would likely be groups of returning soldiers, some wounded and carried in ox-carts.  

This road from Belverus was the only one that crossed the border for fifty miles north to south. It followed a chain of passes through the hills, and on either side stretched a wide region of wild, sparsely inhabited mountains. He kept heading due west, planning to cross the border deep in the wilderness of the hills south of the passes. It was a shorter but harder route, and safer for a fugitive. One man on horseback could travel through country that an army would find impossible.  

But by dawn he still hadn’t reached the hills; they stretched before him like a long, low blue wall along the horizon. There were no farms or villages here, no white villas among clustered trees. The dawn wind stirred the tall, stiff grass, and there was nothing but long rolling waves of brown earth covered with dry grass, and far off, the stark walls of a fortress on a low hill. Too many Aquilonian raiders had crossed the mountains in recent years for this land to be as thickly settled as the regions farther east.  

Dawn spread like wildfire across the grasslands, and high above came a strange crying as a scattered wedge of wild geese flew swiftly south. In a grassy hollow Conan stopped and unsaddled his horse. Its sides were heaving, its coat slick with sweat. He had driven it mercilessly through the hours before dawn.  

While it grazed on the brittle grass and rolled on the ground, he lay on the crest of a low slope, staring east. Far to the north he could see the road he had left, stretching like a white ribbon over a distant rise. No black dots moved along that shining line. There was no sign from the distant castle that anyone there had noticed the lone traveler.  

An hour later the land was still bare. The only sign of life was a glint of steel on the far-off battlements and a raven circling in the sky, dipping and rising as if searching for something. Conan saddled up again and rode westward at a slower pace.  

As he reached the next ridge, a harsh screaming broke out above him. Looking up, he saw the raven flapping high overhead, cawing endlessly. As he rode on, it followed him, keeping its distance and filling the morning with its grating cries, ignoring his attempts to drive it off.  

This went on for hours, until Conan’s nerves were raw, and he felt he’d give half his kingdom just to wring that black neck.  

“Devils of hell!” he roared in helpless fury, shaking his mailed fist at the crazed bird. “Why do you torment me with your squawking? Get away, you black spawn of damnation, and go peck for grain in a farmer’s field!”  

He was climbing the first slope of the hills when he thought he heard an echo of the bird’s cries far behind him. Turning in his saddle, he soon spotted another black speck hanging in the blue sky. Beyond that, he caught the glint of the afternoon sun on steel. That could mean only one thing: armed men. And they weren’t riding along the main road, which lay out of sight beyond the horizon. They were following him.  

His face hardened, and he shivered slightly as he stared at the raven circling above.  

“So it’s more than the whim of a mindless beast?” he muttered. “Those riders can’t see you, spawn of hell—but the other bird can see you, and they can see him. You follow me, he follows you, and they follow him. Are you just a cleverly trained bird, or some demon in feathered form? Did Xaltotun send you after me? Are you Xaltotun yourself?”  

Only a harsh screech answered him, vibrating with mocking laughter.  

Conan wasted no more words on his dark betrayer. Grimly, he settled into the long grind up the hills. He dared not push the horse too hard; the rest he’d given it hadn’t been enough to restore its strength. He was still far ahead of his pursuers, but they would steadily close the gap. Their horses were almost certainly fresher than his, for they had probably changed mounts at that castle he had passed.  

The terrain grew rougher, the landscape more rugged, with steep grassy slopes rising into thickly forested mountainsides. Here, he knew, he might lose his hunters—if not for that cursed bird shrieking endlessly above him. He could no longer see his pursuers in this broken country, but he was sure they still followed, guided unerringly by their feathered ally. That black shape became like a demonic tormentor, hounding him through endless hells. The stones he hurled with a curse missed or fell harmlessly, though in his youth he had brought down hawks in flight.  

The horse was tiring fast. Conan recognized the grim finality of his situation. He felt an unstoppable fate driving him. He could not escape. He was as much a prisoner as he had been in the dungeons of Belverus. But he was no man of the East to submit passively to what seemed inevitable. If he could not escape, he would at least take some of his enemies with him into eternity. He turned into a wide thicket of larches that covered a slope, searching for a place to make his stand.

Then, ahead of him, came a strange, high-pitched scream—human, yet oddly toned. A moment later, he pushed through a screen of branches and saw the source of that eerie cry. In a small clearing below him, four soldiers in Nemedian chain mail were tying a noose around the neck of a gaunt old woman dressed like a peasant. A bundle of firewood bound with rope on the ground nearby showed what she had been doing when these stragglers caught her.

Conan felt a slow, burning rage rise in his chest as he silently watched the thugs drag her toward a tree whose low, spreading branches were clearly meant to serve as a gallows. He had crossed the border an hour ago. He was standing on his own land, watching the murder of one of his own people. The old woman struggled with surprising strength and energy, and as he watched, she lifted her head and gave again that strange, haunting, far-carrying cry he had heard before. It was echoed mockingly by a raven flapping above the trees. The soldiers laughed roughly, and one struck her in the mouth.

Conan swung down from his weary horse and dropped down the rocky slope, landing with a clang of armor on the grass. The four men turned at the sound and drew their swords, staring at the armored giant who faced them, sword in hand.

Conan laughed harshly. His eyes were cold as flint.

“Dogs!” he said, without emotion or mercy. “Do Nemedian jackals think themselves executioners, hanging my people as they please? First, you’ll have to take the head of their king. Here I stand—come and try!”

The soldiers stared at him uncertainly as he strode toward them.

“Who’s this madman?” growled a bearded brute. “He wears Nemedian mail, but he speaks with an Aquilonian accent.”

“No matter,” said another. “Cut him down, then we’ll hang the old hag.”

With that, he charged at Conan, sword raised. But before he could strike, the king’s great blade came down, splitting helmet and skull. The man fell dead before him, but the others were tough rogues. They howled like wolves and rushed the lone figure in gray armor, and the clash of steel drowned out the raven’s cries.

Conan didn’t shout. His eyes burned like blue fire, his lips curved in a grim smile, and he struck right and left with his two-handed sword. For all his size, he moved like a cat, constantly shifting, a moving target so that most thrusts and swings cut only air. Yet when he struck, he was perfectly balanced, and his blows landed with crushing power. Three of the four were down, dying in their own blood, and the fourth, bleeding from half a dozen wounds, stumbled backward in panic, parrying wildly—when Conan’s spur caught in the tunic of one of the fallen men.

The king stumbled, and before he could recover, the Nemedian, desperate and furious, rushed him so savagely that Conan staggered and fell sprawling over the corpse. The Nemedian croaked in triumph and sprang forward, lifting his great sword with both hands over his right shoulder, bracing his legs wide for the killing stroke—and then, over the fallen king, something huge and hairy shot forward like lightning, slamming into the soldier’s chest. His cry of triumph turned into a scream of death.

Scrambling up, Conan saw the man lying dead with his throat torn out, and a great gray wolf stood over him, head lowered as it sniffed the blood pooling on the grass.

The king turned as the old woman spoke to him. She stood tall and straight before him, and despite her ragged clothes, her sharp, aquiline features and piercing black eyes were not those of a common peasant. She called to the wolf, and it trotted to her side like a great dog, rubbing its massive shoulder against her knee while it gazed at Conan with glowing green eyes. Absentmindedly, she laid her hand on its thick neck, and the two stood there, silently watching the king of Aquilonia. He found their steady gaze unsettling, though it held no hostility.

“People say King Conan died under the stones and dirt when the cliffs collapsed near Valkia,” she said in a deep, strong, resonant voice.

“So they say,” he growled. He was in no mood for argument, and he thought of the armored riders who were surely closing in. The raven above cawed harshly, and he glanced up involuntarily, grinding his teeth in irritation.

Up on the ledge, his white horse stood with its head drooping. The old woman looked at it, then at the raven, and then she lifted that same strange, eerie cry as before. As if recognizing the call, the raven wheeled, suddenly silent, and flew eastward. But before it vanished, the shadow of great wings fell across it. An eagle soared up from the trees, rose above it, and struck the black messenger down to the ground. The harsh voice of betrayal was silenced forever.

“Crom!” muttered Conan, staring at the old woman. “Are you a sorceress too?”

“I am Zelata,” she said. “The people of the valleys call me a witch. Was that creature of the night guiding armed men on your trail?”

“Yes.” She didn’t seem to find the answer strange. “They can’t be far behind me.”

“Lead your horse and follow me, King Conan,” she said curtly.

Without a word, he climbed back up the rocks and brought his horse down to the clearing by a winding path. As he came, he saw the eagle reappear, gliding lazily down from the sky to rest for a moment on Zelata’s shoulder, spreading its great wings lightly so as not to crush her with its weight.

Without speaking, she led the way, the great wolf trotting beside her, the eagle soaring above. Through deep thickets and along twisting ledges hanging over dark ravines she guided him, and finally along a narrow, cliff-edged path to a strange stone dwelling—half hut, half cave—beneath a cliff hidden among the gorges and crags. The eagle flew to the top of the cliff and perched there like a motionless sentinel.

Still silent, Zelata stabled the horse in a nearby cave, where leaves and grass were piled high for feed, and a tiny spring bubbled in the dim recesses.

In the hut, she seated the king on a rough bench covered with hides, and she herself sat on a low stool before the small fireplace, where she built a fire from tamarisk wood and prepared a simple meal. The great wolf lay drowsing beside her, facing the flames, his massive head resting on his paws, his ears twitching as he dreamed.

“You’re not afraid to sit in a witch’s hut?” she asked at last, breaking the silence.

Her guest only shrugged impatiently, his gray armor glinting in the firelight. She handed him a wooden dish piled with dried fruit, cheese, and barley bread, along with a large pot of strong upland beer, brewed from barley grown in the high valleys.

“I’ve found the quiet of the glens more pleasing than the noise of city streets,” she said. “The children of the wild are kinder than the children of men.” Her hand brushed the ruff of the sleeping wolf. “My children were far from me today, or I wouldn’t have needed your sword, my king. They were coming when I called.”

“What did those Nemedian dogs have against you?” Conan demanded.

“Stragglers from the invading army wander all over the countryside, from the frontier to Tarantia,” she replied. “The foolish villagers in the valleys told them I had gold hidden away, hoping to draw their attention from their own homes. They demanded treasure from me, and my answers angered them. But neither those stragglers nor the men who hunt you, nor any raven, will find you here.”

He shook his head as he ate ravenously. “I’m heading for Tarantia.”

She shook her head. “You’re walking straight into the dragon’s jaws. You’d do better to seek safety abroad. The heart has gone out of your kingdom.”

“What do you mean?” he demanded. “Battles have been lost before, yet wars still won. A kingdom isn’t lost by one defeat.”

“And you’ll go to Tarantia?”

“Yes. Prospero will be holding it against Amalric.”

“Are you sure?”

“By hell, woman!” he burst out angrily. “What else?”

She shook her head. “I feel it’s otherwise. Let’s see. The veil is not easily torn—but I’ll tear it a little, and show you your capital city.”

Conan didn’t see what she threw into the fire, but the wolf whimpered in his sleep, and a green smoke rose and spread through the hut. As he watched, the walls and ceiling seemed to stretch and fade away, merging with endless space. The smoke rolled around him, blotting out everything. Shapes moved within it, fading and returning, until they stood out with startling clarity.

He saw the familiar towers and streets of Tarantia, where a mob raged and screamed. At the same time, he somehow saw the banners of Nemedia advancing steadily westward through the smoke and fire of a ravaged land. In the great square of Tarantia, the frantic crowd surged and shouted, crying that the king was dead, that the barons were preparing to divide the land among themselves, and that even the rule of Valerius was better than chaos. Prospero, shining in his armor, rode among them, trying to calm them, urging them to trust Count Trocero, to man the walls, and to help his knights defend the city. But they turned on him, screaming in fear and blind rage, calling him Trocero’s butcher, a worse enemy than Amalric himself. They hurled garbage and stones at his knights.

The image blurred slightly, as if time had passed, and then Conan saw Prospero and his knights riding out through the gates, spurring their horses southward. Behind them, the city was in turmoil.

“Fools!” Conan muttered thickly. “Fools! Why couldn’t they trust Prospero? Zelata, if you’re mocking me with some trick—”

“This has already happened,” Zelata said calmly, though her tone was grim. “It was the evening of the day just past when Prospero rode out of Tarantia, with Amalric’s army almost in sight. From the walls, men saw the flames of their plundering. So I read it in the smoke. At sunset, the Nemedians rode into Tarantia unopposed. Look! Even now, in the royal hall of Tarantia—”

Suddenly Conan was looking into the great coronation hall. Valerius stood on the royal dais, dressed in ermine robes, while Amalric, still in his dusty, bloodstained armor, placed a gleaming circlet on his yellow hair—the crown of Aquilonia! The people cheered; long lines of steel-clad Nemedian soldiers looked on grimly, and nobles once disgraced at Conan’s court strutted proudly, wearing Valerius’s emblem on their sleeves.

“Crom!” Conan’s curse burst from his lips as he leapt to his feet, his huge fists clenched, the veins on his temples bulging, his face twisted with fury. “A Nemedian placing the crown of Aquilonia on that traitor—in the royal hall of Tarantia!”

As if driven away by his rage, the smoke faded, and he saw Zelata’s dark eyes gleaming at him through the thinning mist.

“You have seen it—the people of your capital have thrown away the freedom you won for them with your sweat and blood. They’ve sold themselves to slavers and butchers. They’ve shown they don’t trust their own destiny. Can you depend on them to help you win back your kingdom?”

“They thought I was dead,” he grunted, regaining some composure. “I have no son. Men can’t be ruled by a memory. So what if the Nemedians have taken Tarantia? The provinces, the barons, and the country folk still remain. Valerius has won nothing but hollow glory.”

“You are stubborn, as a warrior should be. I can’t show you the future, nor all the past. No—I show you nothing. I only open windows in the veil, through powers beyond understanding. Do you want to look into the past for a clue to the present?”

“Yes.” He sat down abruptly.

Again the green smoke rose and swirled. Again images formed before him—strange, alien, and seemingly unrelated. He saw towering black walls, pedestals half lost in shadow, supporting images of monstrous, half-beast gods. Men moved in the gloom—dark, wiry men wearing red silk loincloths. They carried a green jade sarcophagus down a vast black corridor. But before he could take in more, the scene shifted. He saw a cavern, dim and shadowy, filled with an eerie, intangible dread. On an altar of black stone stood a curious golden vessel shaped like a scallop shell. Into this cavern came some of the same dark, wiry men who had carried the mummy case. They seized the golden vessel, and then the shadows swirled around them, and he could not tell what happened. But he saw a glimmer in the whirling darkness, like a ball of living fire. Then the smoke was only smoke again, rising from the tamarisk fire, thinning and fading away.

“But what does this mean?” he demanded, confused. “What I saw in Tarantia I can understand. But what is the meaning of that vision of Zamorian thieves sneaking through an underground temple of Set in Stygia? And that cavern—I’ve never seen or heard of anything like it in all my travels. If you can show me that much, those scraps of vision that make no sense, disconnected and incomplete, why can’t you show me everything that’s going to happen?”

Zelata stirred the fire without answering.

“These things are ruled by unchangeable laws,” she said at last. “I can’t make you understand; I don’t fully understand myself, though I’ve sought wisdom in the silence of the high places for more years than I can remember. I can’t save you, though I would if I could. In the end, every man must find his own salvation. Still, maybe wisdom will come to me in dreams, and in the morning I might be able to give you the key to the mystery.”

“What mystery?” he demanded.

“The mystery that stands before you—the reason you’ve lost your kingdom,” she answered. Then she spread a sheepskin on the floor in front of the hearth. “Sleep,” she said simply.

Without a word, he stretched out on it and sank into a restless but deep sleep, through which silent phantoms drifted and monstrous, shapeless shadows crept. Once, outlined against a purple, sunless horizon, he saw the mighty walls and towers of a great city, unlike any that existed on the waking earth he knew. Its colossal pylons and purple minarets reached toward the stars, and above it, floating like a vast mirage, hovered the bearded face of the man Xaltotun.

Conan woke in the cold whiteness of early dawn to see Zelata crouched beside the small fire. He hadn’t woken once during the night, and the sound of the great wolf leaving or returning should have roused him. Yet the wolf was there, beside the hearth, its shaggy coat wet with dew—and with more than dew. Blood glistened darkly in its thick fur, and there was a cut on its shoulder.

Zelata nodded without turning, as if she could read the thoughts of her royal guest.

“He hunted before dawn, and the hunt was red. I think the man who hunted a king will hunt no more—neither man nor beast.”

Conan stared at the great beast with strange fascination as he moved to take the food Zelata offered him.

“When I come back to my throne, I won’t forget,” he said shortly. “You’ve been a friend to me—by Crom, I can’t remember the last time I lay down and slept at the mercy of man or woman as I did last night. But what about the riddle you said you’d explain this morning?”

A long silence followed, broken only by the crackling of the tamarisks on the hearth.

“Find the heart of your kingdom,” she said at last. “There lies both your defeat and your strength. You’re fighting more than mortal man. You’ll never reclaim your throne unless you find the heart of your kingdom.”

“Do you mean the city of Tarantia?”

She shook her head. “I am only an oracle, through whose lips the gods speak. They seal my lips so I won’t say too much. You must find the heart of your kingdom. I can say no more. The gods open and close my lips as they will.”

Dawn was still pale on the peaks when Conan rode westward. A glance back showed Zelata standing in the doorway of her hut, as unreadable as ever, the great wolf beside her.

A gray sky stretched overhead, and a moaning wind carried the chill promise of winter. Brown leaves drifted slowly down from bare branches, settling on his armored shoulders.

All day he pushed through the hills, avoiding roads and villages. Toward nightfall he began descending from the heights, level by level, until he saw the wide plains of Aquilonia spread out below him.

Villages and farms lay close to the foot of the hills on the western side of the mountains, for over half a century most of the raiding across the frontier had been done by the Aquilonians. But now only embers and ashes marked where farmhouses and villas had once stood.

In the deepening twilight Conan rode slowly on. He had little fear of being discovered, whether by friend or foe. The Nemedians had settled old grudges during their westward advance, and Valerius had made no effort to restrain his allies. He didn’t care about winning the love of the common people. A vast path of destruction had been carved through the land from the foothills westward. Conan cursed as he rode over blackened stretches that had once been rich fields, and saw the gaunt, burned gables of ruined houses jutting against the sky. He moved through an empty, abandoned land, like a ghost from a forgotten age.

The speed with which the army had crossed the land showed how little resistance it had met. Yet if Conan had been leading his Aquilonians, the invaders would have had to pay for every foot of ground with their blood. The bitter truth sank deep into his heart: he wasn’t the heir of a dynasty. He was only a lone adventurer. Even the drop of royal blood Valerius claimed gave him more hold over men’s minds than the memory of Conan and the freedom and strength he had brought to the kingdom.

No pursuers followed him down from the hills. He kept watch for wandering or returning Nemedian troops but saw none. Stragglers gave him a wide berth, thinking him one of the conquerors because of his armor. Groves and rivers were far more plentiful on the western side of the mountains, and there was no shortage of places to hide.

So he moved across the ravaged land, stopping only to rest his horse, eating sparingly of the food Zelata had given him, until one dawn, as he lay hidden on a riverbank thick with willows and oaks, he caught sight, far away across the rolling plains dotted with rich groves, of the blue and golden towers of Tarantia.

He was no longer in a desolate land, but one alive with people and movement. From then on, his progress was slow and cautious, through dense woods and little-used paths. It was dusk when he reached the estate of Servius Galannus.

The countryside around Tarantia had escaped the worst of the devastation that had ravaged the eastern provinces. There were signs of a conquering army’s passage—broken hedges, plundered fields, looted granaries—but fire and sword had not been unleashed on a large scale.

There was only one grim stain on the landscape—a burned stretch of ashes and blackened stone where, Conan knew, the grand villa of one of his most loyal supporters had once stood.  

The king didn’t dare approach the Galannus farm openly, since it lay only a few miles from the city. At twilight he rode through a wide forest until he spotted a gamekeeper’s lodge among the trees. Dismounting and tying his horse, he walked up to the thick arched door, planning to send the keeper to fetch Servius. He didn’t know what enemies might be hiding in the manor house. He had seen no soldiers, but they could easily be scattered throughout the countryside. As he drew near, however, he saw the door open and a compact man in silk hose and a richly embroidered doublet stride out and take a path that wound away through the woods.  

“Servius!”  

At the low call, the master of the estate spun around with a startled cry. His hand flew to the short hunting sword at his hip, and he recoiled from the tall, gray steel figure standing in the dusk before him.  

“Who are you?” he demanded. “What is your—Mitra!”  

His breath hissed inward and his ruddy face went pale. “Back! Why have you come back from the gray lands of death to haunt me? I was always your loyal man while you lived—”  

“As I still expect you to be,” said Conan. “Stop trembling, man; I’m flesh and blood.”  

Sweating with uncertainty, Servius stepped closer and stared into the face of the armored giant. Then, convinced that what he saw was real, he dropped to one knee and removed his plumed cap.  

“Your Majesty! This is a miracle beyond belief! The great bell in the citadel tolled your funeral days ago. People said you died at Valkia, crushed under a mountain of earth and broken stone.”  

“It was another man wearing my armor,” grunted Conan. “But we’ll talk later. If you have a piece of beef on your table—”  

“Forgive me, my lord!” cried Servius, jumping to his feet. “The dust of travel is gray on your armor, and I keep you standing here without rest or food! Mitra! I see clearly now that you’re alive, but I swear, when I turned and saw you standing there all gray and dim in the twilight, the marrow in my knees turned to water. It’s a dreadful thing to meet a man you thought dead in the woods at dusk.”  

“Tell the keeper to see to my horse—it’s tied behind that oak,” said Conan. Servius nodded and led the king up the path. The patrician, recovering from his supernatural fright, had become extremely nervous.  

“I’ll send a servant from the manor,” he said. “The keeper is in his lodge—but I dare not trust even my servants these days. It’s better that only I know you’re here.”  

As they neared the large house that glimmered faintly through the trees, he turned aside onto a little-used path that ran between tightly packed oaks whose interlocking branches formed a vault overhead, shutting out the dim light of the deepening dusk. Servius hurried through the darkness without speaking, his manner close to panic, and soon led Conan through a small side door into a narrow, dimly lit corridor. They moved quickly and silently through it, and Servius brought the king into a spacious chamber with a high, oak-beamed ceiling and richly paneled walls. Logs blazed in the wide fireplace, for the air had a frosty edge, and a great meat pie on a stone platter stood steaming on a broad mahogany table. Servius locked the heavy door and snuffed out the candles in the silver candlestick on the table, leaving the room lit only by the fire on the hearth.  

“Forgive me, your Majesty,” he said apologetically. “These are dangerous times; spies are everywhere. It’s best that no one can look through the windows and recognize you. This pie is fresh from the oven—I meant to eat it after speaking with my keeper. If your Majesty would be so kind—”  

“The light’s enough,” grunted Conan, sitting down without ceremony and drawing his dagger.  

He dug hungrily into the rich dish and washed it down with great gulps of wine from grapes grown in Servius’s own vineyards. He seemed completely unconcerned about danger, but Servius shifted uneasily on his seat by the fire, nervously fingering the heavy gold chain around his neck. He kept glancing at the diamond-paned windows glimmering faintly in the firelight and cocked his ear toward the door, as if half expecting to hear the soft tread of stealthy feet in the corridor outside.  

When he finished eating, Conan rose and sat on another bench before the fire.  

“I won’t endanger you long by staying here, Servius,” he said abruptly. “By dawn I’ll be far from your estate.”  

“My lord—” Servius lifted his hands in protest, but Conan waved him silent.  

“I know your loyalty and your courage. Both are beyond question. But if Valerius has taken my throne, it would mean death for you to shelter me if you were discovered.”  

“I’m not strong enough to defy him openly,” admitted Servius. “The fifty men-at-arms I could muster would be nothing but a handful of straw. You saw the ruins of Emilius Scavonus’s estate?”  

Conan nodded, frowning darkly.  

“He was the strongest noble in this province, as you know. He refused to swear allegiance to Valerius. The Nemedians burned him alive in the ruins of his own villa. After that, the rest of us saw how useless resistance would be—especially since the people of Tarantia refused to fight. We submitted, and Valerius spared our lives, though he imposed a tax that will ruin many. But what could we do? We thought you were dead. Many of the barons had been killed, others captured. The army was shattered and scattered. You have no heir to claim the crown. There was no one to lead us—”  

“Was there not Count Trocero of Poitain?” demanded Conan harshly.  

Servius spread his hands helplessly.  

“It’s true that his general, Prospero, was still in the field with a small army. Retreating before Amalric, he urged men to rally to his banner. But with your Majesty dead, men remembered old wars and civil feuds, and how Trocero and his Poitanians once rode through these provinces just as Amalric rides now, with fire and sword. The barons were jealous of Trocero. Some men—perhaps spies of Valerius—shouted that the Count of Poitain meant to seize the crown for himself. Old regional hatreds flared up again. If we’d had even one man of royal blood, we would have crowned him and followed him against Nemedia. But we had none.”

The barons who had loyally followed you would never follow one of their own, since each thought himself as good as the next, and each feared the ambitions of the others. You were the cord that held the bundle of sticks together. When that cord was cut, the sticks fell apart. If you had had a son, the barons would have rallied around him with loyalty. But there was no single point for their patriotism to focus on.  

The merchants and commoners, terrified of anarchy and the return of the feudal days when every baron ruled by his own law, cried out that any king was better than none—even Valerius, who at least had the blood of the old dynasty. No one stood against him when he rode up at the head of his armored hosts, the scarlet dragon of Nemedia flying above him, and struck his lance against the gates of Tarantia.  

No, the people threw open the gates and knelt in the dust before him. They had refused to help Prospero hold the city. They said they would rather be ruled by Valerius than by Trocero. They said—truthfully—that the barons would not rally to Trocero, but many would accept Valerius. They said that by surrendering to Valerius, they would avoid the destruction of civil war and the wrath of the Nemedians. Prospero rode south with his ten thousand knights, and the Nemedian horsemen entered the city a few hours later. They did not follow him. They stayed to see Valerius crowned in Tarantia.  

“Then the old witch’s smoke showed the truth,” muttered Conan, feeling a strange chill crawl down his spine. “Amalric crowned Valerius?”  

“Yes, in the coronation hall, with the blood of slaughter barely dried on his hands.”  

“And do the people prosper under his kind rule?” asked Conan with bitter sarcasm.  

“He lives like a foreign prince in the middle of a conquered land,” answered Servius bitterly. “His court is filled with Nemedians, his palace guards are of the same breed, and a large garrison of them occupies the citadel. Yes, the Hour of the Dragon has come at last.  

“Nemedians strut through the streets like lords. Women are violated and merchants robbed daily, and Valerius either cannot or will not stop them. No, he is only their puppet, their figurehead. Men of sense knew he would be, and now the people are beginning to see it too.  

“Amalric has ridden out with a strong army to subdue the outer provinces where some barons have defied him. But there is no unity among them. Their jealousy of one another is stronger than their fear of Amalric. He will crush them one by one. Many castles and cities, realizing this, have already surrendered. Those who resist suffer terribly. The Nemedians are feeding their long hatred. And their ranks are growing with Aquilonians whom fear, gold, or the need for survival are forcing into their armies. It’s a natural consequence.”  

Conan nodded grimly, staring at the red reflections of the firelight on the richly carved oak panels.  

“Aquilonia has a king instead of the anarchy they feared,” said Servius at last. “But Valerius does not protect his subjects from his allies. Hundreds who could not pay the ransom demanded of them have been sold to the Kothic slave traders.”  

Conan’s head snapped up, and a deadly light flared in his blue eyes. He cursed fiercely, his massive hands clenching into iron fists.  

“Yes, white men are selling white men and white women, just like in the old feudal days. In the palaces of Shem and Turan they will live out their lives as slaves. Valerius is king, but the unity the people hoped for, even if it came by the sword, is still incomplete.  

“Gunderland in the north and Poitain in the south remain unconquered, and there are still free provinces in the west, where the border barons have the support of the Bossonian archers. Yet these outer provinces are no real threat to Valerius. They must stay on the defensive and will be lucky if they can hold their independence. Here, Valerius and his foreign knights rule supreme.”  

“Then let him enjoy it while he can,” said Conan grimly. “His time is short. The people will rise when they learn I’m alive. We’ll take Tarantia back before Amalric can return with his army. Then we’ll sweep these dogs out of the kingdom.”  

Servius said nothing. The crackling of the fire was loud in the silence.  

“Well,” exclaimed Conan impatiently, “why do you sit there with your head down, staring at the hearth? Do you doubt what I’ve said?”  

Servius avoided the king’s gaze.  

“What any mortal man can do, you will do, Your Majesty,” he said. “I’ve ridden behind you in battle, and I know no mortal being can stand before your sword.”  

“What then?”  

Servius pulled his fur-lined tunic closer around him and shivered despite the fire.  

“Men say your fall was caused by sorcery,” he said after a pause.  

“So what?”  

“What mortal can fight against sorcery? Who is this veiled man who, as people say, meets with Valerius and his allies at midnight, appearing and disappearing so mysteriously? Men whisper that he’s a great magician who died thousands of years ago, but has returned from the gray lands of death to overthrow the king of Aquilonia and restore the dynasty to which Valerius belongs.”  

“What does it matter?” Conan burst out angrily. “I escaped from the demon-haunted pits of Belverus and from witchcraft in the mountains. If the people rise—”  

Servius shook his head.  

“Your strongest supporters in the eastern and central provinces are dead, fled, or imprisoned. Gunderland is far to the north, Poitain far to the south. The Bossonians have withdrawn to their western borders. It would take weeks to gather and unite these forces, and before that could happen, each levy would be attacked separately by Amalric and destroyed.”  

“But an uprising in the central provinces would turn the tide for us!” exclaimed Conan. “We could seize Tarantia and hold it against Amalric until the Gundermen and Poitanians could arrive.”  

Servius hesitated, and his voice dropped to a whisper.  

“Men say you died cursed. They say this veiled stranger cast a spell on you to kill you and break your army. The great bell has already tolled your funeral. People believe you’re dead. And the central provinces would not rise, even if they knew you lived. They wouldn’t dare. Sorcery defeated you at Valkia. Sorcery carried the news to Tarantia, for that very night men were shouting it in the streets.”

A Nemedian priest unleashed dark magic again in the streets of Tarantia to kill men who still stayed loyal to your memory. I saw it myself. Armed men dropped like flies and died in the streets in ways no one could explain. And the thin priest laughed and said, “I am only Altaro, just an acolyte of Orastes, who is himself only an acolyte of the one who wears the veil; the power isn’t mine—it only works through me.”

“Well,” said Conan harshly, “isn’t it better to die with honor than to live in disgrace? Is death worse than oppression, slavery, and total ruin?”

“When fear of sorcery takes hold, reason disappears,” replied Servius. “The fear in the central provinces is too strong for them to rise for you. The outer provinces would fight for you—but the same sorcery that destroyed your army at Valkia would destroy you again. The Nemedians control the broadest, richest, and most heavily populated parts of Aquilonia, and they can’t be defeated by the forces you still command. You’d be sacrificing your loyal followers for nothing. It pains me to say it, but it’s true: King Conan, you are a king without a kingdom.”

Conan stared into the fire without answering. A burning log collapsed among the flames with a crash and a burst of sparks. It might have been the sound of his kingdom falling.

Once again Conan felt the presence of a grim truth behind the illusion of the material world. He sensed the relentless push of a merciless fate. A wave of furious panic pulled at his soul—a feeling of being trapped—and a red rage that burned to destroy and kill.

“Where are the officials of my court?” he demanded at last.

“Pallantides was badly wounded at Valkia, ransomed by his family, and now lies in his castle in Attalus. He’ll be lucky if he ever rides again. Publius, the chancellor, fled the kingdom in disguise—no one knows where. The council has been dissolved. Some were imprisoned, others banished. Many of your loyal subjects have been executed. Tonight, for example, Countess Albiona dies under the executioner’s axe.”

Conan started and glared at Servius, anger smoldering in his blue eyes so fiercely that the patrician shrank back.

“Why?”

“Because she refused to become Valerius’s mistress. Her lands are confiscated, her followers sold into slavery, and at midnight, in the Iron Tower, her head will fall. Take my advice, my king—to me you will always be my king—and flee before you’re discovered. No one is safe these days. Spies and informers creep among us, betraying even the smallest act or word of discontent as treason and rebellion. If you reveal yourself to your people, it will only lead to your capture and death.

“My horses and every man I can trust are at your service. Before dawn we can be far from Tarantia, well on our way to the border. If I can’t help you win back your kingdom, I can at least follow you into exile.”

Conan shook his head. Servius looked at him uneasily as he sat staring into the fire, his chin resting on his mighty fist. The firelight glowed red on his steel mail and in his fierce eyes. They burned in the light like a wolf’s eyes. Servius felt again, stronger than ever, that there was something alien about the king. That great body under the mail was too hard and flexible for a civilized man; the raw fire of the primitive burned in those smoldering eyes. Now the barbaric side of the king showed more clearly, as if, in his extremity, the outer layers of civilization had been stripped away to reveal the primal core. Conan was reverting to his original nature. He didn’t act as a civilized man would in the same situation, nor did his thoughts follow the same paths. He was unpredictable. It was only a short step from the king of Aquilonia to the fur-clad warrior of the Cimmerian hills.

“I’ll ride to Poitain, if I can,” Conan said at last. “But I’ll ride alone. And I have one last duty to perform as king of Aquilonia.”

“What do you mean, Your Majesty?” asked Servius, shaken by a sense of dread.

“I’m going into Tarantia tonight for Albiona,” answered the king. “I’ve failed all my other loyal subjects, it seems—if they take her head, they can have mine too.”

“This is madness!” cried Servius, jumping up and clutching his throat as if he already felt a noose tightening around it.

“There are secrets about the Tower that few know,” said Conan. “Anyway, I’d be a dog to leave Albiona to die for her loyalty to me. I may be a king without a kingdom, but I’m not a man without honor.”

“It will destroy us all!” whispered Servius.

“It will destroy no one but me if I fail. You’ve risked enough. I ride alone tonight. All I want you to do is get me an eye patch, a walking staff, and clothes like a traveler would wear.”

“It is the King or His Ghost!”

Many people passed through the great arched gates of Tarantia between sunset and midnight—late travelers, merchants from far away with heavily loaded mules, free laborers from nearby farms and vineyards. Now that Valerius ruled the central provinces, there was no strict inspection of the steady stream of people entering through the wide gates. Discipline had slackened. The Nemedian soldiers on guard were half drunk and too busy watching for pretty peasant girls or rich merchants they could bully to notice workmen or dusty travelers—even one tall wanderer whose worn cloak couldn’t hide the hard lines of his powerful body.

This man carried himself with a straight, confident bearing that came so naturally he didn’t even realize it, much less try to hide it. A large patch covered one eye, and his leather hood, pulled low over his brow, cast his face in shadow. With a long, thick staff in his strong brown hand, he walked calmly through the arch where the torches flickered and smoked, and, ignored by the drunken guards, stepped onto the wide streets of Tarantia.

On those well-lit streets, the usual crowds went about their business, and shops and stalls stood open with their goods on display. One constant thread ran through the scene: Nemedian soldiers, alone or in groups, swaggered through the crowds, pushing their way with deliberate arrogance. Women hurried out of their path, and men stepped aside with dark looks and clenched fists. The Aquilonians were a proud people, and these were their ancestral enemies.

The tall traveler’s knuckles tightened around his staff, but like the others, he stepped aside to let the armored men pass. In the mixed and colorful crowd, his plain, dusty clothes didn’t draw much notice. But once, as he passed a sword-seller’s stall and the light from its wide doorway fell directly on him, he thought he felt someone staring at him intensely. Turning quickly, he saw a man in the brown jerkin of a free workman watching him closely. The man turned away too quickly and disappeared into the moving crowd. Conan turned into a narrow side street and quickened his pace. It might have been nothing more than idle curiosity, but he couldn’t afford to take chances.

The grim Iron Tower stood apart from the citadel, surrounded by a maze of narrow streets and crowded houses. The poorer buildings, taking over space that the more refined avoided, had crept into a part of the city that was usually beyond their reach. The Tower was really a fortress—an ancient, massive structure of heavy stone and black iron that had once served as the citadel in a rougher, earlier age.

Not far from it, hidden among a tangle of half-deserted tenements and warehouses, stood an old watchtower so ancient and forgotten that it hadn’t appeared on city maps for a hundred years. Its original purpose had long been lost to memory, and those who noticed it at all never realized that the seemingly ancient lock keeping beggars and thieves from using it as a shelter was actually fairly new and extremely strong, cleverly disguised to look like rusty old metal. Fewer than six men in the entire kingdom had ever known the secret of that tower.

No keyhole showed in the massive, green-crusted lock. But Conan’s skilled fingers moved over it, pressing hidden knobs invisible to the casual eye. The door opened silently inward, and he stepped into total darkness, closing it behind him. A light would have revealed the tower to be empty—a bare, cylindrical shaft of solid stone.

Feeling his way with practiced familiarity, he found the small ridges he was searching for on a stone slab in the floor. Quickly he lifted it and, without hesitation, lowered himself into the opening beneath. His feet found stone steps leading downward into a narrow tunnel that he knew ran straight toward the foundations of the Iron Tower, three streets away.

The bell on the citadel, which rang only at midnight or for the death of a king, suddenly boomed. In a dimly lit chamber of the Iron Tower, a door opened and a figure stepped into a corridor. The inside of the Tower was as grim as its outside. The thick stone walls were rough and bare. The flagstones of the floor were deeply worn by generations of hesitant feet, and the vaulted ceiling loomed darkly in the dim torchlight.

The man walking down that gloomy corridor matched his surroundings. He was tall and powerfully built, dressed in tight-fitting black silk. A black hood covered his head and shoulders, with two holes cut for his eyes. A loose black cloak hung from his shoulders, and over one shoulder he carried a heavy ax—neither quite a tool nor a weapon.

As he walked down the corridor, a bent, grumpy old man came hobbling toward him, stooped under the weight of his pike and the lantern he carried in one hand.

“You’re not as prompt as the last headsman,” the old man grumbled. “Midnight’s just struck, and the masked men have gone to milady’s cell. They’re waiting for you.”

“The bell’s echoes are still fading among the towers,” the executioner replied. “If I’m not as quick to jump at the call of these Aquilonians as the dog who held this post before me, they’ll still find my arm just as ready. Go do your duty, old watchman, and let me do mine. I think mine’s the better trade, by Mitra—you wander cold halls and stare at rusty dungeon doors, while I take off the fairest head in Tarantia tonight.”

The watchman limped away down the corridor, still muttering, and the headsman continued on at his own pace. A few strides brought him around a bend, where he noticed a door on his left standing partly open. If he had thought about it, he would have realized that the door must have been opened after the watchman passed—but thinking wasn’t part of his job. He was already walking past the door before he sensed something was wrong, and by then it was too late.

A soft, catlike step and the rustle of a cloak warned him, but before he could turn, a strong arm hooked around his throat from behind, crushing his cry before it could escape. In the brief instant he had, he realized with a surge of panic the incredible strength of his attacker—his own powerful muscles were useless against it. He sensed, without seeing, the raised dagger.

“Nemedian dog!” a voice growled in his ear, thick with fury. “You’ve taken your last Aquilonian head!”

And that was the last thing he ever heard.

* * * * * * *

In a damp dungeon lit only by a flickering torch, three men stood around a young woman who knelt on the rush-covered floor, staring up at them in terror. She wore only a thin shift; her golden hair fell in shining waves over her white shoulders, and her wrists were tied behind her back. Even in the uncertain light, despite her disheveled state and the pallor of fear, her beauty was striking. She knelt silently, wide-eyed, staring up at her tormentors. The men were masked and cloaked. Even in a conquered land, such a deed required masks. She knew them all, but what she knew would harm no one—after that night.

“Our merciful sovereign offers you one last chance, Countess,” said the tallest of the three, speaking Aquilonian without an accent. “He bids me tell you that if you soften your proud, rebellious spirit, he will still welcome you into his arms. If not—” he gestured toward a grim wooden block in the center of the cell. It was darkly stained and deeply notched, as though a sharp edge, cutting through something soft, had bitten into the wood many times.

Albiona shuddered and turned pale, shrinking back. Every fiber of her strong young body trembled with the will to live. Valerius was young too, and handsome. Many women loved him, she told herself, fighting desperately for her life. But she couldn’t speak the word that would save her soft young body from the block and the dripping ax. She couldn’t reason it out—she only knew that when she imagined Valerius’s arms around her, her skin crawled with a revulsion stronger than her fear of death. She shook her head helplessly, driven by a force more powerful than the instinct to survive.

“Then there’s nothing more to say!” snapped one of the others impatiently, his voice carrying a Nemedian accent. “Where’s the executioner?”

As if summoned by the word, the dungeon door opened silently, and a massive figure appeared in the doorway, like a black shadow risen from the underworld.

Albiona let out a low, involuntary cry at the sight of that grim shape, and the others stared in silence for a moment, perhaps themselves struck with superstitious fear at the sight of the silent, hooded figure. Through the coif, the eyes burned like blue fire, and as those eyes rested on each man in turn, each felt a strange chill crawl down his spine.

Then the tall Aquilonian roughly grabbed the girl and dragged her to the block. She screamed uncontrollably and struggled in vain, wild with terror, but he forced her to her knees and bent her golden head down to the bloodstained block.

“Why do you hesitate, headsman?” he shouted angrily. “Do your duty!”

He was answered by a short, harsh burst of laughter that was indescribably menacing. Everyone in the dungeon froze where they stood, staring at the hooded figure—the two cloaked men, the masked man bending over the girl, and the girl herself, kneeling and twisting her head to look up.

“What’s the meaning of this laughter, you cur?” demanded the Aquilonian uneasily.

The man in black tore the hood from his head and threw it to the ground; he set his back against the closed door and lifted the executioner’s ax.

“Do you know me, dogs?” he growled. “Do you know me?”

The breathless silence broke with a scream.

“The king!” shrieked Albiona, wrenching herself free from her captor’s loosened grip. “Oh, Mitra, the king!”

The three men stood frozen like statues, and then the Aquilonian started and spoke, as if doubting his own senses.

“Conan!” he gasped. “It’s the king—or his ghost! What devil’s trick is this?”

“Devil’s work to match devils!” mocked Conan, his lips smiling but his eyes blazing with fury. “Come on then, gentlemen. You have your swords, and I have this cleaver. Aye, I think this butcher’s tool suits the work at hand, my fine lords!”

“Get him!” muttered the Aquilonian, drawing his sword. “It’s Conan—we must kill or be killed!”

And like men waking from a trance, the Nemedians drew their blades and rushed at the king.

The executioner’s ax wasn’t made for such fighting, but the king swung the heavy, awkward weapon as easily as a hatchet, and his quick footwork, constantly shifting position, kept them from surrounding him all at once.

He caught the first man’s sword on the ax-head and crushed in his chest with a murderous counterstroke before the man could step back or parry. The next Nemedian, missing a wild swing, had his skull smashed before he could regain his balance, and an instant later the Aquilonian was backed into a corner, desperately parrying the crashing blows raining down on him, unable even to cry for help.

Suddenly Conan’s long left arm shot out and ripped the mask from the man’s head, revealing his pale face.

“Dog!” snarled the king. “I thought I knew you. Traitor! Damned turncoat! Even this filthy steel is too noble for your foul head. No—you’ll die as thieves die!”

The ax fell in a terrible arc, and the Aquilonian screamed and dropped to his knees, clutching the severed stump of his right arm, from which blood spurted. It had been cut clean off at the elbow, and the ax, unchecked in its swing, had slashed deep into his side so that his entrails bulged out.

“Lie there and bleed out,” grunted Conan, tossing the ax aside in disgust. “Come on, Countess!”

Bending down, he slashed the cords binding her wrists and, lifting her as easily as a child, strode out of the dungeon. She was sobbing hysterically, her arms thrown around his thick neck in a desperate embrace.

“Easy now,” he muttered. “We’re not out of this yet. If we can reach the dungeon with the secret door that opens onto the stairs leading to the tunnel—damn it, they’ve heard that racket, even through these walls.”

Down the corridor came the clatter of weapons and the tramp and shouting of men echoing under the vaulted ceiling. A bent figure came hobbling quickly toward them, lantern held high, and its light fell full on Conan and the girl. With a curse, the Cimmerian leapt toward him, but the old watchman, dropping both lantern and pike, scurried away down the corridor, shrieking for help at the top of his cracked voice. Deeper shouts answered him.

Conan turned swiftly and ran the other way. He was cut off from the dungeon with the secret lock and hidden door through which he had entered the Tower—and by which he had hoped to leave—but he knew this grim building well. Before he was king, he had been imprisoned here.

He turned into a side passage and quickly emerged into another, broader corridor that ran parallel to the one he had come down, and which was, for the moment, empty. He followed it only a few yards before turning again into another side passage. This brought him back into the corridor he had left, but at a strategic point. A few feet farther up stood a heavy, bolted door, and before it was a bearded Nemedian in corselet and helmet, his back to Conan as he peered up the corridor toward the growing noise and flickering lanterns.

Conan didn’t hesitate. Setting the girl down, he ran at the guard swiftly and silently, sword in hand. The man turned just as the king reached him, shouted in surprise and fear, and lifted his pike—but before he could bring the clumsy weapon to bear, Conan brought his sword down on the man’s helmet with a force that would have felled an ox. Helmet and skull split together, and the guard crumpled to the floor.

In an instant, Conan had drawn the massive bolt that barred the door—too heavy for an ordinary man to move—and called quickly to Albiona, who stumbled toward him. Grabbing her unceremoniously with one arm, he carried her through the door and into the outer darkness.

They had entered a narrow alley, black as pitch, with the Tower’s wall on one side and the sheer stone backs of a row of buildings on the other. Conan, moving through the darkness as quickly as he dared, felt along the latter wall for doors or windows but found none.

Behind them, the great door clanged open, and men poured out, their torches flashing on breastplates and bare swords. They glared around, shouting, unable to pierce the darkness that their torches only lit for a few feet in any direction, then rushed down the alley at random—heading the opposite way from Conan and Albiona.

“They’ll realize their mistake soon enough,” he muttered, quickening his pace. “If we can just find a crack in this cursed wall—damn! The street watch!”

Ahead, a faint glow appeared where the alley opened into a narrow street, and he saw dim figures outlined against it, steel glinting faintly. It was indeed the street watch, drawn by the noise echoing down the alley.

“Who goes there?” they shouted, and Conan ground his teeth at the hated Nemedian accent.

“Stay behind me,” he told the girl. “We’ve got to cut our way through before the prison guards come back and trap us between them.”

Drawing his sword, he charged straight at the figures ahead. The advantage of surprise was his. He could see them, outlined against the faint light, while they couldn’t see him coming out of the black depths of the alley. He was among them before they realized it, striking with the silent fury of a wounded lion.

His only chance was to hack through before they could recover. But there were ten of them, armored veterans of the border wars, whose battle instincts replaced their momentary confusion. Three went down before they realized only one man was attacking, but their reaction was instant. The clash of steel rang deafeningly, sparks flying as Conan’s sword crashed against helmets and mail. He could see better than they could, and in the dim light his swift movements made him a hard target. Blades slashed empty air or glanced off his sword, and when he struck, it was with the force and precision of a storm.

But behind him came the shouts of the prison guards, running up the alley, and still the armored watchmen blocked his way with a wall of steel. In moments, the guards would be on his back—desperate, he redoubled his blows, striking like a blacksmith at his forge. Then suddenly he sensed a change. Out of nowhere behind the watchmen rose a score of dark figures, and the sound of deadly blows filled the air. Steel flashed in the gloom, and men cried out, cut down from behind. In an instant the alley was strewn with writhing bodies. A dark, cloaked figure leapt toward Conan, who raised his sword, catching a glint of steel in the man’s right hand—but the other hand was empty, extended toward him, and a voice hissed urgently, “This way, your Majesty! Quickly!”

With a muttered curse of surprise, Conan swept Albiona up in one massive arm and followed his unknown rescuer. He wasn’t about to hesitate, not with thirty prison guards closing in behind him.

Surrounded by the mysterious figures, he hurried down the alley, carrying the countess as if she were a child. He could tell nothing about his rescuers except that they wore dark cloaks and hoods. Doubt and suspicion flickered through his mind, but they had struck down his enemies, and he saw no better choice than to follow.

Sensing his uncertainty, the leader touched his arm lightly and said, “Do not fear, King Conan; we are your loyal subjects.” The voice was unfamiliar, but the accent was Aquilonian, from the central provinces.

Behind them, the guards shouted as they stumbled over the bodies in the mud, then came charging down the alley, seeing the vague dark mass moving between them and the light of the distant street. But the hooded men suddenly turned toward what looked like a blank wall, and Conan saw a door open there. He swore under his breath. He had walked that alley by day in the past and had never noticed a door there. But through it they went, and it closed behind them with the click of a lock. The sound wasn’t reassuring, but his guides hurried him on, moving with practiced ease, guiding Conan with a hand on each arm. It felt like walking through a tunnel, and Conan felt Albiona trembling in his arms. Then, ahead, a faint opening appeared—a slightly lighter arch in the darkness—and they passed through it.

After that came a confusing series of dim courtyards, shadowy alleys, and winding corridors, all in complete silence, until at last they emerged into a broad, lighted chamber. Conan couldn’t even guess where they were; their twisting route had disoriented even his keen sense of direction.

Not all of his guides entered the chamber. When the door closed, Conan saw only one man standing before him—a slim figure in a black cloak and hood. The man threw it back, revealing a pale, oval face with calm, finely carved features.

Conan set Albiona on her feet, but she still clung to him, staring around nervously. The chamber was large, its marble walls partly covered with black velvet hangings, and thick, rich carpets spread across the mosaic floor, glowing softly in the golden light of bronze lamps.

Conan instinctively rested a hand on his sword hilt. There was blood on his hand and dried blood around the mouth of his scabbard, for he had sheathed his blade without cleaning it.

“Where are we?” he demanded.

The stranger bowed deeply, and Conan, though suspicious, detected no hint of mockery.

“In the temple of Asura, your Majesty.”

Albiona gasped faintly and clung tighter to Conan, staring fearfully at the black arched doors as if expecting some monstrous shape to emerge from the shadows.

“Do not be afraid, my lady,” said their guide. “There is nothing here that can harm you, despite the common superstitions. If your king was wise enough to recognize the innocence of our faith and protect us from the ignorance of others, then surely one of his subjects has no reason to fear.”

“Who are you?” demanded Conan.

“I am Hadrathus, priest of Asura. One of my followers recognized you when you entered the city and brought word to me.”

Conan grunted a curse.

“Don’t worry that anyone else discovered who you are,” Hadrathus assured him. “Your disguise would have fooled anyone except a follower of Asura, whose faith teaches us to look beneath illusions. You were followed to the watchtower, and some of my people went into the tunnel to help you if you came back that way. Others, myself included, surrounded the tower. And now, King Conan, you command here. In the temple of Asura, you are still king.”

“Why would you risk your lives for me?” asked the king.

“You were our friend when you sat on your throne,” Hadrathus replied. “You protected us when the priests of Mitra tried to drive us out of the land.”

Conan looked around curiously. He had never before entered the temple of Asura and hadn’t even been sure one existed in Tarantia. The priests of that faith were known for hiding their temples in remarkable ways. The worship of Mitra dominated the Hyborian nations, but the cult of Asura survived despite official bans and public hatred. Conan had heard dark stories of secret temples where thick smoke rose endlessly from black altars, where kidnapped victims were sacrificed before a huge coiled serpent whose terrible head swayed forever in the haunted shadows.

Persecution had forced the followers of Asura to hide their temples with clever skill and to shroud their rituals in secrecy; that secrecy, in turn, inspired even darker suspicions and tales of evil.

But Conan had the broad tolerance of a barbarian, and he had refused to persecute the followers of Asura or allow others to do so on nothing more than rumors and unproven accusations. “If they’re black magicians,” he had said, “how would they let you harass them? If they’re not, then there’s no evil in them. Crom’s devils! Let men worship whatever gods they choose.”

At Hadrathus’s respectful invitation, he sat down on an ivory chair and motioned for Albiona to take another, but she preferred to sit on a golden stool at his feet, pressing close against his leg as if seeking safety in his presence. Like most devout followers of Mitra, she had an instinctive horror of the followers and faith of Asura, planted in her since childhood by wild tales of human sacrifice and monstrous gods wandering through shadowy temples.

Hadrathus stood before them, his bare head bowed.

“What is your wish, Your Majesty?”

“Food first,” Conan grunted, and the priest struck a golden gong with a silver rod.

The mellow notes had barely faded when four hooded figures entered through a curtained doorway carrying a large four-legged silver tray filled with steaming dishes and crystal vessels. They set it before Conan, bowed low, and the king wiped his hands on the fine cloth and smacked his lips with open satisfaction.

“Be careful, Your Majesty!” whispered Albiona. “These people eat human flesh!”

“I’ll bet my kingdom this is nothing but good roast beef,” Conan replied. “Come on, girl, eat! You must be starving after that prison food.”

Following his lead, and trusting the man whose word was law to her, the countess obeyed and ate hungrily though delicately, while her lord tore into the meat and drank the wine with as much pleasure as if he hadn’t already eaten once that night.

“You priests are clever, Hadrathus,” he said, holding a big beef bone in his hands and speaking with his mouth full. “I’d welcome your help in my campaign to win back my kingdom.”

Hadrathus slowly shook his head, and Conan slammed the bone down on the table in a burst of anger.

“Crom’s devils! What’s wrong with the men of Aquilonia? First Servius—now you! Can none of you do anything but shake your foolish heads when I talk about driving out those dogs?”

Hadrathus sighed and answered slowly, “My lord, it pains me to say this, and I wish I could say otherwise. But the freedom of Aquilonia is finished. No—perhaps the freedom of the whole world is finished! Age follows age in the history of the world, and now we enter an age of horror and slavery, as it was long ago.”

“What do you mean?” demanded the king uneasily.

Hadrathus sank into a chair, resting his elbows on his knees and staring at the floor.

“It’s not just the rebel lords of Aquilonia and the armies of Nemedia that stand against you,” Hadrathus said. “It’s sorcery—horrible black magic from the dark youth of the world. A dreadful being has risen out of the shadows of the past, and no one can stand before it.”

“What do you mean?” Conan repeated.

“I speak of Xaltotun of Acheron, who died three thousand years ago, yet walks the earth today.”

Conan was silent, while in his mind floated an image—the image of a bearded face of calm, inhuman beauty. Again he was haunted by a strange sense of familiarity. Acheron—the sound of the word stirred instinctive echoes of memory and association in his mind.

“Acheron,” he repeated. “Xaltotun of Acheron—man, are you insane? Acheron has been a myth for more centuries than I can count. I’ve often wondered if it ever existed at all.”

“It was a dark reality,” said Hadrathus, “an empire of black magicians steeped in evil long forgotten. It was finally destroyed by the Hyborian tribes of the west. The wizards of Acheron practiced vile necromancy, the darkest kind of sorcery, terrible magic taught to them by demons. And of all the sorcerers of that cursed kingdom, none was greater than Xaltotun of Python.”

“Then how was he ever defeated?” asked Conan skeptically.

“By some means, a source of cosmic power that he guarded jealously was stolen and turned against him. That source has now been returned to him, and he cannot be defeated.”

Albiona, clutching the executioner’s black cloak around her, stared from the priest to the king, not understanding their words. Conan shook his head angrily.

“You’re making fun of me,” he growled. “If Xaltotun has been dead for three thousand years, how can this man be him? It’s just some scoundrel who’s taken the old one’s name.”

Hadrathus leaned over an ivory table and opened a small golden chest that stood there. From it, he took something that gleamed dully in the soft light—a broad gold coin of ancient minting.

“You’ve seen Xaltotun with your own eyes? Then look at this. It’s a coin that was struck in ancient Acheron before its fall. That dark empire was so steeped in sorcery that even this coin still holds power for magical use.”

Conan took it and frowned down at it. There was no mistaking its great age. He had handled many coins over his years of plundering and had a practical knowledge of them. The edges were worn, and the inscription was almost gone. But the face stamped on one side was still sharp and distinct. Conan’s breath hissed between his teeth. The room wasn’t cold, yet he felt his scalp prickle and his skin tighten with a chill. The face was that of a bearded man, unreadable, with a calm, inhuman beauty.

“By Crom! It’s him!” Conan muttered. Now he understood the strange familiarity that had struck him when he first saw the bearded man. He had seen a coin like this once before, long ago in a distant land.

He shook his shoulders and growled, “The resemblance is just coincidence—or if he’s clever enough to take a forgotten wizard’s name, he’s clever enough to copy his face.” But he didn’t sound convinced. The sight of that coin had shaken the very foundation of his world. He felt as if reality itself were crumbling into an abyss of illusion and sorcery. A wizard he could understand—but this was madness beyond reason.

“We cannot doubt that it truly is Xaltotun of Python,” said Hadrathus. “It was he who brought down the cliffs at Valkia with his spells that command the elementals of the earth—it was he who sent the creature of darkness into your tent before dawn.”

Conan scowled. “How do you know that?”

“The followers of Asura have secret ways of learning things. That’s not important. What matters is—do you realize how useless it is to sacrifice your people in a hopeless attempt to win back your crown?”

Conan rested his chin on his fist and stared grimly into space. Albiona watched him anxiously, her mind struggling to grasp the tangled problem before him.

“Isn’t there any wizard in the world who could make magic strong enough to fight Xaltotun’s magic?” he asked finally.

Hadrathus shook his head. “If there were, we of Asura would know. People say our faith is a remnant of the old Stygian serpent-worship. That’s a lie. Our ancestors came from Vendhya, beyond the Vilayet Sea and the blue Himelian Mountains. We are sons of the East, not the South, and we know of all the wizards of the East, who are greater than those of the West. And not one of them could stand before the black power of Xaltotun.”

“But he was defeated once,” Conan insisted.

“Yes; a cosmic force was turned against him. But now that force is back in his hands, and he’ll make sure it’s never stolen again.”

“And what is this cursed force?” Conan demanded irritably.

“It’s called the Heart of Ahriman. When Acheron fell, the primitive priest who had stolen it and used it against Xaltotun hid it in a haunted cavern and built a small temple above it. Three times afterward the temple was rebuilt, each time larger and more elaborate, but always on the same spot, though men forgot why. The memory of the hidden relic faded from common knowledge and survived only in priestly records and secret writings. No one knows where it came from. Some say it’s the actual heart of a god; others say it’s a star that fell from the heavens long ago. Until it was stolen, no one had seen it for three thousand years.

“When the magic of the Mitran priests failed against the magic of Xaltotun’s disciple, Altaro, they remembered the old legend of the Heart. The high priest and an acolyte went down into the dark, dreadful crypt beneath the temple—a place no priest had entered for three thousand years. In the ancient iron-bound books that speak of the Heart in their strange symbols, it’s also written of a creature of darkness left by that ancient priest to guard it.

“Far below, in a square chamber with arched doorways leading into endless blackness, the priest and his acolyte found a black stone altar glowing faintly with an eerie light.

“On that altar lay a strange golden vessel shaped like a double-valved seashell, clinging to the stone like a barnacle. But it was open and empty. The Heart of Ahriman was gone. As they stared in horror, the guardian of the crypt—the creature of darkness—fell upon them and tore the high priest so badly that he died. But the acolyte fought off the being—a mindless, soulless thing from the pits, brought long ago to guard the Heart—and escaped up the long, narrow, black stairway carrying the dying priest. Before he died, the priest gasped out the news to his followers, told them to submit to a power they could not defeat, and ordered secrecy. But the rumor spread among the priests, and we of Asura learned of it.”

“And Xaltotun draws his power from this relic?” Conan asked, still doubtful.

“No. His power comes from the black abyss. But the Heart of Ahriman came from some far universe of blazing light, and against it the powers of darkness cannot stand when it’s in the hands of one who knows how to use it. It’s like a sword that could strike him down, not one he can wield himself. It gives life and can take life. He stole it not to use against his enemies, but to keep them from using it against him.”

“A shell-shaped golden bowl on a black altar in a deep cavern,” Conan muttered, frowning as he tried to grasp the elusive image. “That reminds me of something I’ve heard or seen. But what, in Crom’s name, is this mysterious Heart?”

“It’s shaped like a great jewel, like a ruby, but it pulses with a blinding fire no ruby ever held. It glows like living flame—”

But Conan suddenly leapt to his feet and slammed his right fist into his left palm with a sound like thunder.

“Crom!” he roared. “What a fool I’ve been! The Heart of Ahriman! The heart of my kingdom! ‘Find the heart of your kingdom,’ Zelata said. By Ymir, it was the jewel I saw in the green smoke—the jewel Tarascus stole from Xaltotun while he lay in the sleep of the black lotus!”

Hadrathus was on his feet too, his calm falling away like a discarded cloak.

“What are you saying? The Heart stolen from Xaltotun?”

“Aye!” Conan thundered. “Tarascus feared Xaltotun and wanted to weaken his power, which he believed was bound to the Heart. Maybe he thought the wizard would die if the Heart were lost. By Crom—ahhh!” With a savage grimace of frustration and disgust, he dropped his clenched hand to his side.

“I forgot. Tarascus gave it to a thief to throw into the sea. By now the man must be near Kordava. Before I can catch him, he’ll board a ship and send the Heart to the bottom of the ocean.”

“The sea will not hold it!” exclaimed Hadrathus, trembling with excitement. “Xaltotun would have thrown it into the ocean himself long ago if he hadn’t known the first storm would cast it ashore again. But on what unknown beach might it land!”

“Well,” Conan said, regaining some of his usual confidence, “there’s no guarantee the thief will throw it away. If I know thieves—and I should, since I was one in Zamora in my youth—he won’t toss it into the sea. He’ll sell it to some rich merchant. By Crom!” He paced back and forth, his excitement growing. “It’s worth searching for! Zelata told me to find the heart of my kingdom, and everything else she showed me proved true. Could it be that the power to defeat Xaltotun lies in that crimson gem?”

“Aye! I’d stake my life on it!” cried Hadrathus, his face alight with fervor, his eyes blazing, his fists clenched. “With it in our hands, we can challenge Xaltotun’s powers! I swear it! If we can recover it, we have a real chance to reclaim your crown and drive the invaders from our gates. It’s not Nemedia’s swords that Aquilonia fears, but Xaltotun’s black magic.”

Conan looked at him for a moment, impressed by the priest’s passion.

“It’s like a quest out of a nightmare,” he said at last. “Yet your words echo Zelata’s, and all else she said was true. I’ll search for this jewel.”

“It holds Aquilonia’s fate,” said Hadrathus with conviction. “I’ll send men with you—”

“No!” snapped the king impatiently, unwilling to be slowed down by priests, no matter how skilled in hidden arts. “This is a task for a warrior. I go alone. First to Poitain, where I’ll leave Albiona with Trocero. Then to Kordava, and to the sea beyond if I must. It may be that, even if the thief means to obey Tarascus’s order, he’ll have trouble finding a ship sailing out at this time of year.”

“And if you find the Heart,” cried Hadrathus, “I’ll prepare the way for your return. Before you come back to Aquilonia, I’ll spread word through secret channels that you live and are returning with a power greater than Xaltotun’s. I’ll have men ready to rise when you return. They’ll rise if they believe they’ll be protected from Xaltotun’s sorcery.

“And I’ll help you on your journey.”

He rose and struck a gong.

“A secret tunnel runs beneath this temple to a place outside the city walls. You’ll travel to Poitain on a pilgrim’s boat. No one will dare interfere with you.”

“As you wish.” With a clear goal burning in his mind, Conan was filled with impatience and fierce energy. “Only let it be done quickly.”

* * * * *

Meanwhile, events were moving swiftly elsewhere in the city. A breathless messenger burst into the palace where Valerius was amusing himself with his dancing girls. Throwing himself to one knee, he gasped out a confused story of a bloody prison break and the escape of a beautiful captive. He also brought word that Count Thespius, who had been charged with carrying out Albiona’s execution, was dying and begging to speak with Valerius before he passed.

Throwing on a cloak, Valerius followed the man through winding corridors until they reached a chamber where Thespius lay. There was no doubt the count was dying; bloody froth bubbled from his lips with each shuddering breath. His severed arm had been bound to stop the bleeding, but even without that, the wound in his side was fatal.

Alone in the room with the dying man, Valerius swore softly.

“By Mitra, I thought only one man alive could strike such a blow.”

“Valerius!” gasped the dying man. “He lives! Conan lives!”

“What are you saying?” exclaimed Valerius.

“I swear by Mitra!” gurgled Thespius, choking on the blood that filled his mouth. “It was he who carried off Albiona! He’s not dead—not some ghost come back from hell to haunt us. He’s flesh and blood, and more terrible than ever. The alley behind the tower is full of dead men. Beware, Valerius—he’s come back—to kill us all—”

A violent shudder ran through the blood-smeared body, and Count Thespius went limp.

Valerius frowned down at the corpse, glanced quickly around the empty chamber, then strode to the door and flung it open. The messenger and a group of Nemedian guards stood several paces down the corridor. Valerius muttered something that might have been satisfaction.

“Have all the gates been closed?” he demanded.

“Yes, your Majesty.”

“Triple the guards at each one. Let no one enter or leave the city without the strictest inspection. Send men to scour the streets and search every quarter. A very valuable prisoner has escaped, aided by an Aquilonian rebel. Did any of you recognize the man?”

“No, your Majesty. The old watchman caught a glimpse of him, but all he could say was that the man was a giant, dressed in the black clothes of an executioner—the same one whose naked body we found in an empty cell.”

“He’s a dangerous man,” said Valerius. “Don’t take any chances with him. You all know Countess Albiona. Search for her, and if you find her, kill her and her companion immediately. Don’t try to take them alive.”

When Valerius returned to his chamber in the palace, he summoned four men who looked strange and foreign. They were tall and thin, with yellowish skin and expressionless faces. They looked almost identical, dressed in long black robes that revealed only their sandaled feet. Their hoods cast shadows over their faces. They stood before Valerius with their hands hidden in their wide sleeves, arms folded. Valerius looked at them without pleasure. In his long travels, he had met many strange peoples.

“When I found you starving in the Khitan jungles,” he said abruptly, “exiles from your own land, you swore to serve me. You’ve served me well enough, in your own vile way. I need one more service from you, and then I’ll release you from your oath.

“Conan the Cimmerian, King of Aquilonia, still lives—whether in spite of Xaltotun’s sorcery or because of it, I don’t know. The dark mind of that resurrected devil is too twisted and cunning for any mortal to understand. But as long as Conan lives, I’m not safe. The people accepted me as the lesser of two evils when they thought he was dead. If he reappears, the throne will be shaking under me in rebellion before I can even raise my hand.

“Maybe my allies plan to use him to replace me if they decide I’ve outlived my usefulness. I don’t know. But I do know this world isn’t big enough for two kings of Aquilonia. Find the Cimmerian. Use your strange powers to track him down wherever he hides or runs. He has many friends in Tarantia. He had help when he carried off Albiona. It took more than one man—even a man like Conan—to cause all that slaughter in the alley outside the tower. But enough talk. Take your staffs and follow his trail. Where it leads, I can’t say. But find him! And when you find him—kill him!”

The four Khitans bowed together, still silent, then turned and slipped noiselessly out of the chamber.

11 - SWORDS OF THE SOUTH  
Swords of the South

The dawn rising over the distant hills shone on the sails of a small boat drifting down the river that curved to within a mile of Tarantia’s walls before looping southward like a great shining serpent. This boat was unlike the usual vessels that traveled the wide Khorotas—fishing boats and merchant barges heavy with goods. It was long and narrow, with a high, curving prow, and painted black as ebony, with white skulls along the gunwales. In the middle stood a small cabin with its windows tightly covered. Other boats gave the ominous craft a wide berth, for it was clearly one of those “pilgrim boats” that carried a dead follower of Asura on his final mysterious journey southward, to where, far beyond the Poitanian mountains, a river finally met the blue sea. Inside that cabin, people assumed, lay the corpse of the departed worshipper. Everyone knew the sight of those gloomy boats, and even the most fanatical follower of Mitra would never dare touch or interfere with their somber voyages.

No one knew where those journeys ended. Some said Stygia; others claimed a nameless island beyond the horizon; still others whispered it was in the enchanted and mysterious land of Vendhya, where the dead finally came home. But no one knew for sure. They only knew that when a follower of Asura died, the body went south down the great river in a black boat rowed by a giant slave—and neither boat, corpse, nor slave was ever seen again. Unless, of course, the dark rumors were true, and it was always the same slave rowing every boat southward.

The man rowing this particular boat was as huge and dark-skinned as the others, though a closer look might have revealed that his skin color came from carefully applied dyes. He wore only a leather loincloth and sandals, and he handled the long oars with great strength and skill. But no one came near the grim vessel, for everyone knew the followers of Asura were cursed, and that these pilgrim boats carried dark magic. So other sailors steered clear and muttered protective prayers as the black craft glided past—never realizing they were helping their king and Countess Albiona escape.

It was a strange journey, that flight down the great river in the black, slender boat—nearly two hundred miles to where the Khorotas turned eastward, skirting the Poitanian mountains. The ever-changing landscape drifted by like a dream. During the day, Albiona lay quietly in the small cabin, as still as the corpse she pretended to be. Only late at night, after the pleasure boats with their noble passengers lounging on silk cushions under torchlight had left the river, and before dawn brought the fishermen back, did she dare come out. Then she took the long oar, which had been cleverly tied in place with ropes to help her, while Conan caught a few hours of sleep. But the king needed little rest. The fire of his purpose drove him relentlessly, and his powerful body could endure the strain. Without pause, they pressed southward.

So they fled down the river—through nights when the flowing current reflected a million stars, and through days of golden sunlight, leaving winter behind as they sped south. They passed cities at night, their skies glowing with countless lights, and grand riverside villas surrounded by fertile groves. At last, the blue mountains of Poitain rose before them, tier upon tier like the ramparts of the gods, and the great river, turning from those towering cliffs, thundered through the marching hills in rapids and foaming waterfalls.

Conan studied the shoreline carefully, then turned the long oar and steered toward land at a point where a narrow strip of earth jutted into the water, and fir trees grew in a strangely perfect circle around a gray, oddly shaped rock.

“I can’t figure out how these boats manage to ride those falls we hear roaring ahead,” he grunted. “Hadrathus said they could—but this is where we stop. He said someone would be waiting for us with horses, but I don’t see anyone. I don’t even know how word of our coming could have gotten here before us.”

He steered the boat toward the shore and tied the prow to a curved root jutting from the low bank. Then he plunged into the water, washed the brown paint from his skin, and came out dripping, his natural color restored. From the cabin he brought out a suit of Aquilonian chain mail that Hadrathus had gotten for him, along with his sword. He put them on while Albiona dressed in clothes fit for mountain travel. When Conan was fully armed and turned toward the shore, he froze and his hand went to his sword. Standing under the trees was a black-cloaked figure holding the reins of a white palfrey and a bay warhorse.

“Who are you?” demanded the king.

The stranger bowed low.

“A follower of Asura. A command came. I obeyed.”

“How did it ‘come’?” asked Conan, but the man only bowed again.

“I’ve come to guide you through the mountains to the first Poitanian stronghold.”

“I don’t need a guide,” Conan replied. “I know these hills well. I thank you for the horses, but the countess and I will draw less attention if we travel alone than if we’re accompanied by an acolyte of Asura.”

The man bowed deeply, handed Conan the reins, and stepped into the boat. Casting off, he drifted down the swift current toward the distant roar of the unseen rapids. Shaking his head in confusion, Conan lifted the countess into the palfrey’s saddle, mounted the warhorse himself, and turned toward the peaks that crowned the sky like castles.

The rolling land at the base of the towering mountains was now a border region in turmoil, where barons had reverted to feudal ways and bands of outlaws roamed freely. Poitain hadn’t officially declared independence from Aquilonia, but for all practical purposes it was a self-contained kingdom ruled by its hereditary count, Trocero. The southern lowlands had nominally submitted to Valerius, but he hadn’t dared to force the mountain passes guarded by fortresses where the crimson leopard banner of Poitain flew defiantly.

The king and his fair companion rode up the long blue slopes in the soft evening light. As they climbed higher, the rolling country spread out beneath them like a vast purple cloak, streaked with the shine of rivers and lakes, the golden glint of wide fields, and the white gleam of distant towers. Ahead and far above, they saw the first of the Poitanian strongholds—a mighty fortress commanding a narrow pass, its crimson banner streaming against the clear blue sky.

Before they reached it, a band of knights in gleaming armor rode out from the trees, and their leader sternly ordered them to halt. They were tall men with the dark eyes and black hair of the south.

“Halt, sir, and state your business, and why you ride toward Poitain.”

“Is Poitain in rebellion, then?” asked Conan, watching him closely. “That a man in Aquilonian armor is stopped and questioned like a foreigner?”

“Many rogues ride out of Aquilonia these days,” the other answered coldly. “As for rebellion—if you mean rejecting a usurper, then yes, Poitain is in revolt. We’d rather serve the memory of a dead man than the scepter of a living dog.”

Conan pulled off his helmet, shook back his black mane, and looked the speaker straight in the eye. The Poitanian stared, went pale, and gasped.

“Saints above! It’s the king—alive!”

The others stared in shock, then a roar of wonder and joy burst from them. They crowded around Conan, shouting their war cries and waving their swords in wild excitement. The cheers of Poitanian warriors were enough to terrify a timid man.

“Oh, Trocero will weep tears of joy to see you, sire!” one cried.

“Aye, and Prospero!” shouted another. “The general’s been sunk in gloom, cursing himself day and night for not reaching Valkia in time to die beside his king!”

“Now we’ll strike for empire!” yelled another, swinging his great sword over his head. “Hail, Conan, king of Poitain!”

The clash of bright steel and the thunder of their cheers startled flocks of brightly colored birds from the surrounding trees. The hot southern blood of the knights was aflame—they wanted nothing more than for their rediscovered king to lead them into battle and plunder.

“What are your orders, sire?” they cried. “Let one of us ride ahead and spread the news of your coming through Poitain! Banners will wave from every tower, roses will carpet the road before your horse’s hooves, and all the beauty and chivalry of the south will honor you as you deserve—”

Conan shook his head.

“Who could doubt your loyalty? But the winds that blow over these mountains carry word into the lands of my enemies, and I’d rather they didn’t know I’m alive—yet. Take me to Trocero, and keep my identity secret.”

So what the knights had hoped would be a triumphal procession became more like a secret flight. They traveled quickly, speaking to no one except for a quiet word to the captain on duty at each pass, and Conan rode among them with his visor lowered.

The mountains were uninhabited except for outlaws and the garrisons that guarded the passes. The pleasure-loving Poitanians had neither the need nor the desire to wrest a hard living from those harsh slopes. South of the ranges stretched the rich, beautiful plains of Poitain, reaching to the river Alimane; beyond that river lay the land of Zingara.

Even now, when winter was crisping the leaves beyond the mountains, tall, lush grass waved on the plains where grazed the horses and cattle that made Poitain famous. Palm trees and orange groves glowed in the sun, and the purple, gold, and crimson towers of castles and cities shone in the golden light. It was a land of warmth and abundance, of handsome men and fierce warriors. Hard lands aren’t the only ones that breed hard men. Poitain was surrounded by greedy neighbors, and her sons had learned toughness through endless wars. To the north, the mountains guarded the land, but to the south only the Alimane separated Poitain’s plains from those of Zingara—and that river had run red not once but a thousand times. To the east lay Argos, and beyond it Ophir, proud and covetous kingdoms. The knights of Poitain held their lands by the weight and edge of their swords, and they knew little of ease or idleness.

So Conan soon came to the castle of Count Trocero.  

Conan sat on a silk-covered couch in a luxurious room where the warm breeze stirred the thin curtains. Trocero paced the floor like a panther, a lean, restless man with a narrow waist and the broad shoulders of a swordsman, who carried his years lightly.  

“Let us proclaim you King of Poitain!” urged the count. “Let those northern swine wear the yoke they’ve already bowed their necks to. The south is still yours. Stay here and rule us, among the flowers and the palms.”  

But Conan shook his head. “There’s no nobler land on earth than Poitain. But it can’t stand alone, no matter how brave its sons are.”  

“It did stand alone for generations,” Trocero shot back, his voice sharp with the quick, jealous pride of his people. “We weren’t always part of Aquilonia.”  

“I know. But things aren’t as they were then, when every kingdom was broken into small principalities that fought each other. The age of dukedoms and free cities is over; the age of empires has come. Rulers dream of empires now, and only unity brings strength.”  

“Then let’s unite Zingara with Poitain,” argued Trocero. “Half a dozen princes fight each other there, and the land is torn apart by civil wars. We’ll conquer it, province by province, and add it to your realm. Then, with the help of the Zingarans, we’ll conquer Argos and Ophir. We’ll build an empire—”  

Again Conan shook his head. “Let others dream of empires. I only want to hold what’s mine. I’ve no wish to rule an empire forged by blood and fire. It’s one thing to take a throne with the help of its people and rule with their consent. It’s another to crush a foreign land and rule it through fear. I don’t want to become another Valerius. No, Trocero, I’ll rule all Aquilonia and nothing less—or I’ll rule nothing at all.”  

“Then lead us over the mountains and we’ll strike the Nemedians.”  

Conan’s fierce eyes gleamed with approval.  

“No, Trocero. That would be a useless sacrifice. I’ve told you what I must do to win back my kingdom. I have to find the Heart of Ahriman.”  

“But that’s madness!” protested Trocero. “The ravings of a heretic priest, the mutterings of a crazy witch-woman.”  

“You weren’t in my tent before Valkia,” Conan answered grimly, glancing unconsciously at his right wrist, where faint blue marks still showed. “You didn’t see the cliffs crash down and destroy the flower of my army. No, Trocero, I’m convinced. Xaltotun isn’t a mortal man, and only with the Heart of Ahriman can I stand against him. So I’m riding to Kordava—alone.”  

“But that’s dangerous,” protested Trocero.  

“Life is dangerous,” rumbled the king. “I won’t go as the King of Aquilonia, or even as a knight of Poitain, but as a wandering mercenary, as I once rode in Zingara. Oh, I’ve got enemies enough south of the Alimane, in the lands and seas of the south. Many who won’t know me as King of Aquilonia will remember me as Conan of the Barachan pirates, or Amra of the black corsairs. But I’ve got friends too, and men who’ll help me for their own reasons.” A faint, reminiscent grin touched his lips.  

Trocero dropped his hands helplessly and looked toward Albiona, who sat on a nearby couch.  

“I understand your doubts, my lord,” she said. “But I too saw the coin in the temple of Asura, and look—you remember Hadrathus said it was dated five hundred years before the fall of Acheron. If Xaltotun is the man pictured on that coin, as his Majesty swears he is, then he wasn’t an ordinary wizard even in his former life, for his years were counted by centuries, not as other men’s lives are.”  

Before Trocero could reply, a respectful knock came at the door and a voice called, “My lord, we’ve caught a man sneaking around the castle who says he wishes to speak with your guest. I await your orders.”  

“A spy from Aquilonia!” hissed Trocero, grabbing for his dagger, but Conan raised his voice and called, “Open the door and let me see him.”  

The door opened, and a man stood framed in it, held firmly by two stern-faced guards. He was a slender man, dressed in a dark hooded robe.  

“Are you a follower of Asura?” asked Conan.  

The man nodded, and the strong guards looked shocked, glancing uncertainly at Trocero.  

“The word has come south,” said the man. “Beyond the Alimane we can’t help you, for our sect goes no farther south, but stretches eastward along the Khorotas. But this I’ve learned: the thief who stole the Heart of Ahriman from Tarascus never reached Kordava. In the mountains of Poitain he was killed by bandits. The jewel fell into the hands of their chief, who, not knowing what it really was, and being hunted after his band was destroyed by Poitanian knights, sold it to the Kothic merchant Zorathus.”  

“Ha!” Conan leapt to his feet, electrified. “And what of Zorathus?”  

“Four days ago he crossed the Alimane, heading for Argos, with a small group of armed servants.”  

“He’s a fool to cross Zingara in times like these,” said Trocero.  

“Yes, the times are troubled across the river. But Zorathus is a bold man, reckless in his way. He’s in great haste to reach Messantia, where he hopes to find a buyer for the jewel. Maybe he plans to sell it finally in Stygia. Maybe he suspects what it really is. In any case, instead of following the long road that winds along the borders of Poitain and eventually reaches Argos far from Messantia, he’s gone straight across eastern Zingara, taking the shorter, more direct route.”  

Conan slammed his fist on the table so hard the heavy board shook.  

“Then, by Crom, fortune has finally rolled the dice for me! A horse, Trocero, and the gear of a Free Companion! Zorathus has a long head start, but not too long for me to catch him—even if I have to chase him to the ends of the earth!”

12 - THE FANG OF THE DRAGON  
The Fang of the Dragon  

At dawn, Conan guided his horse through the shallow waters of the Alimane and reached the wide caravan road that ran southeast. Behind him, on the far bank, Trocero sat silently on his horse at the head of his steel-clad knights, the crimson leopard of Poitain fluttering its long folds above him in the morning breeze. The dark-haired men in shining armor sat motionless until the figure of their king disappeared into the blue distance that brightened toward sunrise.  

Conan rode a massive black stallion, a gift from Trocero. He no longer wore the armor of Aquilonia. His gear marked him as a veteran of the Free Companies, men of all races. His helmet was a plain, dented, and battered morion. The leather and chain mesh of his hauberk were worn and polished from long use, and the scarlet cloak hanging loosely from his armored shoulders was tattered and stained. He looked every bit the seasoned mercenary—one who had known all the ups and downs of fortune, rich with plunder one day, broke and hungry the next.  

And more than just looking the part, he felt it. Old memories stirred within him—the wild, reckless, glorious days before he had taken the imperial path, when he had been a wandering soldier of fortune, drinking, brawling, and adventuring without a thought for tomorrow, desiring nothing but strong ale, red lips, and a sharp sword to swing on the battlefields of the world.  

Without realizing it, he slipped back into his old habits; a new swagger appeared in his posture and the way he sat his horse. Half-forgotten oaths came naturally to his lips, and as he rode, he hummed old songs he had once roared in chorus with his reckless comrades in countless taverns, along dusty roads, and on bloody fields.  

He rode through a restless land. The cavalry patrols that usually guarded the river against raids from Poitain were nowhere to be seen. Civil strife had left the borders unprotected. The long white road stretched empty from horizon to horizon. No camel caravans, rumbling wagons, or herds moved along it now—only scattered groups of horsemen in leather and steel, hawk-faced, hard-eyed men who rode close together and kept alert. They gave Conan sharp, measuring looks but rode on, for his worn armor promised no loot, only hard fighting.  

Villages lay burned and deserted, the fields and meadows untended. Only the boldest dared travel the roads now, and the native population had been decimated by civil wars and raids from across the river. In more peaceful times, the road had been crowded with merchants traveling between Poitain and Messantia in Argos, but now they preferred the safer route east through Poitain and then south across Argos. It was longer, but far less dangerous. Only a foolhardy man would risk his life and goods on this road through Zingara.  

At night, the southern horizon glowed with fire, and by day, thin columns of smoke drifted upward. In the cities and plains to the south, men were dying, thrones were falling, and castles were burning. Conan felt the old pull of the professional soldier—to turn his horse southward and plunge into the chaos, the fighting, the looting, as in the old days. Why should he struggle to reclaim the rule of a people who had already forgotten him? Why chase a phantom crown that was gone forever? Why not lose himself again in the red tides of war and plunder that had so often engulfed him before? Could he not carve out another kingdom for himself? The world was entering an age of iron, an age of war and ambition; some strong man might rise above the ruins of nations as a supreme conqueror. Why not him? So whispered his familiar inner demon, and the ghosts of his lawless, bloody past crowded his mind. But he did not turn aside; he rode on, following a quest that grew dimmer the farther he went, until it seemed at times that he was chasing a dream that had never existed.  

He pushed the black stallion as hard as he dared, but the long white road stretched empty before him, from horizon to horizon. Zorathus had a long head start, but Conan rode steadily, knowing he was traveling faster than the burdened merchants could. And so he came to the castle of Count Valbroso, perched like a vulture’s nest on a bare hill overlooking the road.  

Valbroso rode down with his men-at-arms—a lean, dark man with glittering eyes and a sharp, predatory nose. He wore black plate armor and was followed by thirty spearmen, black-mustached hawks of the border wars, as greedy and ruthless as their master. Lately, the toll from caravans had been poor, and Valbroso cursed the civil wars that had emptied the roads of rich traffic, even as he blessed them for giving him a free hand with his neighbors.  

He hadn’t expected much from the lone rider he had spotted from his tower, but anything that came his way was worth investigating. With a practiced eye, he took in Conan’s worn mail and scarred, dark face, and came to the same conclusion as the riders who had passed the Cimmerian on the road—an empty purse and a ready sword.  

“Who are you, knave?” he demanded.  

“A mercenary, riding for Argos,” Conan replied. “What do names matter?”  

“You’re riding the wrong way for a Free Companion,” Valbroso grunted. “The fighting—and the plunder—are to the south. Join my company. You won’t go hungry. The road’s empty of fat merchants to rob, but I plan to take my men south and sell our swords to whichever side looks strongest.”  

Conan didn’t answer right away, knowing that if he refused outright, Valbroso’s men might attack him on the spot. Before he could decide, the Zingaran spoke again:  

“You Free Company rogues always know tricks to make men talk. I’ve got a prisoner—the last merchant I caught, by Mitra, and the only one I’ve seen in a week—and the dog’s stubborn. He’s got an iron box that none of us can open, and I haven’t been able to persuade him to unlock it. By Ishtar, I thought I knew every method of persuasion there was, but maybe you, as a veteran Free Companion, know some I don’t. Anyway, come with me and see what you can do.”

Valbroso’s words immediately made up Conan’s mind. That sounded a lot like Zorathus. Conan didn’t know the merchant personally, but any man stubborn enough to try traveling the Zingaran road in times like these was probably stubborn enough to resist torture.  

He fell in beside Valbroso and rode up the uneven road to the top of the hill where the grim castle stood. As a man-at-arms, he should have ridden behind the count, but habit made him careless, and Valbroso didn’t seem to care. Years of living on the border had taught the count that the frontier wasn’t the royal court. He understood the independence of mercenaries, behind whose swords many kings had climbed to their thrones.  

There was a dry moat, half filled with rubble in some spots. They clattered across the drawbridge and through the gate arch. Behind them, the portcullis dropped with a heavy clang. They entered a bare courtyard overgrown with scraggly grass, with a well in the center. Shacks for the men-at-arms leaned against the bailey wall, and women—some slovenly, others dressed in cheap finery—peered from the doorways. Fighters in rusty mail tossed dice on the flagstones under the arches. The place looked more like a bandit’s stronghold than a nobleman’s castle.  

Valbroso dismounted and motioned for Conan to follow. They went through a doorway and down a vaulted corridor, where they met a scarred, hard-faced man in armor coming down a stone staircase—clearly the captain of the guard.  

“Well, Beloso,” said Valbroso. “Has he talked?”  

“He’s stubborn,” muttered Beloso, casting a suspicious glance at Conan.  

Valbroso swore violently and stormed up the winding stairs, followed by Conan and the captain. As they climbed, the groans of a man in mortal agony reached their ears. Valbroso’s torture chamber was high above the courtyard instead of buried in a dungeon below. In that room, where a gaunt, hairy brute in leather breeches squatted, gnawing a beef bone greedily, stood the instruments of torture—racks, boots, hooks, and all the devices human cruelty had invented to tear flesh, break bones, and rip veins and ligaments.  

On a rack a man lay stretched naked, and one glance told Conan he was dying. The unnatural length of his limbs and body showed that his joints had been pulled apart and his insides torn. He was a dark man with an intelligent, hawk-like face and quick dark eyes. Now they were glazed and bloodshot with pain, and sweat from agony glistened on his face. His lips were drawn back from blackened gums.  

“There’s the box.” Valbroso kicked a small but heavy iron chest viciously. It was intricately carved with tiny skulls and writhing dragons intertwined in strange patterns, but Conan saw no latch or hinge that could open the lid. Marks of fire, axe, hammer, and chisel showed on it only as scratches.  

“This is the dog’s treasure chest,” said Valbroso angrily. “Everyone in the south knows of Zorathus and his iron box. Mitra only knows what’s inside it. But he won’t give up its secret.”  

Zorathus! So it was true—the man he sought lay before him. Conan’s heart pounded painfully as he leaned over the writhing figure, though he showed no sign of his intense eagerness.  

“Loosen those ropes, you dog!” he ordered the torturer sharply, and Valbroso and his captain stared. In his moment of forgetfulness, Conan had used his commanding tone, and the brute in leather instinctively obeyed the edge of authority in that voice. He loosened the ropes slowly, for a sudden release would have been as painful to the torn joints as further stretching.  

Grabbing a jug of wine that stood nearby, Conan pressed it to the man’s lips. Zorathus gulped convulsively, the wine spilling over his heaving chest.  

A flicker of recognition came into the bloodshot eyes, and the foam-smeared lips parted. A broken whisper came out in the Kothic tongue.  

“Is this death, then? Is the long torment over? For this is King Conan, who died at Valkia, and I am among the dead.”  

“You’re not dead,” said Conan. “But you’re dying. You’ll be tortured no more—I’ll see to that. But I can’t help you further. Before you die, tell me how to open your iron box!”  

“My iron box,” mumbled Zorathus in delirious, broken phrases. “The chest forged in unholy fires among the burning mountains of Khrosha; the metal no chisel can cut. How many treasures it has carried across the world! But none like the one it holds now.”  

“Tell me how to open it,” urged Conan. “It can’t help you anymore, but it might help me.”  

“Yes, you are Conan,” muttered the Kothian. “I’ve seen you sitting on your throne in the great hall of Tarantia, with your crown on your head and your scepter in your hand. But you’re dead—you died at Valkia. So I know my own end is near.”  

“What’s the dog saying?” demanded Valbroso impatiently, not understanding Kothic. “Will he tell us how to open the box?”  

As if the voice sparked a last flicker of life in him, Zorathus rolled his bloodshot eyes toward the speaker.  

“Only Valbroso will I tell,” he gasped in Zingaran. “Death is upon me. Lean close, Valbroso!”  

The count did so, his dark face glowing with greed; behind him, his grim captain Beloso pressed closer.  

“Press the seven skulls on the rim, one after another,” gasped Zorathus. “Then press the head of the dragon that coils across the lid. Then press the sphere in the dragon’s claws. That will release the hidden catch.”  

“Quick, the box!” shouted Valbroso with an oath.  

Conan lifted it and set it on a dais, and Valbroso shoved him aside.  

“Let me open it!” cried Beloso, stepping forward.  

Valbroso cursed him back, greed blazing in his black eyes.  

“No one but me will open it!” he shouted.

Conan, whose hand had instinctively gone to his sword hilt, glanced at Zorathus. The man’s eyes were glazed and bloodshot, but they were locked on Valbroso with burning intensity. Was that a grim, twisted smile forming on the dying man’s lips? Only when the merchant knew he was dying had he given up the secret. Conan turned to watch Valbroso, just as the dying man watched him.  

Along the edge of the lid, seven skulls were carved among intertwining branches of strange trees. An inlaid dragon twisted its way across the top of the lid amid ornate designs. Valbroso pressed the skulls in frantic haste, and as he jammed his thumb down on the carved dragon’s head, he cursed sharply and jerked his hand back, shaking it in irritation.  

“A sharp point in the carving,” he snarled. “I pricked my thumb.”  

He pressed the golden orb clutched in the dragon’s talons, and the lid flew open suddenly. Their eyes were blinded by a golden blaze. It seemed to their dazed minds that the carved box was full of glowing fire that spilled over the rim and drifted through the air in trembling flakes. Beloso cried out, and Valbroso sucked in his breath. Conan stood speechless, his mind caught in the brilliance.  

“Mitra, what a jewel!” Valbroso’s hand plunged into the chest and came out holding a great pulsing crimson sphere that filled the room with a flickering glow. In its light, Valbroso looked like a corpse. The dying man on the loosened rack suddenly laughed wildly.  

“Fool!” he screamed. “The jewel is yours! I give you death with it! The scratch on your thumb—look at the dragon’s head, Valbroso!”  

They all turned and stared. Something tiny and dull gleamed from the open, carved mouth.  

“The dragon’s fang!” shrieked Zorathus. “Soaked in the venom of the black Stygian scorpion! Fool, fool to open the box of Zorathus with your bare hand! Death! You’re a dead man now!”  

And with bloody foam on his lips, he died.  

Valbroso staggered, crying out. “Ah, Mitra, I burn!” he screamed. “My veins are filled with liquid fire! My joints are bursting apart! Death! Death!” He reeled and crashed to the floor. There was a moment of horrible convulsions, his limbs twisting into grotesque, unnatural shapes, and then he froze in that position, his glassy eyes staring upward, his lips drawn back from blackened gums.  

“Dead,” muttered Conan, bending to pick up the jewel where it had rolled from Valbroso’s stiff hand. It lay on the floor like a trembling pool of sunset fire.  

“Dead,” muttered Beloso, madness in his eyes. Then he moved.  

Conan was caught off guard, his eyes dazzled, his mind dazed by the gem’s glow. He didn’t realize Beloso’s intent until something crashed down on his helmet with terrible force. The jewel’s glow flared redder, and he dropped to his knees under the blow.  

He heard rushing footsteps, a bellow of animal-like agony. He was stunned but not completely senseless, and realized that Beloso had grabbed the iron box and smashed it down on his head as he bent over. Only his helmet had saved his skull. He staggered up, drawing his sword, trying to shake the fog from his vision. The room swam before his eyes. But the door was open, and quick footsteps were fading down the winding stairs. On the floor, the brutish torturer was gasping out his life with a deep wound under his chest. And the Heart of Ahriman was gone.  

Conan stumbled out of the chamber, sword in hand, blood streaming down his face from under his helmet. He ran unsteadily down the steps, hearing the clash of steel in the courtyard below, shouts, then the frantic pounding of hooves. Bursting into the bailey, he saw the men-at-arms milling in confusion while women screamed. The postern gate stood open, and a soldier lay across his pike with his head split open. Horses, still bridled and saddled, ran neighing around the yard, Conan’s black stallion among them.  

“He’s gone mad!” screamed a woman, wringing her hands as she ran about in panic. “He came out of the castle like a mad dog, slashing at everyone! Beloso’s gone mad! Where’s Lord Valbroso?”  

“Which way did he go?” roared Conan.  

Everyone turned and stared at the stranger’s blood-smeared face and drawn sword.  

“Through the postern!” shrieked a woman, pointing eastward, and another shouted, “Who is this man?”  

“Beloso has killed Valbroso!” yelled Conan, leaping forward and grabbing his stallion’s mane as the men-at-arms hesitated. A wild uproar broke out at his words, but their reaction was exactly what he expected. As far as he knew, the door had been bolted from the inside—but now it stood open, and four men entered the room. At the sight of them, his skin crawled. Publio had seen many strange people in his life, but never anyone like these. “Where is Conan, the one who was king of Aquilonia?” asked the tallest of the four in a flat, emotionless tone that made Publio shiver. It sounded like the hollow ringing of a Khitan temple bell.

“He has been here,” said the other, his voice unchanged. “His horse is in the courtyard. Tell us where he is before we harm you.”

He had never fallen asleep on duty before. But his master’s voice was shrill with hysteria, and the Shemite charged like a bull at the strangers, his thick arm drawing back for a gutting thrust. But the blow never landed.

The merchant wasn’t a coward, but these unnatural visitors had turned his blood to ice. “Your servant?” asked the Khitan.

“None!” gasped Publio, his lips white. “Go!”

The thug bowed and hurried out, vaguely thinking that Publio was a man of weak stomach and few words.

The four Khitans emerged from behind the tapestry.

“Who was that man talking about?” the tallest one demanded.

“A wandering stranger who wronged me,” panted Publio.

‘You’re lying,’ said the Khitan calmly. ‘He spoke of the king of Aquilonia. I saw it in your face. Sit down on that couch and don’t move or speak. I’ll stay here with you while my three companions go search for the body.’

So Publio sat trembling with fear before the silent, unreadable figure watching him, until the three Khitans returned with the news that Conan’s body wasn’t lying on the sands. Publio didn’t know whether to feel relieved or afraid.

‘We found the place where the fight happened,’ they said. ‘There was blood on the sand. But the king was gone.’

The fourth Khitan traced invisible symbols on the carpet with his staff, which gleamed like scales in the lamplight.

‘Did you read anything from the sands?’ he asked.

‘Yes,’ they answered. ‘The king lives, and he’s gone southward on a ship.’

The tall Khitan lifted his head and stared at Publio until the merchant broke into a heavy sweat.

‘What do you want from me?’ he stammered.

‘A ship,’ said the Khitan. ‘A ship well-manned for a very long voyage.’

‘How long a voyage?’ Publio stuttered, not even thinking to refuse.

‘To the ends of the world, maybe,’ said the Khitan, ‘or to the molten seas of hell beyond the sunrise.’

Conan’s first feeling as he regained consciousness was motion; there was no solid ground beneath him, only constant rolling and pitching. Then he heard the wind whistling through ropes and spars and knew he was on a ship even before his blurred vision cleared. He heard voices muttering, and then a splash of water hit him full in the face, jerking him sharply awake. He shot up with a curse, braced his legs, and glared around him, hearing coarse laughter and smelling the stench of unwashed bodies.

He was standing on the poop deck of a long galley running before the northern wind, its striped sail bulging tight against the ropes. The sun was just rising in a blaze of gold, blue, and green. To the left, the shoreline was a dim purple shadow; to the right stretched the open sea. Conan took all this in at a glance, along with the ship itself.

It was long and narrow, a typical southern trading ship, high at both bow and stern, with cabins at each end. Conan looked down into the open middle section, from which rose that sickening, foul smell he knew too well—the body odor of chained oarsmen. They were all black men, forty on each side, each fastened by a chain around his waist, the other end welded to a heavy ring set deep in the solid beam running the ship’s length. Life as a slave on an Argossean galley was a hell beyond imagining. Most were Kushites, but about thirty of the blacks now resting on their oars and staring up at him with dull curiosity were from the far southern islands—the homelands of the corsairs. Conan recognized them by their straighter features, their hair, and their leaner, stronger builds. Among them he saw men who had once followed him.

But all this he noticed in a single sweeping glance as he rose, before turning his attention to the men around him. Still unsteady on his legs, fists clenched in anger, he glared at the sailors gathered nearby. The one who had thrown the water stood grinning, the empty bucket still in his hand, and Conan cursed him furiously, instinctively reaching for his sword—only to realize he was unarmed and wearing nothing but short leather breeches.

‘What cursed ship is this?’ he roared. ‘How did I end up here?’

The sailors laughed mockingly—stocky, bearded Argosseans, every one of them—and one, whose finer clothes and commanding air marked him as captain, folded his arms and said arrogantly, ‘We found you lying on the sand. Someone had knocked you out and taken your clothes. We needed another man, so we brought you aboard.’

‘What ship is this?’ Conan demanded.

‘The Venturer, out of Messantia,’ the captain said, ‘carrying mirrors, scarlet silk cloaks, shields, gilded helmets, and swords to trade with the Shemites for copper and gold ore. I’m Demetrio, captain of this ship—and your master from now on.’

‘Then I’m headed where I wanted to go after all,’ Conan muttered, ignoring the last part. They were sailing southeast, following the long curve of the Argossean coast. These trading ships never strayed far from shore. Somewhere ahead, he knew, that dark Stygian galley was speeding southward.

‘Have you seen a Stygian galley—’ Conan began, but the burly, rough-faced captain’s beard bristled. He wasn’t interested in any questions from his new “recruit” and decided it was time to put this defiant stranger in his place.

‘Get forward!’ he roared. ‘I’ve wasted enough time on you! I did you the honor of bringing you up here to wake you and answered enough of your damned questions. Get off this deck! You’ll work your passage on this galley—’

‘I’ll buy your ship—’ Conan started to say, before remembering he was broke.

A roar of laughter greeted his words, and the captain turned purple, thinking Conan was mocking him.

‘You mutinous dog!’ he bellowed, stepping forward with his hand on the knife at his belt. ‘Get forward before I have you flogged! Keep a civil tongue, or by Mitra, I’ll have you chained with the blacks to pull an oar!’

Conan’s volcanic temper, never long restrained, exploded. Not in years—not even before he was king—had anyone spoken to him like that and lived.

‘Don’t raise your voice to me, you tar-stained cur!’ he roared, his voice booming like the sea wind while the sailors stared in shock. ‘Draw that toy and I’ll feed you to the sharks!’

‘Who do you think you are?’ gasped the captain.

“I’ll show you!” roared the enraged Cimmerian, spinning around and leaping toward the rail where the weapons hung in their brackets.  

The captain drew his knife and charged at him, shouting, but before he could strike, Conan seized his wrist and twisted with such force that the arm tore clean out of its socket. The captain bellowed like an ox in pain, then rolled across the deck as Conan contemptuously hurled him aside. Conan ripped a heavy axe from the rail and spun like a cat to face the rushing sailors. They charged, shouting like hounds, clumsy and slow compared to the panther-like Cimmerian. Before they could reach him with their knives, he sprang among them, striking right and left too fast for the eye to follow. Blood and brains splattered as two bodies hit the deck.  

Knives slashed wildly through the air as Conan broke through the stumbling, gasping mob and bounded onto the narrow bridge that spanned the waist from poop to forecastle, just out of reach of the slaves below. Behind him, the few sailors on the poop were stumbling after him, shaken by the slaughter of their comrades, while the rest of the crew—about thirty in all—came running across the bridge toward him, weapons in hand.  

Conan leaped onto the bridge and stood poised above the upturned black faces, axe raised, black mane whipping in the wind.  

“Who am I?” he shouted. “Look at me, you dogs! Look, Ajonga, Yasunga, Laranga! Who am I?”  

And from the waist rose a shout that swelled into a mighty roar: “Amra! It’s Amra! The Lion has returned!”  

The sailors who caught and understood the meaning of that terrifying cry went pale and shrank back, staring in sudden fear at the wild figure on the bridge. Could this truly be that bloodthirsty demon of the southern seas who had vanished mysteriously years ago, yet still lived on in bloody legend? The blacks were now in a frenzy, shaking and tearing at their chains, screaming the name of Amra like a prayer. Kushites who had never seen Conan before took up the cry. The slaves in the pen beneath the after-cabin began battering at the walls, shrieking like the damned.  

Demetrio, dragging himself along the deck on one hand and his knees, his face pale with the agony of his dislocated arm, screamed, “Get in there and kill him, dogs, before the slaves break loose!”  

Driven to desperation by that word—the one all galley men feared most—the sailors charged onto the bridge from both ends. But with a lion-like leap, Conan left the bridge and landed catlike on his feet on the walkway between the benches.  

“Death to the masters!” he thundered, and his axe came crashing down full on a shackle chain, splitting it like kindling. In an instant, a screaming slave was free, smashing his oar into a club. Men were racing frantically along the bridge above, and chaos exploded aboard the Venturer. Conan’s axe rose and fell without pause, and with every stroke another frothing, screaming black giant broke free, mad with hatred and the fury of freedom and vengeance.  

Sailors leaping down into the waist to grapple with or strike at the naked white giant hacking like a madman at the shackles found themselves dragged down by the hands of slaves still chained, while others, their broken chains whipping around their limbs, surged up from the waist like a blind black flood, screaming like demons, striking with broken oars and bits of iron, tearing and rending with claws and teeth. In the midst of the chaos, the slaves in the pen smashed down the walls and came surging up onto the deck, and with fifty blacks freed from their benches, Conan left off breaking chains and bounded up onto the bridge to add his notched axe to the bludgeons of his followers.  

Then came the slaughter. The Argosseans were strong, sturdy, and fearless, like all their race, hardened by the brutal life of the sea. But they could not stand against these maddened giants led by the tigerish barbarian. Beatings, torment, and hellish suffering were avenged in one red storm of fury that raged like a typhoon from one end of the ship to the other. When it was over, only one white man lived aboard the Venturer—the blood-smeared giant around whom the chanting blacks gathered, throwing themselves prostrate on the bloody deck and beating their heads against the boards in a frenzy of worship.  

Conan, his massive chest heaving and glistening with sweat, the red axe gripped in his blood-smeared hand, glared around him like the first chief of men might have glared in the dawn of time, shaking back his black mane. In that moment, he was not the king of Aquilonia; he was once again the lord of the black corsairs, who had hacked his way to power through fire and blood.  

“Amra! Amra!” chanted the delirious blacks, those who still lived to chant. “The Lion has returned! Now the Stygians will howl like dogs in the night, and the black dogs of Kush will howl! “Stop that racket, you dogs!” Conan roared, his voice drowning out the snap of the sail in the wind. “Ten of you go below and free the oarsmen still chained. With shouts, laughter, and wild singing, they leaped to obey. The corpses, white and black alike, were hurled overboard, where triangular fins were already slicing through the water.  

Conan stood on the poop, frowning down at the black men who watched him expectantly. His heavy brown arms were folded, his long black hair, grown wild during his wanderings, whipped in the wind. A wilder, more barbaric figure had never stood on a ship’s bridge, and few of the courtiers of Aquilonia would have recognized their king in this fierce corsair.  

“There’s food in the hold!” he roared. “Plenty of weapons for you, for this ship carried blades and armor to the Shemites along the coast. There are enough of us to sail her—and to fight! You rowed in chains for the Argossean dogs; will you row as free men for Amra?”

“Aye!” they shouted. “We’re your children! Lead us wherever you want!”  

“Then get to work and clean out that deck,” he ordered. “Free men don’t live in such filth. Three of you come with me and fetch food from the after-cabin. By Crom, I’ll put some meat on your bones before this voyage is over.”  

Another roar of approval answered him as the half-starved black men hurried to obey. The sail swelled as the wind swept over the waves with renewed strength, and the white crests danced along the wind’s path. Conan braced his feet against the rolling deck, took a deep breath, and stretched his mighty arms. He might no longer be King of Aquilonia, but he was still king of the blue sea.  

The Venturer sped southward like a living creature, her oars now pulled by free and willing hands. She had been changed from a peaceful merchant ship into a war galley, as much as such a transformation was possible. Men now sat at the benches with swords at their sides and gilded helmets on their curly heads. Shields hung along the rails, and bundles of spears, bows, and arrows decorated the mast. Even the elements seemed to favor Conan now; the broad purple sail swelled with a steady wind that held day after day, needing little help from the oars.  

But though Conan kept a lookout posted on the mast day and night, they never saw the long, low, black galley fleeing south ahead of them. Day after day the blue waters stretched empty before them, broken only by fishing boats that scattered like frightened birds at the sight of the shields hanging along the rail. The trading season was nearly over for the year, and they saw no other ships.  

When the lookout finally spotted a sail, it was to the north, not the south. Far on the horizon behind them appeared a racing galley with a full spread of purple sail. The black crew urged Conan to turn and attack it, but he shook his head. Somewhere to the south, a slim black galley was racing toward the ports of Stygia. That night, before darkness fell, the lookout’s last glimpse showed the racing galley still on the horizon, and at dawn it was still there, far behind, tiny in the distance. Conan wondered if it was following him, though he could think of no reason why. Still, he paid it little attention.  

Each day that carried him farther south filled him with greater impatience. Doubt never touched him. As surely as he believed in the rising and setting of the sun, he believed that a priest of Set had stolen the Heart of Ahriman. And where else would a priest of Set take it but to Stygia? The black crew sensed his urgency and worked harder than they ever had under the lash, though they didn’t know his goal. They expected a bloody campaign of raiding and plunder and were satisfied. The men from the southern islands knew no other trade, and the Kushites among them eagerly joined in the thought of looting their own people, with the cold indifference of their race. Blood ties meant little; a victorious leader and personal gain meant everything.  

Soon the coastline began to change. No longer did they sail past steep cliffs with blue hills behind them. Now the shore was a stretch of wide meadows barely above sea level, fading into the hazy distance. This was the coast of Shem, where there was no law except what each city-state could enforce for itself. Far to the east, Conan knew, the meadows gave way to desert, where no cities stood and nomadic tribes roamed freely.  

Still they rowed southward, past the unchanging view of meadowlands dotted with cities, until at last the scenery began to shift again. Clumps of tamarind appeared, palm groves grew thicker. The shoreline became more broken, a marching wall of green fronds and trees, and behind them rose bare, sandy hills. The river was the Styx, the true border of Stygia. Khemi was Stygia’s greatest port and, at that time, its most important city. The king lived in ancient Luxur, but the priesthood ruled in Khemi, though people said the heart of their dark religion lay far inland, in a mysterious, deserted city near the Styx. This river, springing from some unknown source far in the unexplored lands south of Stygia, ran north for a thousand miles before turning west for several hundred more, finally emptying into the ocean.  

The Venturer, showing no lights, slipped past the port in the night, and before dawn she lay anchored in a small bay a few miles south of the city. It was surrounded by marshland, a green tangle of mangroves, palms, and vines, crawling with crocodiles and snakes. Discovery was highly unlikely. Conan knew the place well; he had hidden there before, in his corsair days.  

As they glided silently past the city, whose great black bastions jutted out on the points of land enclosing the harbor, torches flared and smoldered redly, and the low thunder of drums reached their ears. The port was not crowded with ships like the harbors of Argos. The Stygians did not base their glory and power on fleets and ships. The Stygians were an ancient race, dark, mysterious, powerful, and ruthless. Long ago their rule had stretched far north of the Styx, beyond the meadowlands of Shem, into the fertile uplands now held by the peoples of Koth, Ophir, and Argos. Their borders had once touched those of ancient Acheron. If anyone had looked closely, they might have noticed his stride was a bit too confident, his posture too straight for a humble fisherman. In build, he wasn’t unlike the warrior caste of the Stygians, who were tall and muscular. Sun-browned, he was nearly as dark as many of them. His black hair, cut square and held by a copper band, added to the resemblance. What set him apart were the subtle differences in his movement, his foreign features, and his blue eyes.

Still, the cloak served as a good disguise, and he stayed in the shadows as much as possible, turning his head away when a native passed too close.

It was a dangerous game, and he knew he couldn’t keep up the deception for long. Khemi wasn’t like the Hyborian seaports, where people of every race mingled. The only foreigners here were black and Shemite slaves, and he looked like neither—though he resembled the Stygians more than he did them. Strangers weren’t welcome in Stygian cities; they were tolerated only as ambassadors or licensed traders, and even those weren’t allowed ashore after dark. Now, there were no Hyborian ships in the harbor at all. A strange unease hung over the city—a stirring of old ambitions, a whispering that only those who whispered could understand. Conan sensed it instinctively, his sharpened primitive instincts detecting the unrest around him.

If he were discovered, his fate would be horrifying. They would kill him simply for being a stranger; if they realized he was Amra, the pirate chief who had ravaged their coasts with sword and fire—an involuntary shiver ran through Conan’s broad shoulders. He did not fear human enemies, nor any death by blade or flame. But this was a dark land of sorcery and nameless horrors. People said Set the Old Serpent, long ago banished from the Hyborian races, still lingered in the shadows of the cryptic temples, and terrible, mysterious deeds were done in the night-shrouded shrines.

He had moved away from the waterfront streets with their wide steps leading down to the water and was entering the long, shadowy streets of the main part of the city. There was no scene like those of any Hyborian city—no blaze of lamps and torches, no brightly dressed people laughing and strolling along the pavements, no shops and stalls open wide to display their goods.

Here, the stalls were shut at dusk. The only lights along the streets were torches, flaring and smoking at wide intervals. The people walking the streets were few; they moved quickly and silently, and their numbers dwindled as the night grew later. Conan found the scene gloomy and unreal—the silence of the people, their secretive haste, the great black stone walls rising on both sides of the streets. There was a grim heaviness about Stygian architecture that felt overwhelming and oppressive.

Few lights showed anywhere except in the upper parts of the buildings. Conan knew most of the people were lying on the flat roofs, among the palms of artificial gardens under the stars. Somewhere, strange music murmured faintly. But the people still walking the streets on foot were commoners—slaves, merchants, prostitutes, laborers—and they grew fewer as he went on. He was heading toward the temple of Set, where he knew he was likely to find the priest he sought. He believed he would recognize Thutothmes if he saw him, though his only glimpse had been in the half-darkness of the Messantian alley. He was certain the man he had seen there had been the priest. Only sorcerers high in the ranks of the dreadful Black Ring possessed the power of the black hand that killed by touch; and only such a man would dare to defy Thoth-Amon, whom the western world knew only as a figure of terror and legend.

The Cimmerian recoiled, remembering the tales he had heard—serpents were sacred to Set, god of Stygia, who was said to be a serpent himself. Monsters like this were kept in the temples of Set, and when they grew hungry, they were allowed to crawl into the streets to take whatever prey they desired. Their ghastly feasts were considered sacrifices to the scaly god.

The Stygians within Conan’s sight dropped to their knees, men and women alike, passively awaiting their fate. One of them the great serpent would choose, wrapping it in scaly coils, crushing it to a red pulp, and swallowing it as a rat-snake swallows a mouse. The others would live. That was the will of the gods.

But it wasn’t Conan’s will. The python glided toward him, probably drawn by the fact that he was the only person still standing. Gripping his great knife beneath his cloak, Conan hoped the slimy creature would pass him by. But it stopped in front of him and reared up horribly in the flickering torchlight, its forked tongue darting in and out, its cold eyes gleaming with the ancient cruelty of the serpent race. Its neck arched, but before it could strike, Conan whipped his knife from under his cloak and struck like lightning. The broad blade split the wedge-shaped head and cut deep into the thick neck.

Conan yanked his knife free and leapt back as the massive body twisted, coiled, and lashed violently in its death throes. For a moment he stood, staring in grim fascination, the only sound the heavy thud and swish of the snake’s tail against the stones.

Then from the stunned worshippers came a terrible cry: “Blasphemer! He has killed the sacred son of Set! Kill him! Kill! Kill!”

Stones whistled past him as the crazed Stygians rushed at him, screaming hysterically. From all sides, others poured out of their houses and joined the cry. Cursing, Conan spun around and darted into the black mouth of an alley. He heard the slap of bare feet on the paving stones behind him as he ran, guided more by instinct than sight, the walls echoing with the vengeful shouts of his pursuers. Then his left hand brushed against a break in the wall, and he turned sharply into another, narrower alley. On both sides rose sheer black stone walls. High above, he could see a thin strip of stars. These towering walls, he knew, belonged to temples. Behind him, he heard the mob sweep past the dark opening, still howling. Their shouts grew fainter and faded away. They had missed the smaller alley and run straight on into the darkness. He kept moving forward too, though the thought of meeting another of Set’s “sons” in the dark made him shudder.

Then, somewhere ahead, he noticed a faint moving glow, like that of a crawling firefly. He stopped, pressed himself against the wall, and gripped his knife. He knew what it was—a man carrying a torch. Now it was close enough for him to see the dark hand holding it and the dim oval of a dark face. A few more steps and the man would surely see him. Conan crouched low, tense as a tiger. The torch stopped moving. A door was briefly outlined in the glow as the torchbearer fumbled with it. Then it opened, the tall figure slipped inside, and darkness swallowed the alley again. There was something furtive and sinister about that shadowy figure sneaking through the door in the dark—perhaps a priest returning from some secret errand.

Conan moved toward the door. If one man had come down this alley with a torch, others might come at any moment. To go back the way he came could mean running straight into the mob still hunting him. They might return at any time, find the narrow alley, and come charging down it. The high, smooth walls hemmed him in, and he longed for escape, even if it meant entering an unknown building.

The heavy bronze door wasn’t locked. It opened under his fingers, and he peered through the crack. He saw a large square chamber of massive black stone. A torch burned low in a wall niche. The room was empty. He slipped through the lacquered door and closed it behind him.

His sandaled feet made no sound as he crossed the black marble floor. A teak door stood partly open, and gliding through it, knife in hand, he entered a vast, dim, shadowy space whose high ceiling was only a suggestion of darkness far above, where the black walls rose upward. All around, black-arched doorways opened into the great silent hall. It was lit by strange bronze lamps that gave off a dim, eerie glow. On the far side of the hall, a broad black marble staircase without a railing climbed upward into the gloom, and above him, on all sides, dim galleries hung like black stone ledges.

Conan shivered; he was inside a temple of some Stygian god—if not Set himself, then one nearly as grim. And the shrine was not empty. In the center of the great hall stood a black stone altar, massive and plain, without carvings or decoration, and upon it lay one of the great sacred serpents, its iridescent scales shimmering in the lamplight. It didn’t move, and Conan remembered tales that the priests kept these creatures drugged part of the time. The Cimmerian took a cautious step out from the doorway, then suddenly shrank back—not into the room he had just left, but into a velvet-curtained alcove. He had heard a soft footstep nearby.

From one of the black arches came a tall, powerfully built man wearing sandals and a silk loincloth, with a wide cloak flowing from his shoulders. His face and head were hidden by a huge mask, half-beast and half-human, with a crest from which a cluster of ostrich feathers waved.  

In certain rituals, the Stygian priests wore masks. Conan hoped the man wouldn’t notice him, but some instinct alerted the Stygian. He suddenly turned away from his intended path, which seemed to be the staircase, and walked straight toward the alcove. As he yanked aside the velvet curtain, a hand shot out from the shadows, crushed the cry in his throat, dragged him headfirst into the recess, and the knife struck home.  

Conan’s next move was the obvious one dictated by reason. He removed the grinning mask and pulled it over his own head. He threw the fisherman’s cloak over the priest’s body, hid it behind the curtains, and wrapped the priest’s mantle around his own broad shoulders. Fate had handed him a disguise. All of Khemi was probably searching for the blasphemer who had dared to defend himself against a sacred serpent—but who would think to look for him under the mask of a priest?  

He strode confidently out of the alcove and headed toward one of the arched doorways at random. But he had taken no more than a dozen steps when he spun around, every sense alert for danger.  

A group of masked figures was coming down the stairs, dressed exactly as he was. He froze, caught out in the open, and stood still, trusting his disguise, though cold sweat gathered on his forehead and the backs of his hands. No one spoke. Like ghosts, they descended into the great hall and moved past him toward a black archway. The leader carried a black staff topped with a grinning white skull, and Conan realized it was one of those ritual processions so mysterious to outsiders, yet deeply—and often darkly—woven into Stygian religion. The last figure turned his head slightly toward the motionless Cimmerian, as if expecting him to follow. To refuse to do what was clearly expected would instantly draw suspicion. Conan fell in behind the last man and matched his stride to their slow, measured pace.  

They moved through a long, dark, vaulted corridor where Conan noticed uneasily that the skull on the staff glowed with a ghostly light. A wild, instinctive panic surged through him, urging him to draw his knife and slash at these eerie figures, to flee madly from the grim, shadowed temple. But he forced himself to stay calm, fighting down the vague monstrous fears that stirred in the back of his mind and filled the darkness with imagined horrors. At last he barely suppressed a sigh of relief as they passed through a huge double door, three times the height of a man, and stepped out into the starlight.  

Conan wondered if he dared slip away into some dark alley, but he hesitated, uncertain. Down the long, dim street they padded silently, while the few people they met turned their heads away and fled. The procession kept well away from the walls; to suddenly dart into one of the alleys would draw too much attention. While he fumed inwardly and cursed his luck, they reached a low-arched gate in the southern wall and passed through it. Ahead and around them lay clusters of low, flat-roofed mud houses and palm groves, shadowy under the stars. Now, if ever, thought Conan, was the time to escape his silent companions.  

But as soon as they were beyond the gate, those companions were silent no longer. They began muttering excitedly among themselves. Their slow, ritual pace was abandoned, the staff with its skull was tucked carelessly under the leader’s arm, and the whole group broke formation and hurried forward. Conan hurried with them, for in their low conversation he caught a word that electrified him. The word was: “Thutothmes!”  

Conan stared intently at his masked companions. One of them was Thutothmes, or else they were heading to meet him. And he realized where they were going when, beyond the palms, he saw a black triangular shape rising against the shadowy sky.  

They passed through the belt of huts and groves, and if anyone saw them, he made sure not to show himself. The huts were dark. Behind them, the black towers of Khemi loomed against the starry sky, their reflections shimmering in the harbor waters; ahead stretched the dim desert, and somewhere a jackal yelped. The soft tread of the silent priests made no sound in the sand. They might have been ghosts moving toward that massive pyramid rising from the desert gloom. The whole land lay silent and still.  

Conan’s heart beat faster as he looked at the grim black wedge outlined against the stars. His eagerness to confront Thutothmes—whatever that meeting might bring—was mixed with a deep unease. No man could approach one of those dark stone pyramids without feeling dread. Their very name was a symbol of horror among the northern peoples, and legends whispered that the Stygians had not built them at all; that they had already stood there when the dark-skinned race first came to the land of the great river, in ages beyond memory.  

As they neared the pyramid, he saw a faint glow near its base, which soon revealed itself as a doorway, flanked by stone lions with women’s heads—cryptic, unreadable, nightmares carved in stone. The leader of the group went straight toward the doorway, in the deep shadow of which Conan saw a dim figure standing.  

The leader paused briefly beside this shadowy figure, then vanished into the dark interior, and one by one the others followed. As each masked priest passed through the gloomy entrance, he was stopped for a moment by the mysterious guardian, and something passed between them—a word or gesture Conan couldn’t make out. Seeing this, the Cimmerian deliberately hung back, pretending to adjust the strap of his sandal. Only when the last of the masked figures had disappeared did he straighten up and approach the doorway.  

He wondered uneasily whether the temple guardian was even human, remembering some of the tales he had heard. But his doubts were soon dispelled. A dim bronze lamp burning just inside the door lit a long, narrow corridor stretching into darkness, and a man stood silent at its entrance, wrapped in a wide black cloak. No one else was in sight. Clearly, the masked priests had already gone down the corridor.

Over the cloak that covered the lower part of his face, the Stygian’s sharp eyes studied Conan closely. With his left hand he made a strange gesture. On a hunch, Conan copied it. But apparently another gesture was expected; the Stygian’s right hand flashed from beneath his cloak, steel glinting, and his murderous thrust would have pierced the heart of an ordinary man.  

But he was facing someone whose muscles were trained to the speed of a jungle cat. Even as the dagger gleamed in the dim light, Conan caught the dark wrist and smashed his clenched right fist into the Stygian’s jaw. The man’s head snapped back against the stone wall with a dull crunch that told of a shattered skull.  

For a moment Conan stood over him, listening intently. The cresset burned low, throwing vague shadows around the doorway. Nothing stirred in the darkness beyond, though far away and below, he thought he heard the faint, muffled sound of a gong.  

He bent down and dragged the body behind the great bronze door, which stood wide open inward, and then the Cimmerian moved cautiously but quickly down the corridor, toward whatever fate awaited him—something he didn’t even try to guess.  

He hadn’t gone far before he stopped, uncertain. The corridor split into two branches, and he had no way of knowing which path the masked priests had taken. Taking a chance, he chose the left. The floor sloped slightly downward and was worn smooth by many feet. Here and there a dim cresset cast a faint, eerie twilight. Conan wondered uneasily for what purpose these massive structures had been built, and in what forgotten age. This was an ancient land—so ancient that no one knew how many ages the black temples of Stygia had stood beneath the stars.  

Narrow black arches opened occasionally to the right and left, but he kept to the main corridor, though a growing conviction told him he had taken the wrong path. Even with their head start, he should have caught up with the priests by now. He was becoming uneasy. The silence felt almost solid, and yet he sensed he wasn’t alone. More than once, as he passed a dark archway, he felt the unseen gaze of eyes fixed on him. He paused, half tempted to turn back to where the corridor had first divided. Suddenly he spun around, knife raised, every nerve alert.  

A girl stood at the entrance of a smaller tunnel, staring straight at him. Her ivory skin marked her as a Stygian of some ancient noble line, and like all such women she was tall, graceful, and voluptuous, her hair a great mass of black waves, with a sparkling ruby gleaming among them. Except for her velvet sandals and the broad, jewel-studded belt around her supple waist, she was completely nude.  

“What are you doing here?” she demanded.  

To answer would reveal that he was a foreigner. He stayed silent, a grim, dark figure in the hideous mask with its floating plumes. His sharp gaze searched the shadows behind her but saw nothing. Still, there could be armed men within her call.  

She stepped toward him, apparently unafraid, though clearly suspicious.  

“You’re not a priest,” she said. “You’re a warrior. Even with that mask, it’s obvious. There’s as much difference between you and a priest as between a man and a woman. By Set!” she exclaimed suddenly, her eyes widening. “I don’t believe you’re even a Stygian!”  

With a movement too fast to follow, his hand closed around her smooth throat, lightly, almost like a caress.  

“Not a sound from you,” he muttered.  

Her smooth ivory skin was cold as marble, yet there was no fear in the wide, dark, incredible eyes that looked at him.  

“Don’t be afraid,” she said calmly. “I won’t betray you. But are you insane, coming here—a stranger and foreigner—into the forbidden temple of Set?”  

“I’m looking for the priest Thutothmes,” he said. “Is he in this temple?”  

“Why do you seek him?” she countered.  

“He has something of mine that was stolen.”  

“I’ll take you to him,” she offered so quickly that his suspicions flared.  

“Don’t play games with me, girl,” he growled.  

“I’m not playing. I have no love for Thutothmes.”  

He hesitated, then decided; after all, he was as much in her power as she was in his.  

“Walk beside me,” he ordered, shifting his grip from her throat to her wrist. “But walk carefully. If you make one suspicious move—”  

She led him down the sloping corridor, farther and farther, until there were no more cressets, and he had to feel his way in the dark, aware of her presence more by touch and instinct than by sight. Once, when he spoke to her, she turned her head toward him, and he was startled to see her eyes glowing like golden fire in the darkness. Vague doubts and monstrous suspicions haunted his mind, but he followed her through a twisting maze of black corridors that confused even his primitive sense of direction. He silently cursed himself for a fool, letting himself be led into this black lair of mystery—but it was too late to turn back now. Again he sensed life and movement in the darkness around him, felt danger and hunger lurking impatiently in the shadows. Unless his ears deceived him, he caught a faint sliding sound that stopped and retreated at a muttered command from the girl.  

At last she led him into a chamber lit by a strange seven-branched candelabrum, where black candles burned with an eerie glow. He knew they were deep beneath the earth. The room was square, with walls and ceiling of polished black marble, furnished in the style of ancient Stygia; there was an ebony couch covered with black velvet, and on a black stone platform lay a carved mummy case.  

Conan stood waiting, alert, watching the various black arches that opened into the chamber. But the girl made no move to go farther. Stretching out on the couch with feline grace, she laced her fingers behind her sleek head and looked at him from under long, drooping lashes.  

“Well?” he demanded impatiently. “What are you doing? Where’s Thutothmes?”  

“There’s no hurry,” she said lazily. “What’s an hour—or a day, or a year, or even a century, for that matter? Take off your mask. Let me see your face.”  

With a grunt of irritation, Conan pulled off the heavy headpiece, and the girl nodded approvingly as she studied his dark, scarred face and blazing eyes.

“There’s great strength in you—real strength; you could strangle an ox.”  

He shifted uneasily, suspicion growing in his eyes. With one hand on his sword hilt, he peered into the shadowy arches.  

“If you’ve led me into a trap,” he said, “you won’t live long enough to enjoy it. Are you going to get up from that couch and do what you promised, or do I have to—”  

His voice faded. He was staring at the mummy case, its lid carved with the face of the one inside—ivory features rendered with the eerie realism of a lost art. There was something disturbingly familiar about that carved face, and with a jolt he realized what it was: the face looked almost exactly like the girl lounging on the black couch. She could have been the model for it, but he knew the carving was ancient—centuries old. Strange hieroglyphs were scrawled across the lacquered lid, and digging through scraps of knowledge gathered over a lifetime of wandering, he slowly sounded them out and said aloud, “Akivasha!”  

“You’ve heard of Princess Akivasha?” asked the girl on the couch.  

“Who hasn’t?” he grunted. The name of that ancient, wicked, beautiful princess still lived across the world in song and legend, though ten thousand years had passed since the daughter of Tuthamon had feasted in the black halls of old Luxur.  

“Her only sin was that she loved life and everything it meant,” said the Stygian girl. “To win life, she courted death. She couldn’t bear the thought of growing old, shriveled, and worn, dying like a hag. She wooed Darkness like a lover, and his gift was life—life that, not being mortal life, can never age or fade. She went into the shadows to cheat age and death—”  

Conan glared at her, his eyes suddenly narrowing to burning slits. He spun around and ripped the lid off the sarcophagus. It was empty. Behind him, the girl’s laughter rang out, freezing the blood in his veins. He whirled back toward her, the short hairs on his neck bristling.  

“You are Akivasha!” he growled.  

She laughed, tossing back her shining hair, and spread her arms in a sensual gesture.  

“I am Akivasha! I am the woman who never died, who never grew old! Fools say the gods lifted me from the earth in the full bloom of my youth and beauty to reign forever in some heavenly realm! No—it’s in the shadows that mortals find immortality! Ten thousand years ago I died to live forever! Give me your lips, strong man!”  

She rose gracefully, came to him, stood on tiptoe, and threw her arms around his massive neck. Scowling down into her upturned, beautiful face, he felt both a terrible fascination and a cold dread.  

“Love me!” she whispered, head thrown back, eyes closed, lips parted. “Give me your blood to renew my youth and keep my eternal life! I’ll make you immortal too! I’ll teach you the wisdom of the ages, the secrets that have survived through endless time in the darkness beneath these temples. I’ll make you king of the shadowy host that revels among the tombs of the ancients when night covers the desert and bats cross the moon. I’m tired of priests and magicians, and of captive girls dragged screaming through the gates of death. I want a man. Love me, barbarian!”  

She pressed her dark head against his broad chest, and he felt a sharp sting at the base of his throat. Cursing, he tore her away and hurled her sprawling across the couch.  

“Damn vampire!” Blood trickled from a small wound on his neck.  

She reared up on the couch like a serpent ready to strike, the golden fires of hell blazing in her wide eyes. Her lips drew back, showing sharp white teeth.  

“Fool!” she screamed. “Do you think you can escape me? You’ll live and die in darkness! I’ve brought you far below the temple. You’ll never find your way out alone. You can’t fight your way through those who guard the tunnels. Without my protection, the sons of Set would have devoured you long ago. Fool, I’ll drink your blood yet!”  

“Stay back or I’ll cut you in half,” he snarled, his skin crawling with disgust. “You may be immortal, but steel will still tear you apart.”  

As he backed toward the archway he’d entered through, the light went out. Every candle was snuffed at once, though he couldn’t see how—it wasn’t Akivasha’s doing. Her laughter rose behind him, mocking and sweet as poison, like the music of hell itself. Sweating, nearly panicked, he groped through the darkness for the archway. His fingers found an opening and he plunged through it. Whether it was the same arch he’d entered by, he didn’t know or care. His only thought was to escape that cursed chamber where the beautiful, hideous, undead creature had lingered for countless centuries.  

His wandering through the black, twisting tunnels was a nightmare of sweat and terror. Behind and around him he heard faint slithering and gliding sounds, and once the echo of that sweet, infernal laughter he’d heard in Akivasha’s chamber. He slashed wildly at noises and movements, real or imagined, in the darkness near him, and once his sword cut through something soft and thin that might have been cobwebs. He had the dreadful sense that he was being toyed with, lured deeper and deeper into endless night before being torn apart by demonic claws and fangs.  

And through his fear ran a sickening disgust at what he had discovered. The legend of Akivasha was ancient, and even in the evil tales told of her there had always been a thread of beauty and idealism—of eternal youth. To countless dreamers, poets, and lovers she had been not just the wicked princess of Stygian myth, but the symbol of everlasting youth and beauty, shining forever in some divine realm. And this was the truth—the vile, twisted reality. This foul corruption was what that “eternal life” truly meant. Alongside his physical revulsion came the sense of a shattered dream of human worship, its glittering gold revealed as slime and cosmic decay. A wave of hopelessness swept over him—a dim fear that all human dreams and idols were lies.

And now he knew his ears weren’t deceiving him. He was being followed, and whoever or whatever was behind him was getting closer. In the darkness came shuffling and sliding sounds that no human feet could have made—nor the feet of any normal animal. Maybe the underworld had its own kind of beasts. They were behind him. He turned to face them, though he could see nothing, and slowly backed away. Then the sounds stopped, even before he turned his head and saw, somewhere far down the long corridor, a faint glow of light.

19 - IN THE HALL OF THE DEAD  
In the Hall of the Dead

Conan moved carefully toward the light he had seen, listening intently behind him, but there was no more sound of pursuit, though he could feel the darkness alive with unseen awareness.

The glow wasn’t still; it moved, bobbing strangely along. Then he saw what caused it. The tunnel he was in crossed another, wider corridor some distance ahead. Along that corridor came a bizarre procession—four tall, thin men in black hooded robes, leaning on staffs. The one in front held a torch high above his head—a torch that burned with a strange, steady light. Like ghosts they passed across his narrow field of vision and vanished, leaving only a fading glow to mark their passing. Their appearance was indescribably eerie. They weren’t Stygians, nor like any people Conan had ever seen. He doubted they were even human. They looked like black phantoms, stalking ghoulishly through the haunted tunnels.

But his situation couldn’t get any worse. Before the inhuman feet behind him could start slithering closer again as the distant light faded, Conan was already running down the corridor. He plunged into the other tunnel and saw, far ahead, small in the distance, the strange procession moving within the glowing sphere of light. He followed silently after them, then suddenly pressed himself against the wall as he saw them stop and gather together as if discussing something. They turned as if to come back the way they had come, and he slipped into the nearest archway. Feeling his way in the darkness—by now he was so used to it that he could almost see through it—he found that the tunnel curved and twisted. He moved back beyond the first bend so that the strangers’ light wouldn’t fall on him as they passed.

But as he stood there, he became aware of a low hum of sound from somewhere behind him, like the murmur of human voices. Moving down the corridor toward it, he confirmed his suspicion. Abandoning his plan to follow the ghostly travelers to wherever they were going, he turned toward the voices instead.

Soon he saw a faint glimmer of light ahead, and turning into the corridor it came from, he saw a wide arch glowing dimly at the far end. On his left, a narrow stone staircase led upward, and instinctively he turned and climbed it. The voices he heard came from beyond that glowing arch.

The sounds faded beneath him as he climbed, and soon he emerged through a low arched doorway into a vast open space filled with an eerie light.

He was standing on a shadowy balcony overlooking a broad, dimly lit hall of immense size. It was a hall of the dead, seen only by the silent priests of Stygia. Along the black walls rose tier upon tier of carved and painted sarcophagi. Each stood in a niche in the dark stone, and the tiers climbed higher and higher until they were lost in the shadows above. Thousands of carved faces stared down impassively at the group in the center of the hall, making them seem small and meaningless before that vast host of the dead.

There were ten in the group, all priests, and though they had removed their masks, Conan recognized them as the same priests he had accompanied to the pyramid. They stood before a tall, hawk-faced man beside a black altar on which lay a mummy wrapped in rotting bandages. The altar seemed to stand in the heart of a living fire that pulsed and shimmered, dripping flakes of trembling golden flame onto the black stones around it. This dazzling light came from a large red jewel resting on the altar, and in its glow the faces of the priests looked pale and corpse-like. As Conan watched, he felt the weight of all the weary miles and sleepless nights of his long quest pressing down on him, and he trembled with the wild urge to rush among those silent priests, cut them down with his sword, and seize the red gem with desperate hands. But he forced himself to stay still, crouching in the shadow of the stone railing. A quick glance showed him a stairway leading down from the gallery into the hall, hugging the wall and half hidden in shadow. He peered into the dimness of the vast chamber, searching for other priests or worshipers, but saw only the group around the altar.

In that great emptiness, the voice of the man beside the altar echoed hollow and ghostly:

“…And so the word came southward. The night wind whispered it, the ravens croaked it as they flew, and the grim bats told it to the owls and the serpents that hide in ancient ruins. Werewolves and vampires knew, and the black-bodied demons that prowl by night. The sleeping Night of the World stirred and shook its heavy mane, and there began a throbbing of drums in deep darkness, and the echoes of strange cries frightened men who walked at dusk. For the Heart of Ahriman had returned to the world to fulfill its hidden destiny.

“Ask me not how I, Thutothmes of Khemi and the Night, heard the word before Thoth-Amon, who calls himself prince of all wizards. There are secrets not meant for ears such as yours, and Thoth-Amon is not the only master of the Black Ring.

“I knew, and I went to meet the Heart as it came southward. It was like a magnet drawing me without fail. From death to death it came, riding on a river of human blood. Blood feeds it; blood calls to it. Its power is greatest when there is blood on the hands that hold it, when it is taken by slaughter from its owner. Wherever it shines, blood is spilled and kingdoms fall, and the forces of nature are thrown into chaos.

“And here I stand, master of the Heart, and I have summoned you here in secret, you who are loyal to me, to share in the dark empire that will come. Tonight you shall witness the breaking of Thoth-Amon’s chains that enslave us, and the birth of an empire.”

Who am I, even I, Thutothmes, to know what powers lie hidden and dreaming in those crimson depths? It holds secrets forgotten for three thousand years. But I will learn them. These will tell me!  

He gestured toward the silent figures lining the hall.  

See how they sleep, staring through their carved masks! Kings, queens, generals, priests, sorcerers—the dynasties and nobility of Stygia for ten thousand years! The touch of the Heart will awaken them from their long sleep. For ages the Heart throbbed and pulsed in ancient Stygia. This was its home long before it journeyed to Acheron. The ancients knew its full power, and they will tell me when, by its magic, I bring them back to life to serve me.  

I will rouse them, awaken them, and learn their forgotten wisdom—the knowledge locked inside those withered skulls. With the lore of the dead, we will enslave the living! Yes, kings, generals, and wizards of old will be our helpers and our slaves. Who will stand against us?  

Look! This dried, shriveled thing on the altar was once Thothmekri, a high priest of Set, who died three thousand years ago. He was a master of the Black Ring. He knew of the Heart. He will tell us of its powers.  

Lifting the great jewel, the speaker placed it on the mummy’s withered chest and raised his hand to begin an incantation. But the spell was never finished. With his hand raised and his lips parted, he froze, staring past his acolytes. They turned to look in the same direction.  

Through the black arch of a doorway, four gaunt, black-robed figures entered the great hall. Their faces were dim yellow ovals beneath their hoods.  

Who are you? demanded Thutothmes, his voice as dangerous as a cobra’s hiss. Are you insane, to invade the holy shrine of Set?  

The tallest of the strangers spoke, his voice flat and toneless like a Khitan temple bell.  

We follow Conan of Aquilonia.  

He is not here, said Thutothmes, shaking back his mantle from his right hand in a menacing gesture, like a panther unsheathing its claws.  

You lie. He is in this temple. We tracked him from a corpse behind the bronze door of the outer gate, through a maze of corridors. We were following his twisting trail when we became aware of this gathering. We go now to take it up again. But first, give us the Heart of Ahriman.  

Death is the fate of madmen, murmured Thutothmes, moving closer to the speaker. His priests crept forward on silent feet, but the strangers seemed not to notice.  

Who can look upon it without desire? said the Khitan. In Khitai we have heard of it. It will give us power over the people who cast us out. Glory and wonder dream within its crimson depths. Give it to us before we kill you.  

A fierce cry rang out as a priest leaped forward, steel flashing. Before he could strike, a scaly staff darted out and touched his chest, and he fell like a dead man. In an instant the mummies were staring down upon a scene of blood and horror. Curved knives flashed and turned red, serpentine staffs darted in and out, and whenever they touched a man, he screamed and died.  

At the first blow, Conan had leaped to his feet and was racing down the stairs. By the time Conan’s pounding feet reached the floor, the fight was nearly over. Three of the Khitans lay dead, slashed to ribbons and disemboweled, but of the Stygians only Thutothmes still stood.

Conan realized he had been lucky to return from the vampire’s chamber by a different route than the one he had taken there. Otherwise, he would have run straight into these yellow fiends instead of spotting them from afar as they tracked his scent like human bloodhounds, using whatever strange power they possessed.

That doesn’t matter; the long chase ends here.

It was a debt to pay, said the Khitan. To you, who are about to die, I will not withhold the truth. We were vassals of the king of Aquilonia, Valerius. The staff shot out like a viper’s strike, but Conan’s knife was faster. The staff fell in two twitching pieces, there was another flash of sharp steel like a streak of lightning, and the Khitan’s head rolled across the floor.

The jewel’s glow lit the black tunnels like a magic lamp, dripping golden fire. Once Conan caught a glimpse of pale flesh in the shadows—he thought he saw the vampire Akivasha shrinking away from the jewel’s light; and with her, other, less human shapes scurried or lurched back into the darkness.  

The dead man strode straight ahead, never looking to either side, his pace steady and relentless as doom itself. Cold sweat covered Conan’s skin. Icy doubts gnawed at him. How could he know this dreadful being from the past was leading him to freedom? But he knew that alone he could never find his way through this cursed maze of corridors and tunnels. He followed his ghastly guide through the blackness that surrounded them, filled with lurking horrors that shrank from the blinding glow of the Heart.  

Then the bronze doorway appeared before him, and Conan felt the night wind blowing across the desert. He saw the stars, and the starlit sands stretching out, the great black shadow of the pyramid streaming across them. Thothmekri silently pointed toward the desert, then turned and walked soundlessly back into the darkness. Conan watched that silent figure fade into the blackness, moving on quiet, inevitable feet like one returning to eternal sleep.  

With a curse, the Cimmerian leapt from the doorway and ran into the desert as if chased by demons. He didn’t look back at the pyramid or at the dark towers of Khemi looming faintly across the sands. He ran south toward the coast, driven by uncontrollable panic. The violent effort cleared his mind of dark cobwebs; the clean desert wind blew the nightmares from his soul, and his horror turned into wild exhilaration before the desert gave way to swampy thickets, beyond which he saw the black water and the Venturer anchored there.  

He crashed through the undergrowth, wading hip-deep through the marshes; then dove headfirst into the deep water, not caring about sharks or crocodiles, and swam to the galley. He was climbing up the chain onto the deck, dripping and triumphant, before the watch even saw him.  

“Wake up, you dogs!” roared Conan, knocking aside the spear the startled lookout thrust at his chest. “Raise the anchor! Set the sails! Give that fisherman a helmet full of gold and put him ashore! Dawn’s coming soon, and before sunrise we’ll be racing for the nearest port in Zingara!”  

He swung the great jewel above his head, and it scattered bursts of light that splashed the deck with golden fire.  

20 - OUT OF THE DUST SHALL ACHERON ARISE  
Out of the Dust Shall Acheron Arise  

Winter had passed from Aquilonia. Leaves sprouted on the trees, and fresh grass smiled under the touch of warm southern winds. But many fields lay empty and untended, and many blackened heaps of ashes marked where proud villas or thriving towns had once stood. Wolves prowled openly along the grass-covered roads, and bands of gaunt, masterless men crept through the forests. Only in Tarantia was there feasting, wealth, and splendor.

His tax collectors crushed rich and poor alike; the wealth of a plundered kingdom poured into Tarantia, which now looked less like a royal capital and more like a garrison of conquerors in a conquered land. Its merchants grew rich, but their prosperity was fragile; no one knew when he might be accused of treason on false charges, his property seized, and he himself thrown into prison or sent to the executioner’s block.  

Valerius made no effort to win over his people. He held power through Nemedian soldiers and desperate mercenaries. He knew he was Amalric’s puppet. He knew he ruled only by the Nemedian’s permission. He knew he could never hope to unite Aquilonia under his rule or throw off his masters’ yoke, for the outer provinces would resist him to the last drop of blood. And the Nemedians themselves would throw him from the throne if he tried to strengthen his kingdom. He was trapped in his own snare. The bitterness of defeated pride ate at his soul, and he plunged into a life of debauchery, living only for the moment, caring nothing for tomorrow.  

Yet there was cunning in his madness, so deep that even Amalric didn’t suspect it. Maybe the wild, chaotic years of exile had bred in him a bitterness beyond normal understanding. Maybe his hatred of his current position had twisted that bitterness into a kind of insanity. Whatever the cause, he lived for one purpose—to bring ruin to everyone connected with him.

He knew his rule would end the moment he had fulfilled Amalric’s purpose. He also knew that as long as he kept oppressing his own kingdom, the Nemedian would allow him to stay on the throne, because Amalric wanted to crush Aquilonia into complete submission, destroy its last trace of independence, and then seize it for himself. He planned to rebuild it in his own way with his immense wealth and use its people and resources to take the crown of Nemedia from Tarascus. Amalric’s ultimate ambition was to sit on an emperor’s throne, and Valerius knew it. Valerius didn’t know whether Tarascus suspected this, but he did know that the king of Nemedia approved of his ruthless actions. Tarascus hated Aquilonia with a hatred born from old wars. He wanted nothing more than the destruction of the western kingdom.  

And Valerius intended to ruin the land so completely that not even Amalric’s wealth could ever rebuild it. He hated the baron just as much as he hated the Aquilonians, and he only hoped to live long enough to see the day when Aquilonia lay in total ruin, and Tarascus and Amalric were locked in a hopeless civil war that would destroy Nemedia as well.  

He believed that conquering the still-defiant provinces of Gunderland, Poitain, and the Bossonian Marches would mark the end of his reign. By then, he would have served Amalric’s purpose and could be discarded. So he delayed the conquest of those provinces, limiting his actions to pointless raids and skirmishes, meeting Amalric’s demands for progress with all kinds of convincing excuses and delays.  

His life became a blur of feasts and wild debauchery. He filled his palace with the most beautiful girls in the kingdom, whether they came willingly or not. He cursed the gods and sprawled drunkenly on the banquet hall floor wearing the golden crown, staining his royal purple robes with spilled wine. In fits of bloodlust, he decorated the gallows in the market square with hanging corpses, fed the headsmen’s axes, and sent his Nemedian horsemen thundering across the land to plunder and burn. Driven to madness, the country was in constant turmoil, with desperate revolts savagely crushed. Valerius plundered, raped, looted, and destroyed until even Amalric protested, warning him that he would bankrupt the kingdom beyond recovery—unaware that this was exactly what Valerius intended.

21 - DRUMS OF PERIL  

The news of war was confirmed when the army of Poitain, ten thousand strong, marched through the southern passes with banners flying and steel gleaming. Apparently, he had turned west through the wild, sparsely settled hill country and entered the Bossonian marches, gathering recruits as he went. Amalric and Valerius, with their army of Nemedians, Aquilonian traitors, and savage mercenaries, moved through the land in frustrated fury, searching for an enemy who never appeared.

Amalric found it impossible to get anything more than vague, general reports about Conan’s movements. His scouting parties often rode out and never came back, and it wasn’t unusual to find a spy nailed to an oak tree. The countryside was rising up the way peasants do—savagely, murderously, and in secret. All Amalric knew for sure was that a large force of Gundermen and northern Bossonians was somewhere north of him, beyond the Shirki River, and that Conan, with a smaller force of Poitanians and southern Bossonians, was somewhere to the southwest.  

He began to fear that if he and Valerius advanced any farther into the wild country, Conan might slip past them entirely, circle around, and invade the central provinces behind them. Amalric pulled back from the Shirki valley and set up camp on a plain a day’s ride from Tanasul. There he waited. Tarascus stayed at Galparan, afraid that Conan’s maneuvers were meant to lure him south and let the Gundermen cross into the kingdom from the north.  

* * * * *  

Xaltotun came to Amalric’s camp in his chariot drawn by those eerie, tireless horses, and entered Amalric’s tent, where the baron was conferring with Valerius over a map spread out on an ivory camp table.  

Xaltotun crumpled the map and tossed it aside.  

“What your scouts can’t find out,” he said, “my spies tell me—though their reports are strangely blurred and incomplete, as if unseen powers were working against me.  

“Conan is advancing along the Shirki River with ten thousand Poitanians, three thousand southern Bossonians, and barons from the west and south with their retainers, numbering about five thousand. They’ve made contact through secret communications used by those cursed priests of Asura, who seem to be working against me—and whom I’ll feed to a serpent when the battle’s done, I swear it by Set!

By the time he arrives, I’ll have cut Conan off from the crossing and destroyed him. Then our combined forces can cross the river and deal with the Gundermen.”  

Xaltotun shook his head impatiently.  

“That plan might work against anyone but Conan. But your twenty-five thousand men aren’t enough to destroy his eighteen thousand before the Gundermen arrive. They’ll fight like wounded panthers. And what if the Gundermen arrive while the battle’s raging? You’ll be trapped between two armies and destroyed before Tarascus can reach you. He’ll get to Tanasul too late to help.”  

“Then what do you suggest?” demanded Amalric.  

“Move with your full strength against Conan,” said the man from Acheron. “Send a rider to tell Tarascus to join us here. We’ll wait for him, then march together to Tanasul.”  

“But if we wait,” protested Amalric, “Conan will cross the river and join the Gundermen.”  

“Conan will not cross the river,” said Xaltotun.  

Amalric’s head snapped up, and he stared into those dark, unreadable eyes.  

“What do you mean?”  

“Suppose there were torrential rains far to the north, at the source of the Shirki. Suppose the river flooded so badly that the crossing at Tanasul became impassable. Then we could bring up our entire force at our leisure, trap Conan on this side of the river, and crush him. And when the flood subsides—which I think it would the next day—we could cross the river and destroy the Gundermen. That way, we could use our full strength against each smaller army in turn.”  

Valerius laughed, as he always did at the thought of anyone’s ruin, friend or foe, and ran a restless hand through his unruly yellow hair. Amalric stared at the man from Acheron with a mix of fear and admiration.

“If we caught Conan in the Shirki Valley, with the hill ridges on his right and the river in flood on his left,” he admitted, “with our full force we could wipe him out completely. Do you think—are you sure—do you believe such rains will fall?”

“I’m going to my tent,” said Xaltotun, standing up. “Necromancy isn’t done with a wave of the hand. Send a rider to Tarascus. And let no one come near my tent.”

That last order was unnecessary. No man in that army could have been bribed to approach that strange black silk pavilion, whose door flaps were always tightly closed. No one but Xaltotun ever entered it, yet voices were often heard coming from within; its walls sometimes billowed though there was no wind, and eerie music drifted out. Sometimes, deep in the night, its silken walls glowed red from flickering flames inside, casting distorted shadows that moved back and forth.

Lying in his own tent that night, Amalric heard the steady rumble of a drum coming from Xaltotun’s tent; through the darkness it boomed on and on, and at times the Nemedian could have sworn he heard a deep, croaking voice mingling with the beat of the drum. He shuddered, for he knew that voice was not Xaltotun’s. The drum muttered and rolled like distant thunder, and before dawn Amalric, glancing from his tent, caught the red flicker of lightning far off on the northern horizon. Everywhere else the great stars blazed white, but the distant lightning flashed without pause, like the crimson gleam of firelight on a tiny turning blade.

At sunset the next day, Tarascus arrived with his army, dusty and exhausted from hard marching, the foot soldiers straggling hours behind the horsemen. They camped on the plain near Amalric’s camp, and at dawn the combined army moved westward.

A swarm of scouts rode ahead, and Amalric waited impatiently for them to return with word that the Poitanians were trapped beside a raging flood. But when the scouts met the column, they brought news that Conan had already crossed the river.

“What?” exclaimed Amalric. “Did he cross before the flood?”

“There was no flood,” said the scouts, confused. “Late last night he reached Tanasul and threw his army across.”

“No flood?” said Xaltotun, startled for the first time since Amalric had known him. “Impossible! There were heavy rains on the headwaters of the Shirki last night and the night before!”

“That may be, my lord,” said the scout. “It’s true the water was muddy, and the people of Tanasul said the river rose maybe a foot yesterday, but that wasn’t enough to stop Conan from crossing.”

Xaltotun’s magic had failed! The thought pounded in Amalric’s mind. His dread of this strange man from the past had grown steadily since that night in Belverus when he had seen a brown, shriveled mummy swell and change into a living man. And the death of Orastes had turned his lurking horror into active fear. In his heart he was grimly convinced that the man—or demon—was unbeatable. Yet now he had undeniable proof of his failure.

Still, even the greatest sorcerers might fail sometimes, thought the baron. In any case, he didn’t dare oppose the man from Acheron—not yet. Orastes was dead, writhing in some nameless hell only Mitra might know, and Amalric knew his sword would hardly succeed where the black wisdom of the renegade priest had failed. Whatever monstrous thing Xaltotun was planning lay in the uncertain future. Conan and his army were a present danger, one that Xaltotun’s sorcery might still be needed to overcome before the game was finished.

They reached Tanasul, a small fortified village where a ridge of rocks formed a natural bridge across the river, passable except in the worst floods. Scouts brought word that Conan had taken position in the Goralian Hills, which began to rise a few miles beyond the river. And just before sundown, the Gundermen had joined his camp.

Amalric looked at Xaltotun, who stood inscrutable and alien in the light of the flaring torches. Night had fallen.

“What now? Your magic has failed. Conan faces us with an army nearly as strong as ours, and he holds the better ground. We have two bad choices: stay here and wait for his attack, or fall back toward Tarantia and wait for reinforcements.”

“We’re finished if we wait,” said Xaltotun. “Cross the river and camp on the plain. We’ll attack at dawn.”

“But his position is too strong!” protested Amalric.

“Fool!” A burst of fury cracked through the wizard’s calm. “Have you forgotten Valkia? Because some obscure elemental force stopped the flood, do you think I’m powerless? I meant for your spears to destroy our enemies, but don’t fear—it will be my arts that crush their army. Conan is trapped. He’ll never see another sunset. Cross the river!”

They crossed by torchlight. The horses’ hooves clinked on the rocky bridge and splashed through the shallows. The torchlight glinted red on shields and breastplates, reflecting in the black water. The rock bridge was wide enough, but even so, it was past midnight before the army was camped on the plain beyond. Above them, they could see fires glowing red in the distance. Conan had turned to make his stand in the Goralian Hills, which had served more than once before as the last refuge of an Aquilonian king.

Amalric left his pavilion and paced restlessly through the camp. A strange glow flickered in Xaltotun’s tent, and from time to time a demonic cry tore through the silence, accompanied by the low, sinister rustling of a drum.

Amalric, his instincts sharpened by the night and the tension, felt that Xaltotun was facing more than physical opposition. Doubts about the wizard’s power gnawed at him. He looked up at the fires high above and his face hardened. He and his army were deep in enemy territory. Up there in those hills lurked thousands of wolfish figures whose hearts and souls had been stripped of everything but a raging hatred for their conquerors, a mad thirst for revenge. Defeat would mean annihilation, retreat through a land swarming with blood-crazed foes. And tomorrow he would have to throw his army against the fiercest warrior in the western world and his desperate horde. If Xaltotun failed them now—

Half a dozen armored soldiers stepped out from the shadows. The firelight gleamed on their breastplates and helmet crests. Between them, they half led, half dragged a gaunt figure dressed in torn rags.  

Saluting, they said, “My lord, this man came to the outposts and said he wanted to speak with King Valerius. He’s an Aquilonian.”  

He looked more like a wolf—a wolf scarred by traps. Old wounds from shackles showed on his wrists and ankles. A large brand, the mark of a hot iron, marred his face. His eyes glared wildly through the tangle of his matted hair as he crouched before the baron.  

“Who are you, you filthy dog?” demanded the Nemedian.  

“Call me Tiberias,” the man replied, his teeth clicking in an involuntary spasm. “I’ve come to tell you how to catch Conan.”  

“A traitor, huh?” rumbled the baron.  

“People say you have gold,” the man muttered, shivering under his rags. “Give me some! Give me gold and I’ll show you how to defeat the king!” His eyes were wide and glassy, his outstretched hands trembling like claws.  

Amalric shrugged in disgust. But no tool was too low for his use.  

“If you’re telling the truth, you’ll have more gold than you can carry,” he said. “If you’re lying or spying, I’ll have you crucified upside down. Bring him along.”  

In Valerius’s tent, the baron pointed to the man crouching and shivering before them, clutching his rags around him.  

“He says he knows a way to help us tomorrow. We’ll need help if Xaltotun’s plan doesn’t work any better than it has so far. Speak, dog.”  

The man’s body twisted in strange spasms. His words came out in a stumbling rush:  

“Conan’s camp is at the head of the Valley of Lions. It’s shaped like a fan, with steep hills on both sides. If you attack him tomorrow, you’ll have to march straight up the valley. You can’t climb the hills on either side. But if King Valerius will accept my service, I’ll guide him through the hills and show him how to come up behind King Conan. But if we’re going to do it, we have to start soon. It’s many hours of riding—you have to go miles west, then miles north, then turn east and come into the Valley of Lions from behind, like the Gundermen did.”  

Amalric hesitated, tugging at his chin. In these chaotic times, it wasn’t unusual to find men willing to sell their souls for a few gold coins.  

“If you lead me astray, you’ll die,” said Valerius. “You understand that, don’t you?”  

The man shivered, but his wide eyes didn’t waver.  

“If I betray you, kill me!”  

“Conan won’t dare split his forces,” mused Amalric. “He’ll need all his men to repel our attack. He can’t spare any to set ambushes in the hills. Besides, this man knows his life depends on leading you as he promised. Would a dog like him sacrifice himself? Nonsense! No, Valerius, I think the man’s telling the truth.”  

“Or he’s an even greater thief than most, since he’d sell out his liberator,” laughed Valerius. “Very well. I’ll follow the dog. How many men can you spare me?”  

“Five thousand should be enough,” answered Amalric. “A surprise attack on their rear will throw them into confusion, and that’ll be enough. I’ll expect your attack around noon.”  

“You’ll know when I strike,” said Valerius.  

As Amalric returned to his pavilion, he was pleased to note that Xaltotun was still in his tent, judging by the blood-chilling cries that occasionally echoed into the night. When he later heard the clink of steel and the jingle of bridles in the darkness, he smiled grimly. Valerius had nearly outlived his usefulness. The baron knew Conan was like a wounded lion—deadly even in his final moments. When Valerius struck from behind, the Cimmerian’s desperate fury might well destroy his rival before he himself fell. All the better. Amalric felt he could easily do without Valerius once the way was cleared for a Nemedian victory.  

* * * * *  

The five thousand horsemen who rode with Valerius were mostly hardened Aquilonian deserters. In the still starlight, they moved out of the sleeping camp, following the westward line of the dark hills rising against the stars ahead. Valerius rode at their head, with Tiberias beside him, a leather thong tied around the traitor’s wrist and held by a soldier riding on his other side. Others followed close behind with drawn swords.  

“Try to trick us and you die instantly,” Valerius warned. “I don’t know every sheep path in these hills, but I know enough about the land to know which way we need to go to come in behind the Valley of Lions. Don’t lead us astray.”  

The man ducked his head, his teeth chattering as he babbled assurances of loyalty, staring stupidly up at the banner above him—the golden serpent of the old dynasty.  

Skirting the far ends of the hills that enclosed the Valley of Lions, they swung wide to the west. After an hour’s ride, they turned north, pushing through wild, rugged hills, following faint trails and twisting paths. By sunrise, they were several miles northwest of Conan’s position, and here the guide turned east, leading them through a maze of ridges and crags. Valerius nodded, judging their position by the peaks rising above the rest. He had kept his bearings well enough to know they were still heading in the right direction.  

But suddenly, without warning, a gray, misty mass came rolling down from the north, covering the slopes and spreading through the valleys. It blotted out the sun; the world became a blind gray void where one could see only a few yards ahead. Their advance turned into a stumbling, groping confusion. Valerius cursed. He could no longer see the peaks that had guided him. Now he had to rely entirely on the treacherous guide. The golden serpent banner hung limp in the windless air.

At that moment, Tiberias seemed confused; he stopped and looked around uncertainly.

“Are you lost, dog?” Valerius demanded harshly.

“Listen!”

Somewhere ahead of them, a faint vibration began—the steady rumble of a drum.

“Conan’s drum!” exclaimed the Aquilonian.

“If we’re close enough to hear the drum,” said Valerius, “why don’t we hear the shouting and the clash of weapons? Surely the battle has begun.”

“The gorges and the winds play strange tricks,” answered Tiberias, his teeth chattering with the fever that often afflicts men who’ve spent too much time in damp underground dungeons.

“Listen!”

Faintly, a low, muffled roar reached their ears.

“They’re fighting down in the valley!” cried Tiberias. “The drum is beating on the heights. Let’s hurry!”

He rode straight toward the sound of the distant drum, as if he finally knew where he was going. Valerius followed, cursing the fog. Then it occurred to him that the mist would conceal his advance. Conan wouldn’t see him coming. He’d be at the Cimmerian’s back before the midday sun burned away the fog.

Right now, he couldn’t tell what lay to either side—cliffs, thickets, or ravines. The drum kept beating, louder as they advanced, but they heard no more sounds of battle. Valerius had no idea which direction they were heading. He started when he saw gray rock walls looming through the smoky haze on either side and realized they were riding through a narrow pass. But the guide showed no sign of fear, and Valerius sighed in relief when the walls widened and disappeared again into the fog. They were through the pass; if there had been an ambush planned, it would have been there.

But now Tiberias stopped again. The drum was louder, and Valerius couldn’t tell from which direction the sound came. Now it seemed ahead, now behind, now to one side or the other. Valerius glared around impatiently, sitting on his warhorse with wisps of mist curling about him and moisture glistening on his armor. Behind him, the long lines of steel-clad riders faded away into the fog like ghosts.

“Why do you hesitate, dog?” he demanded.

The man seemed to be listening to the eerie drum. Slowly, he straightened in his saddle, turned his head, and faced Valerius, and the smile on his lips was terrible to see.

“The fog is thinning, Valerius,” he said in a strange new voice, pointing a bony finger. “Look!”

The drum was silent. The fog was fading. First, the tops of cliffs appeared above the gray clouds, tall and ghostly. Lower and lower the mists sank, shrinking, vanishing. Valerius rose in his stirrups with a cry that the horsemen behind him echoed. On all sides, cliffs towered. They were not in a wide, open valley as he had thought—they were in a dead-end gorge, walled by sheer cliffs hundreds of feet high. The only way in or out was the narrow pass they had just ridden through.

“Dog!” Valerius struck Tiberias full in the mouth with his mailed fist. “What devil’s trick is this?”

Tiberias spat out blood and shook with dreadful laughter.

“A trick to rid the world of a beast! Look, dog!”

Again Valerius cried out, more in rage than fear.

The pass was blocked by a wild and terrible band of men who stood silent as statues—ragged, wild-haired men with spears in their hands—hundreds of them. And up on the cliffs appeared other faces—thousands of faces—wild, gaunt, savage faces, marked by fire, steel, and starvation.

“A trick of Conan’s!” Valerius raged.

“Conan knows nothing of it,” laughed Tiberias. “It was the plan of broken men—men you ruined and turned into beasts. Amalric was right. Conan hasn’t divided his army. We are the rabble who followed him, the wolves who hid in these hills, the homeless, the hopeless. This was our plan, and the priests of Asura helped us with their mist. Look at them, Valerius! Each one bears your mark—on his body or on his heart!

“Look at me! You don’t recognize me, do you? What about this scar your hangman burned into me? Once you knew me. Once I was Lord of Amilius—the man whose sons you murdered, whose daughter your mercenaries violated and killed. You said I wouldn’t sacrifice myself to trap you? By the gods, if I had a thousand lives, I’d give them all to buy your doom!

“And I’ve bought it! Look at the men you broke—dead men who once played the king! Their hour has come! This gorge is your tomb. Try to climb the cliffs—they’re steep, they’re high. Try to fight your way back through the pass—spears will block your path, boulders will crush you from above! Dog! I’ll be waiting for you in hell!”

Throwing back his head, he laughed until the rocks echoed. Valerius leaned from his saddle and slashed down with his great sword, cutting through shoulder and chest. Tiberias sank to the ground, still laughing horribly through a gurgle of blood.

The drums began again, surrounding the gorge with deep, guttural thunder; boulders came crashing down; and above the screams of dying men, arrows shrieked in blinding clouds from the cliffs.

Dawn was just whitening the east when Amalric drew up his forces at the mouth of the Valley of Lions. The valley was flanked by low, rolling but steep hills, and the ground rose in a series of uneven natural terraces. On the highest of these terraces, Conan’s army held its position, waiting for the attack. The host that had joined him, marching down from Gunderland, had not been made up solely of spearmen. With them had come seven thousand Bossonian archers and four thousand barons and their retainers from the north and west, swelling the ranks of his cavalry.

The pikemen were drawn up in a tight wedge-shaped formation at the narrow head of the valley. There were nineteen thousand of them, mostly Gundermen, though about four thousand were Aquilonians from other provinces. On either side of them stood five thousand Bossonian archers. Behind the pikemen, the knights sat motionless on their horses, lances raised—ten thousand knights of Poitain, nine thousand Aquilonians, barons and their retainers.

It was a strong position. His flanks couldn’t be turned, because that would mean climbing the steep, wooded hills straight into the arrows and swords of the Bossonians. His camp lay directly behind him, in a narrow, steep-walled valley that was really just an extension of the Valley of Lions, rising to a higher level. He didn’t fear a surprise attack from behind, since the hills behind him were full of refugees and broken men whose loyalty to him was beyond doubt.

But if his position was hard to take, it was just as hard to escape from. It was both a fortress and a trap for the defenders—a desperate last stand for men who didn’t expect to live unless they won. The only possible retreat was through the narrow valley behind them.

Xaltotun climbed a hill on the left side of the valley, near its wide mouth. This hill rose higher than the others and was called the King’s Altar, though the reason for that name had long been forgotten. Only Xaltotun knew why, and his memory reached back three thousand years.

He wasn’t alone. His two familiars—silent, hairy, secretive, and dark—were with him, carrying a young Aquilonian girl bound hand and foot. They laid her on an ancient stone that looked strangely like an altar, crowning the top of the hill. For centuries it had stood there, worn by wind and rain until many believed it was just a naturally shaped rock. But Xaltotun remembered what it really was, and why it stood there. The familiars left, their bent backs making them look like silent gnomes, and Xaltotun stood alone beside the altar, his dark beard blowing in the wind as he looked down over the valley.

He could see all the way back to the winding Shirki River and up into the hills beyond the valley’s head. He could see the gleaming wedge of steel drawn up at the top of the terraces, the helmets of the archers glinting among the rocks and bushes, the silent knights motionless on their horses, their pennons streaming above their helmets, their lances rising like a forest of spears.

Looking the other way, he could see the long, tight lines of the Nemedians moving in ranks of shining steel into the mouth of the valley. Behind them stretched the bright pavilions of the lords and knights and the dull tents of the common soldiers, reaching almost to the river.

Like a river of molten steel, the Nemedian army flowed into the valley, the great scarlet dragon banner rippling above it. First came the bowmen, marching in even ranks, crossbows half raised, bolts loaded, fingers on the triggers. Behind them came the pikemen, and behind those the real strength of the army—the mounted knights, their banners unfurled in the wind, their lances lifted, riding their great horses forward as if they were heading to a feast.

And higher up on the slopes, the smaller Aquilonian force stood grim and silent.

There were thirty thousand Nemedian knights, and as in most Hyborian kingdoms, the knights were the true weapon of the army. The foot soldiers were used only to clear the way for the charge of the armored cavalry. There were twenty-one thousand of these footmen—pikemen and archers.

The bowmen began shooting as they advanced, keeping their ranks steady, launching their bolts with a whir and a snap. But the bolts fell short or bounced harmlessly off the overlapping shields of the Gundermen. And before the crossbowmen could get close enough to kill, the arching arrows of the Bossonians were tearing through their ranks.

After a short, useless exchange of fire, the Nemedian bowmen began to fall back in confusion. Their armor was light, and their weapons were no match for the Bossonian longbows. The western archers were protected by rocks and bushes. Besides, the Nemedian foot soldiers lacked the spirit of the knights, knowing they were being used only to clear the way for them.

The crossbowmen fell back, and through the gaps they left, the pikemen advanced. Most of them were mercenaries, and their commanders had no hesitation about sacrificing them. They were meant to cover the knights’ advance until the cavalry got close enough to strike. So while the crossbowmen fired from both flanks at long range, the pikemen marched straight into the deadly storm of arrows from above, and behind them the knights came on.

When the pikemen began to waver under the savage hail of death whistling down the slopes, a trumpet sounded, their ranks split to right and left, and through them thundered the armored knights.

They charged straight into a storm of deadly arrows. The long shafts found every gap in their armor and in the coverings of their horses. Horses scrambling up the grassy terraces reared and tumbled backward, dragging their riders with them. Steel-clad bodies littered the slopes. The charge faltered and rolled back.

Down in the valley, Amalric reformed his ranks. Tarascus fought with drawn sword beneath the scarlet dragon, but it was the Baron of Tor who commanded that day. Amalric cursed as he looked at the forest of lance tips visible above and beyond the helmets of the Gundermen. He had hoped his retreat would lure the knights into charging down the slopes after him, where they could be raked from both sides by his archers and crushed by his cavalry’s numbers. But they hadn’t moved. Camp servants brought water skins from the river. Knights took off their helmets and poured water over their sweating heads. The wounded on the slopes screamed in vain for water. In the upper valley, springs supplied the defenders. They didn’t thirst that long, hot spring day.

On the King’s Altar, beside the ancient carved stone, Xaltotun watched the steel tide rise and fall. The knights came on with waving plumes and lowered lances. Through a whistling storm of arrows they charged, only to crash like a thunderous wave against the bristling wall of spears and shields. Axes rose and fell above the plumed helmets, spears thrust upward, bringing down horses and riders. The pride of the Gundermen was as fierce as that of the knights. They were not cannon fodder to be sacrificed for others’ glory. They were the finest infantry in the world, with a tradition that made their courage unbreakable. The kings of Aquilonia had long known the value of such steadfast soldiers. They held their formation firm; over their shining ranks flew the great lion banner, and at the tip of the wedge a giant figure in black armor roared and struck like a storm, wielding a blood-soaked axe that split steel and bone alike.

The Nemedians fought as bravely as their proud traditions demanded. But they couldn’t break through the iron wedge, and from the wooded hills on both sides, arrows tore mercilessly into their tightly packed ranks. Their own archers were useless, their pikemen unable to climb the slopes and engage the Bossonians hand to hand. Slowly, stubbornly, grimly, the hardened knights fell back, counting the empty saddles among them. Above them, the Gundermen made no cries of victory. They closed their ranks, filling the gaps left by the fallen. Sweat ran into their eyes beneath their steel helmets. They gripped their spears and waited, their fierce hearts swelling with pride that their king fought beside them on foot. Behind them, the Aquilonian knights had not moved. They sat their horses, grim and motionless.  

A knight spurred his sweating horse up the hill called the King’s Altar and glared bitterly at Xaltotun.  

“Amalric sends word that it’s time to use your magic, wizard,” he said. “We’re dying like flies down there in the valley. We can’t break their lines.”  

Xaltotun seemed to grow taller, more terrible, and filled with dreadful power.  

“Go back to Amalric,” he said. “Tell him to reform his ranks for a charge, but to wait for my signal. Before that signal comes, he’ll see something he’ll remember until his dying breath!”  

The knight saluted, as if compelled against his will, and galloped down the hill at breakneck speed.  

Xaltotun stood beside the dark altar stone and looked across the valley—at the dead and wounded lying on the terraces, at the grim, blood-stained band holding the slopes, at the dusty, steel-clad ranks reforming in the valley below. He looked up at the sky, then down at the slim white figure lying on the dark stone. Lifting a dagger engraved with ancient hieroglyphs, he began to chant an age-old invocation:  

“Set, god of darkness, scaly lord of shadows, by the blood of a virgin and the sevenfold symbol I call to your sons beneath the black earth! Children of the depths, beneath the red earth, under the black earth, awaken and shake your dreadful manes! Let the hills quake and the stones fall upon my enemies! Let the sky darken above them, the ground tremble beneath their feet! Let a wind from the deep black earth rise beneath them, to blacken and shrivel them—”  

He stopped suddenly, dagger raised. In the tense silence, the roar of the armies below rose up to him, carried by the wind.  

On the other side of the altar stood a man in a black hooded robe, his cowl shadowing pale, delicate features and dark, calm, thoughtful eyes.  

“Dog of Asura!” hissed Xaltotun, his voice like the sound of an enraged serpent. “Are you mad, that you come seeking your death? Ho, Baal! Chiron!”  

“Call again, dog of Acheron!” said the other, laughing. “Call louder. They won’t hear you unless your shouts echo in hell.”  

From a thicket at the edge of the hilltop came a grim old woman in peasant clothes, her hair flowing over her shoulders, a great gray wolf padding at her heels.  

“Witch, priest, and wolf,” muttered Xaltotun darkly, then laughed. “Fools, to pit your cheap tricks against my power! With a wave of my hand I could sweep you aside!”  

“Your power is like straw in the wind, dog of Python,” replied the Asurian. “Did you wonder why the Shirki didn’t flood and trap Conan on the far bank? When I saw the lightning last night, I guessed your plan, and my spells scattered the clouds you summoned before they could pour their torrents. You didn’t even know your rain-magic had failed.”  

“You lie!” shouted Xaltotun, though doubt crept into his voice. “I felt the clash of a strong sorcery against mine—but no man alive could undo the rain-magic once it’s cast, unless he holds the very heart of sorcery itself.”  

“But the flood you planned never came,” said the priest. “Look at your allies in the valley, Pythonian! You’ve led them to the slaughter! They’re caught in the jaws of the trap, and you can’t help them. Look!”  

He pointed. Out of the narrow gorge at the upper end of the valley, behind the Poitanians, a lone horseman came racing, swinging something above his head that flashed in the sunlight. Recklessly he charged down the slopes, through the ranks of the Gundermen, who roared and clashed their spears and shields like thunder rolling through the hills. On the terraces between the armies, the sweat-drenched horse reared and plunged, while its wild rider shouted and waved the thing in his hands like a madman. It was the torn remnant of a scarlet banner, and the sun glinted off the golden scales of a serpent that coiled upon it.  

“Valerius is dead!” cried Hadrathus, his voice ringing. “A fog and a drum lured him to his doom! I gathered that fog, dog of Python, and I scattered it! I, with my magic that is greater than yours!”  

“What does it matter?” roared Xaltotun, a terrifying sight, his eyes blazing, his face twisted with fury. “Valerius was a fool. I don’t need him. I can crush Conan without any mortal help!”  

“Then why have you delayed?” mocked Hadrathus. “Why have you let so many of your allies fall, pierced by arrows and skewered on spears?”  

“Because blood fuels great sorcery!” thundered Xaltotun, his voice shaking the rocks. A fiery aura glowed around his dreadful head. “Because no wizard wastes his strength carelessly. Because I would save my power for the great days to come, rather than spend it on a skirmish in the hills. But now, by Set, I’ll unleash it to its fullest! Watch, dog of Asura, false priest of a dying god, and see a sight that will shatter your mind forever!”  

Hadrathus threw back his head and laughed, and his laughter sounded like the echo of hell itself.  

“Look, black devil of Python!”  

His hand came from beneath his robe, holding something that blazed and burned in the sunlight, turning the light into a pulsing golden glow that made Xaltotun’s flesh look like that of a corpse.

Xaltotun cried out as if he had been stabbed.  

“The Heart! The Heart of Ahriman!”  

“Yes! The one power that’s greater than yours!”  

Xaltotun seemed to shrink and age before their eyes. Suddenly his beard turned white as snow, and gray streaks appeared in his hair.  

“The Heart!” he muttered. “You stole it! Dog! Thief!”  

“Not me! It’s been on a long journey far to the south. But now it’s in my hands, and your dark magic can’t stand against it. Just as it brought you back to life, it will now cast you back into the darkness from which it pulled you. You’ll go down the dark road to Acheron, the road of silence and night. The dark empire will remain unreborn, only a legend and a grim memory. Conan will rule again. And the Heart of Ahriman will return to the cavern beneath the temple of Mitra, to burn as a symbol of Aquilonia’s power for a thousand years!”  

Xaltotun screamed like something not human and rushed around the altar, dagger raised. But from somewhere—maybe the sky, maybe the great jewel blazing in Hadrathus’s hand—a jet of blinding blue light shot forth. It struck Xaltotun square in the chest, and the hills echoed with the explosion. The wizard of Acheron fell as if hit by lightning, and before he even touched the ground, he changed horribly. Beside the altar stone lay not a freshly slain body, but a shriveled mummy—a dry, brown, unrecognizable husk tangled in rotting wrappings.  

Old Zelata looked down grimly.  

“He wasn’t truly alive,” she said. “The Heart gave him only the illusion of life, enough to fool even himself. I never saw him as anything but a mummy.”  

Hadrathus bent to untie the fainting girl on the altar when a strange sight appeared among the trees—Xaltotun’s chariot, drawn by those eerie horses. Silently they came forward to the altar and stopped, the wheel almost touching the withered corpse on the grass. Hadrathus lifted the wizard’s body and placed it in the chariot. Without hesitation, the ghostly steeds turned and moved off to the south, down the hill. Hadrathus, Zelata, and the gray wolf watched them go—down the long road to Acheron, beyond the reach of mortal men.  

* * * * *  

Down in the valley, Amalric stiffened in his saddle when he saw that wild rider prancing and wheeling on the slopes, waving the bloodstained serpent banner. Then some instinct made him turn his head toward the hill called the King’s Altar. His mouth fell open. Every man in the valley saw it—a blazing column of light shooting up from the hilltop, showering golden fire. High above the armies it burst into a blinding flash that for a moment outshone the sun.  

“That’s not Xaltotun’s signal!” roared the baron.  

“No!” shouted Tarascus. “It’s a signal to the Aquilonians! Look!”  

Above them, the motionless ranks were finally moving, and a deep, thunderous roar rolled across the valley.  

“Xaltotun has failed us!” bellowed Amalric in fury. “Valerius has failed us! We’ve been led into a trap! Mitra’s curse on Xaltotun for bringing us here! Sound the retreat!”  

“Too late!” yelled Tarascus. “Look!”  

Up on the slopes, the forest of lances dipped and leveled. The ranks of the Gundermen rolled back to either side like a curtain being drawn apart. And with a thunder like a rising storm, the knights of Aquilonia charged down the slopes.  

The force of that charge was unstoppable. Bolts fired by the panicked crossbowmen glanced off their shields and dented helmets. With plumes and pennons streaming behind them, lances lowered, they swept over the wavering pikemen and thundered down the hill like a wave.  

Amalric shouted an order to charge, and the Nemedians, desperate and brave, spurred their weary horses up the slope. They still outnumbered the attackers.  

But they were exhausted men on tired mounts, charging uphill. The oncoming knights hadn’t fought yet that day. Their horses were fresh, and they were charging downhill like a storm. They struck the Nemedian ranks like a thunderbolt—shattering them, splitting them apart, tearing through them and driving the survivors headlong down the slopes.  

Behind them came the Gundermen on foot, blood-crazed, and the Bossonians were swarming down the hills, shooting as they ran at every enemy still moving.  

Down the slopes the battle rolled, the dazed Nemedians swept along in the flood. Their archers threw down their crossbows and fled. The pikemen who survived the knights’ devastating charge were cut down by the relentless Gundermen.  

The battle surged through the wide mouth of the valley and out onto the plain beyond. All across the plain the warriors swarmed—fleeing and chasing, breaking into single duels and clusters of hacking, slashing knights on rearing horses. But the Nemedians were finished—smashed, broken, unable to regroup or make a stand. Hundreds broke away, spurring for the river. Many reached it, crossed, and rode eastward. But the countryside had risen behind them; the people hunted them like wolves. Few ever made it to Tarantia.  

The final collapse came with Amalric’s fall. The baron, trying in vain to rally his men, rode straight at the group of knights following the giant in black armor whose surcoat bore the royal lion, and above whose head flew the golden lion banner beside the scarlet leopard of Poitain. A tall warrior in shining armor lowered his lance and charged to meet the lord of Tor. They met with a crash like thunder. The Nemedian’s lance struck his opponent’s helmet, snapping bolts and rivets and tearing it off, revealing the face of Pallantides. But the Aquilonian’s lance drove through shield and breastplate, piercing the baron’s heart.  

A roar went up as Amalric was thrown from his saddle, breaking the lance that impaled him, and the Nemedians broke apart like a dam bursting under a tidal wave. They fled for the river in a blind stampede that swept across the plain like a storm. The Hour of the Dragon had ended.

Tarascus did not run. Amalric was dead, the standard-bearer slain, and the royal Nemedian banner trampled into the blood and dust. Most of his knights were fleeing, and the Aquilonians were cutting them down; Tarascus knew the battle was lost, but with a handful of loyal followers he raged through the chaos, driven by one desire—to face Conan the Cimmerian. And at last, he found him.  

The formations were completely shattered, tight ranks broken apart and scattered. The crest of Trocero gleamed in one part of the plain, those of Prospero and Pallantides in others. Conan stood alone. The household troops of Tarascus had fallen one by one. The two kings met face to face.  

As they charged at each other, the horse of Tarascus gave a choking sound and collapsed beneath him. Conan leaped from his own horse and ran toward him as the king of Nemedia freed himself and rose. Steel flashed blindingly in the sunlight, clashed loudly, and blue sparks flew; then came the crash of armor as Tarascus fell full length to the ground beneath a thunderous blow from Conan’s broadsword.  

The Cimmerian placed a mailed foot on his enemy’s chest and raised his sword. His helmet was gone; he shook back his black hair, and his blue eyes blazed with their old fire.  

“Do you surrender?”  

“Will you spare my life?” demanded the Nemedian.  

“Yes. Better than you would have done for me, you dog. Life for you and all your men who throw down their weapons. Though I should split your skull for a damned thief,” Conan added.  

Tarascus turned his head and glared across the plain. The remnants of the Nemedian army were fleeing across the stone bridge, with swarms of victorious Aquilonians at their heels, striking with the fury of vengeance finally satisfied. Bossonians and Gundermen were pouring through the enemy camp, tearing down tents in search of loot, seizing prisoners, ripping open baggage, and overturning wagons.  

Tarascus cursed bitterly, then shrugged as best he could under the circumstances.  

“Very well. I have no choice. What are your terms?”  

“You’ll surrender to me all your current holdings in Aquilonia. Order your garrisons to march out of the castles and towns they occupy, unarmed, and get your damned armies out of Aquilonia as fast as possible. You’ll also return all Aquilonians sold into slavery and pay compensation to be decided later, once the damage your occupation caused has been properly assessed. You’ll remain as a hostage until these terms are fulfilled.”  

“Very well,” said Tarascus. “I’ll surrender all castles and towns now held by my garrisons without resistance, and the rest will be done. What ransom for my person?”  

Conan laughed, took his foot off the steel-clad chest of his foe, grabbed his shoulder, and hauled him to his feet. He started to speak, then turned as he saw Hadrathus approaching. The priest was calm and composed as ever, stepping carefully among the rows of dead men and horses.  

Conan wiped the sweat-streaked dust from his face with a blood-stained hand. He had fought all day—first on foot with the pikemen, then in the saddle, leading the charge. His surcoat was gone, his armor splattered with blood and dented by blows from swords, maces, and axes. He loomed huge against the backdrop of blood and carnage, like some grim pagan hero out of legend.  

“Well done, Hadrathus!” he said heartily. “By Crom, I’m glad to see your signal! My knights were nearly mad with impatience, burning to fight. I couldn’t have held them back much longer. What of the wizard?”  

“He has gone down the dark road to Acheron,” answered Hadrathus. “And I—I am bound for Tarantia. My work here is finished, and I have a duty to perform at the temple of Mitra. All our work here is done. On this field we have saved Aquilonia—and more than Aquilonia. Your ride to your capital will be a triumphal procession through a kingdom wild with joy. All Aquilonia will be cheering the return of their king. And so, until we meet again in the great royal hall—farewell!”  

Conan stood silently watching the priest depart. From different parts of the field, knights were hurrying toward him. He saw Pallantides, Trocero, Prospero, Servius Galannus, their armor splashed with crimson. The thunder of battle was fading into a roar of triumph and celebration. All eyes, still fierce from combat and shining with victory, turned toward the great black figure of the king; mailed arms raised blood-stained swords. A deep, thunderous roar rose like the crashing of sea waves: “Hail, Conan, king of Aquilonia!”  

Tarascus spoke.  

“You still haven’t named my ransom.”  

Conan laughed and slid his sword back into its scabbard. He flexed his mighty arms and ran his blood-streaked fingers through his thick black hair, as if feeling there his regained crown.  

“There’s a girl in your harem named Zenobia.”  

“Yes, there is.”  

“Good.” The king smiled as if recalling a very pleasant memory. “She’ll be your ransom, and nothing else. I’ll come to Belverus for her, as I promised. She was a slave in Nemedia, but I’ll make her queen of Aquilonia!”
