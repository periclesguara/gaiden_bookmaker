What he had heard registered in Conan’s mind, but it meant nothing to him. He was like a mindless machine that could hear and see but not understand. Sights and sounds drifted around him without meaning. He sank again into a deep stupor, only vaguely aware when the chariot stopped in a high-walled courtyard, and many hands lifted him out, carrying him up a winding stone staircase and down a long, dim hallway. Whispers, soft footsteps, and random noises swirled around him, distant and meaningless.  

But when he finally awoke, it was sudden and crystal clear. He remembered everything about the battle in the mountains and what had happened afterward, and he had a good idea of where he was.  

He lay on a velvet couch, dressed as he had been the day before, but his limbs were bound with chains even he could not break. The room around him was darkly magnificent, its walls covered with black velvet tapestries and the floor with thick purple carpets. There was no visible door or window, and a single gold lamp, intricately carved and hanging from the decorated ceiling, cast a strange, reddish light over everything.  

In that light, the figure sitting before him in a silver, throne-like chair looked unreal and dreamlike, its outline blurred by a thin, silken robe. Yet the man’s face was sharply clear—unnaturally so in that uncertain glow. It was as if a strange halo surrounded his head, throwing his bearded face into bold relief, making it the only solid, undeniable reality in that eerie, ghostly chamber.  

It was a striking face, with strong, sculpted features of classical beauty. But there was something unsettling about its calmness, a sense of knowledge beyond human understanding, of certainty beyond mortal confidence. And at the back of Conan’s mind, a faint feeling of recognition stirred. He knew he had never seen this man before, yet something about those features reminded him of someone—or something. It was like meeting in real life a figure that had haunted his nightmares.  

“Who are you?” demanded the king harshly, struggling to sit up despite his chains.  

“Men call me Xaltotun,” came the reply, in a deep, golden voice.  

“What place is this?” Conan asked next.  

“A chamber in the palace of King Tarascus, in Belverus.”  

Conan wasn’t surprised. Belverus, the capital, was also the largest Nemedian city near the border.  

“And where’s Tarascus?”  

“With the army.”  

“Well,” Conan growled, “if you plan to kill me, why don’t you just do it and be done with it?”  

“I didn’t save you from the king’s archers just to murder you in Belverus,” said Xaltotun.  

“What in hell did you do to me?” Conan demanded.  

“I shattered your consciousness,” Xaltotun replied. “How, you wouldn’t understand. Call it black magic, if you like.”  

Conan had already guessed as much, but his mind was turning to something else.  

“I think I see why you spared me,” he rumbled. “Amalric wants to keep me alive as a counter to Valerius, in case the impossible happens and Valerius becomes king of Aquilonia. Everyone knows the baron of Tor is behind this plan to put Valerius on my throne. And if I know Amalric, he doesn’t mean for Valerius to be anything more than a puppet—just like Tarascus is now.”  

“Amalric knows nothing about your capture,” said Xaltotun. “Neither does Valerius. Both believe you died at Valkia.”  

Conan’s eyes narrowed as he stared silently at the man.  

“I knew there was a mind behind all this,” he muttered, “but I thought it was Amalric’s. Are Amalric, Tarascus, and Valerius all just puppets dancing on your strings? Who are you really?”  

“What does it matter? If I told you, you wouldn’t believe me. What if I said I could put you back on the throne of Aquilonia?”  

Conan’s eyes burned like a wolf’s.  

“What’s your price?”  

“Obedience to me.”  

“Go to hell with your offer!” Conan snarled. “I’m no puppet. I won my crown with my sword. And besides, you can’t just buy and sell the throne of Aquilonia as you please. The kingdom isn’t conquered; one battle doesn’t decide a war.”  

“You fight against more than swords,” said Xaltotun. “Was it a mortal’s blade that struck you down in your tent before the battle? No—it was a creature of darkness, a wanderer from the outer void, whose fingers burned with the frozen cold of the black gulfs, freezing the blood in your veins and the marrow in your bones. Cold so intense it seared your flesh like white-hot iron!  

“Was it mere chance that made the man wearing your armor lead his knights into the pass—chance that brought the cliffs crashing down upon them?”  

Conan glared at him without speaking, a chill creeping along his spine. Wizards and sorcerers filled his barbaric legends, and anyone could see this was no ordinary man. Conan felt something about him that set him apart—an alien aura of time and space, a sense of immense and sinister age. But his stubborn spirit refused to yield.  

“The fall of the cliffs was chance,” he said defiantly. “The charge into the pass was what any man would have done.”  

“Not so. You would never have led a charge into it. You would have suspected a trap. You would never have crossed the river at all until you were sure the Nemedian retreat was real. No hypnotic suggestion could have invaded your mind, even in the madness of battle, to drive you into the snare as it did the lesser man who pretended to be you.”  

“Then if all this was planned,” Conan grunted skeptically, “if it was all a scheme to trap my army, why didn’t your ‘creature of darkness’ just kill me in my tent?”  

“Because I wanted you alive. It took no sorcery to foresee that Pallantides would send another man out in your armor. I wanted you alive and unharmed. You may have a place in my plans. There is a living power in you greater than the skill and cunning of my allies. You are a dangerous enemy—but you might make a great servant.”
