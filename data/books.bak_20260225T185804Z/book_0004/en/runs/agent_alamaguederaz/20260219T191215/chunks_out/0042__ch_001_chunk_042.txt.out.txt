Suddenly, a door opened in the wall ahead of him, and he slipped into the shadow of an archway. A figure stepped out of the open door and moved down the alley—not sneaking, but moving with a natural, silent grace, like a jungle predator. Enough starlight filtered into the alley to faintly outline the man’s profile as he passed the doorway where Conan hid. The stranger was a Stygian. There was no mistaking that hawk-like face and shaven head, even in the dim light, nor the cloak draped over his broad shoulders. He continued down the alley toward the beach, and for a moment Conan thought the man might be carrying a lantern hidden in his clothes, because he caught a brief flash of glowing light just as the man disappeared.

But Conan forgot the stranger when he noticed that the door the man had come through was still open. Conan had planned to enter through the main entrance and force Servio to show him the room where the Zingaran slept. But if he could get inside without drawing attention, all the better.

A few long strides brought him to the door, and as his hand touched the lock, he stifled a low grunt. His experienced fingers—trained long ago among the thieves of Zamora—told him the lock had been forced, apparently by some tremendous pressure from the outside that had twisted and bent the heavy iron bolts, tearing the sockets loose from the doorframe. How such damage could have been done so violently without waking the whole neighborhood, Conan couldn’t imagine, but he was sure it had happened that night. A broken lock wouldn’t have gone unrepaired in Servio’s house, not in this district of thieves and killers.

Conan slipped inside quietly, dagger in hand, wondering how he would find the Zingaran’s room. Feeling his way through the total darkness, he suddenly stopped. He sensed death in the room, the way a wild animal senses it—not as danger to himself, but as the presence of something freshly killed. In the dark, his foot struck something heavy and soft. With a sudden chill of foreboding, he groped along the wall until he found the shelf that held a brass lamp, with flint, steel, and tinder beside it. A few seconds later, a flickering, uncertain light sprang up, and he looked around sharply.

A bunk built against the rough stone wall, a bare table, and a bench were all the furnishings in the shabby room. An inner door stood closed and bolted. On the hard-packed dirt floor lay Beloso. He was on his back, his head pulled back between his shoulders so that his wide, glassy eyes seemed to stare at the soot-blackened beams of the cobwebbed ceiling. His lips were drawn back from his teeth in a frozen grimace of agony. His sword lay near him, still sheathed. His shirt was torn open, and on his brown, muscular chest was the mark of a black hand—thumb and four fingers clearly visible.

Conan stared in silence, the short hairs on the back of his neck bristling.

“Crom!” he muttered. “The black hand of Set!”

He had seen that mark before—the death sign of the black priests of Set, the grim cult that ruled in dark Stygia. And suddenly he remembered the strange flash of light he had seen coming from the mysterious Stygian who had left this room.

“The Heart, by Crom!” he muttered. “He was carrying it under his cloak. He stole it. He broke that door with his magic and killed Beloso. He was a priest of Set.”

A quick search confirmed at least part of his suspicions. The jewel was gone from the Zingaran’s body. An uneasy feeling rose in Conan that this hadn’t happened by accident or coincidence; he was convinced that the mysterious Stygian ship had come into Messantia’s harbor for a specific purpose. How could the priests of Set have known the Heart had come south? Yet the thought was no more unbelievable than the sorcery that could kill an armed man with the touch of an open, empty hand.

A stealthy footstep outside the door made him spin around like a great cat. In one motion he snuffed out the lamp and drew his sword. His ears told him there were men out there in the darkness, closing in on the doorway. As his eyes adjusted to the sudden dark, he could make out faint shapes surrounding the entrance. He couldn’t guess who they were, but as always, he took the initiative—leaping suddenly out of the doorway before they could strike.

His unexpected move caught the ambushers off guard. He sensed and heard men all around him, saw a dim, masked figure in the starlight ahead of him; then his sword struck home, and he was sprinting down the alley before the slower-thinking attackers could react.

As he ran, he heard, somewhere ahead, the faint creak of oarlocks, and he forgot the men behind him. A boat was moving out into the bay! Gritting his teeth, he ran faster, but before he reached the beach, he heard the rasp and creak of ropes and the grinding of a great sweep in its socket.

Thick clouds rolling in from the sea blotted out the stars. In near-total darkness, Conan reached the shore, straining his eyes toward the black, restless water. Something was moving out there—a long, low, dark shape gliding away into the night, gathering speed as it went. He could hear the rhythmic clack of long oars. He ground his teeth in helpless rage. It was the Stygian galley, racing out to sea, carrying with it the jewel that meant the throne of Aquilonia to him.

With a savage curse, he stepped toward the waves lapping at the sand, grabbing at his mail shirt, ready to tear it off and swim after the fleeing ship. Then the crunch of a heel in the sand made him whirl around. He had forgotten his pursuers.

Dark figures rushed at him, feet pounding through the sand. The first went down under the Cimmerian’s sweeping sword, but the others didn’t hesitate. Blades flashed dimly around him in the dark or scraped against his armor. Blood and entrails splattered over his hand, and someone screamed as he slashed upward with deadly force. A muttered voice urged the attackers on, and that voice sounded vaguely familiar. Conan fought his way through the clutching, hacking figures toward the sound. A faint light flickered briefly through the drifting clouds, revealing a tall, gaunt man with a large, pale scar on his temple. Conan’s sword sliced through his skull as easily as through a ripe melon.
