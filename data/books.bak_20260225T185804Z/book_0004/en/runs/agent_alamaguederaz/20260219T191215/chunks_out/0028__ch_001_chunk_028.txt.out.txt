They had entered a narrow alley, dark as pitch, with the Tower’s wall on one side and the sheer stone backs of a row of buildings on the other. Conan, moving as quickly as he dared through the darkness, felt along the latter wall for doors or windows but found none.

Behind them, the great door clanged open, and men poured out, their torches flashing on breastplates and bare swords. They glared around, shouting, unable to pierce the darkness that their torches lit only a few feet in any direction, then charged down the alley at random—heading the opposite way from Conan and Albiona.

“They’ll realize their mistake soon enough,” he muttered, quickening his pace. “If we can just find a crack in this cursed wall—damn! The street watch!”

Ahead, a faint glow appeared where the alley opened into a narrow street, and he saw dim figures silhouetted against it, steel glinting faintly. It was indeed the street watch, investigating the noise that had echoed down the alley.

“Who goes there?” they shouted, and Conan clenched his teeth at the hated Nemedian accent.

“Stay behind me,” he told the girl. “We’ve got to cut our way through before the prison guards come back and trap us between them.”

Drawing his sword, he charged straight at the oncoming figures. The advantage of surprise was his. He could see them outlined against the distant light, while they couldn’t see him coming out of the black depths of the alley. He was among them before they realized it, striking with the silent fury of a wounded lion.

His only chance was to hack through before they could recover their senses. But there were about ten of them, in full armor, hardened veterans of the border wars, whose battle instincts could replace their stunned wits. Three went down before they realized it was only one man attacking, but even then their reaction was instant. The clash of steel rang deafeningly, sparks flying as Conan’s sword smashed against helmets and mail. He could see better than they could, and in the dim light his swiftly moving form was a hard target. Blades slashed empty air or glanced off his sword, and when he struck, it was with the force and precision of a storm.

But behind him came the shouts of the prison guards, running up the alley, and still the armored watchmen blocked his way with a wall of steel. In moments the guards would be on his back—desperate, he redoubled his blows, striking like a blacksmith at his forge. Then suddenly he sensed a change. Out of nowhere behind the watchmen rose a group of dark figures, and the sound of deadly blows filled the air. Steel flashed in the gloom, and men cried out, cut down from behind. In an instant the alley was strewn with writhing bodies. A dark, cloaked figure leapt toward Conan, who raised his sword, catching a glint of steel in the man’s right hand. But the other hand reached out empty, and a voice hissed urgently, “This way, your Majesty! Quickly!”

With a muttered curse of surprise, Conan scooped up Albiona in one massive arm and followed his unknown rescuer. He wasn’t about to hesitate, not with thirty prison guards closing in behind him.

Surrounded by the mysterious figures, he hurried down the alley, carrying the countess as if she were a child. He could tell nothing about his rescuers except that they wore dark cloaks and hoods. Doubt and suspicion flickered in his mind, but they had struck down his enemies, and he saw no better choice than to follow.

As if sensing his doubt, the leader lightly touched his arm and said, “Do not fear, King Conan; we are your loyal subjects.” The voice was unfamiliar, but the accent was Aquilonian from the central provinces.

Behind them, the guards shouted as they stumbled over the bodies in the mud, then came charging down the alley, seeing the vague dark mass moving between them and the distant streetlight. But the hooded men suddenly turned toward what looked like a blank wall, and Conan saw a door open there. He swore under his breath. He had walked that alley by day in the past and had never noticed a door. But through it they went, and it closed behind them with the click of a lock. The sound wasn’t reassuring, but his guides hurried him on, moving with practiced ease, guiding Conan with a hand on each elbow. It felt like walking through a tunnel, and Conan felt Albiona trembling in his arms. Then ahead of them a faint opening appeared—a slightly lighter arch in the darkness—and through it they passed.

After that came a confusing series of dim courtyards, shadowy alleys, and winding corridors, all crossed in complete silence, until at last they emerged into a broad, lighted chamber. Conan couldn’t even guess where they were, for their twisting route had confused even his keen sense of direction.

Not all of his guides entered the chamber. When the door closed, Conan saw only one man standing before him—a slim figure in a black cloak and hood. The man threw it back, revealing a pale, oval face with calm, finely carved features.

The king set Albiona on her feet, but she still clung to him, staring around nervously. The chamber was large, its marble walls partly covered with black velvet drapes, thick rich carpets spread over the mosaic floor, and the whole place bathed in the soft golden glow of bronze lamps.

Conan instinctively rested a hand on his sword hilt. There was blood on his hand, and dried blood around the mouth of his scabbard, for he had sheathed his blade without cleaning it.

“Where are we?” he demanded.

The stranger bowed deeply, and Conan, ever suspicious, could detect no hint of mockery in the gesture.

“In the temple of Asura, your Majesty.”

Albiona gave a faint cry and clung tighter to Conan, staring fearfully at the black arched doors as if expecting some monstrous shape to emerge from them.

“Do not be afraid, my lady,” said their guide. “There is nothing here that can harm you, despite the common superstitions. If your king was wise enough to see the innocence of our faith and protect us from the persecution of the ignorant, then surely one of his subjects has no reason to fear.”
