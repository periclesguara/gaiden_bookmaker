The stallion panted, straining with effort. The landscape was changing in the deepening twilight. The bare plains gave way to clusters of oaks and alders. Low hills rose in the distance. Stars began to appear. The stallion gasped and staggered as it ran. But ahead loomed a thick forest stretching to the hills on the horizon, and between it and himself Conan caught sight of the faint shape of the fugitive. He pushed the exhausted stallion harder, seeing that he was gaining on his prey, yard by yard. Above the pounding of hooves, a strange cry rose from the shadows, but neither the pursuer nor the pursued paid attention.

As they raced beneath the branches hanging over the road, they were almost side by side. A fierce cry burst from Conan’s lips as his sword lifted; a pale oval face turned toward him, a sword flashed in a half-seen hand, and Beloso echoed the cry—and then the weary stallion, with a lurch and a groan, lost its footing in the shadows and went head over heels, throwing its dazed rider from the saddle. Conan’s throbbing head struck a stone, and the stars vanished into a deeper darkness.

* * * * *

Conan never knew how long he lay unconscious. His first sensation as awareness returned was that of being dragged by one arm over rough, stony ground and through thick underbrush. Then he was dropped carelessly, and perhaps the jolt brought him fully back to his senses.

His helmet was gone, his head throbbed painfully, nausea twisted his stomach, and blood was clotted thickly in his black hair. But with the vitality of a wild creature, life and consciousness surged back into him, and he became aware of his surroundings.

A broad red moon shone through the trees, telling him it was long past midnight. He had been unconscious for hours—long enough to recover from the brutal blow Beloso had given him, as well as from the fall that had knocked him out. His mind felt clearer than it had during that mad chase after the fugitive.

He realized with surprise that he wasn’t lying beside the white road. The road was nowhere in sight. He lay on grassy ground in a small clearing surrounded by a black wall of tree trunks and tangled branches. His face and hands were scratched and torn as if he had been dragged through thorny bushes. Shifting his body, he looked around. Then he froze in shock—something was crouching over him.

At first Conan doubted his senses, thinking it was only a fever dream. Surely that strange, motionless gray creature squatting on its haunches and staring down at him with unblinking, soulless eyes couldn’t be real.

Conan lay still, staring, half expecting it to fade like a vision. Then a chill of recognition crept down his spine. Half-forgotten memories surged back—grisly tales whispered about the shapes haunting these deserted forests at the foot of the hills marking the Zingaran-Argossean border. Ghouls, people called them—eaters of human flesh, spawn of darkness, children of unholy unions between a lost, forgotten race and the demons of the underworld. Somewhere in these ancient woods, men said, lay the ruins of a cursed city, and among its tombs crept gray, man-shaped shadows. Conan shuddered violently.

He stared at the deformed head looming above him and cautiously reached for the sword at his hip. With a horrible cry that Conan involuntarily echoed, the creature lunged for his throat.

Conan threw up his right arm, and the beast’s dog-like jaws clamped down, driving the mail links into his flesh. Its twisted, human-like hands clawed for his throat, but he twisted and rolled his whole body aside, drawing his dagger with his left hand.

They rolled and fought on the grass, striking and tearing. The muscles beneath that gray, corpse-like skin were wiry and hard as steel cords, stronger than any man’s. But Conan’s own strength was iron, and his mail protected him from the snapping fangs and raking claws long enough for him to drive his dagger home again and again and again. The creature’s horrible vitality seemed endless, and the king’s skin crawled at the feel of that slick, clammy flesh. He poured all his hatred and revulsion into his stabbing arm, and suddenly the monster convulsed beneath him as the blade found its foul heart—then lay still.

Conan rose, shaken and nauseated. He stood uncertainly in the center of the clearing, sword in one hand and dagger in the other. He hadn’t lost his instinctive sense of direction, but he didn’t know where the road was. He had no idea which way the ghoul had dragged him. Conan glared at the silent, black, moonlit forest surrounding him and felt cold sweat bead on his skin. He was without a horse and lost in these haunted woods, and the deformed corpse at his feet was proof of the horrors lurking there. He stood almost holding his breath, straining his ears for any crack of a twig or rustle of grass.

When a sound finally came, he started violently. Suddenly, the scream of a terrified horse split the night air. His stallion! There were panthers in the woods—or maybe ghouls fed on animals as well as men.

He crashed through the brush toward the sound, whistling sharply as he ran, his fear drowned in berserk fury. If his horse was killed, he would lose his last chance to catch Beloso and recover the jewel. Again the stallion screamed in fear and rage, closer now. There was the sound of lashing hooves, a heavy impact, and something giving way.

Conan burst suddenly onto the wide white road and saw the stallion rearing and plunging in the moonlight, ears pinned back, eyes and teeth flashing wildly. It lashed out with its hooves at a slinking shadow that darted around it—and then Conan saw other shapes moving around him: gray, stealthy figures closing in from all sides. A foul, corpse-like stench rose into the night air.
