All day the Venturer stayed anchored in the small bay, surrounded by green branches and tangled vines where brightly colored, loud-voiced birds darted and shiny-scaled, silent reptiles slid through the leaves. As the sun began to set, a small boat slipped out and moved quietly along the shore, searching for and finding what Conan wanted—a Stygian fisherman in his shallow, flat-nosed boat.

They brought the man aboard the Venturer—a tall, dark, leanly built fellow, pale with fear of his captors, who were considered monsters along that coast. He wore nothing but his silk breeches, for in Stygia, even the commoners and slaves dressed in silk, much like the Hyrkanians. In his boat lay a wide cloak such as fishermen used to wrap around themselves against the chill of night.

He dropped to his knees before Conan, expecting torture and death.

“Get up, man, and stop shaking,” said the Cimmerian impatiently, finding it hard to understand such cowardly fear. “You won’t be harmed. Just tell me this—has a galley, a black racing galley returning from Argos, come into Khemi in the last few days?”

“Yes, my lord,” answered the fisherman. “Only yesterday at dawn, the priest Thutothmes returned from a voyage far to the north. People say he went to Messantia.”

“What did he bring back from Messantia?”

“Alas, my lord, I do not know.”

“Why did he go to Messantia?” Conan demanded.

“My lord, I am only a common man. How could I know the minds of the priests of Set? I can only tell you what I’ve seen and what I’ve heard whispered along the docks. People say news of great importance came from the north, though no one knows what it was. It’s well known that Lord Thutothmes left in his black galley in great haste. Now he has returned, but what he did in Argos or what cargo he brought back, no one knows—not even the sailors who manned his ship. Men say he has opposed Thoth-Amon, who is the master of all priests of Set and lives in Luxur, and that Thutothmes seeks hidden power to overthrow the Great One. But who am I to say? When priests fight each other, a common man can only lie flat and hope neither steps on him.”

Conan growled in irritation at this servile way of thinking and turned to his men. “I’m going into Khemi alone to find this thief Thutothmes. Keep this man prisoner, but don’t harm him. Crom’s devils, stop your whining! Do you think we can sail into the harbor and take the city by force? I have to go alone.”

Ignoring their protests, he stripped off his own clothes and put on the prisoner’s silk breeches and sandals, tying his hair with the man’s band, but he refused the short fisherman’s knife. Common Stygian men weren’t allowed to carry swords, and the cloak wasn’t large enough to hide Conan’s long blade. Instead, he strapped to his hip a Ghanata knife, a weapon used by the fierce desert tribes south of Stygia—a broad, heavy, slightly curved blade of fine steel, sharp as a razor and long enough to cut a man apart.

Leaving the Stygian guarded by the corsairs, Conan climbed into the fisherman’s boat.

“Wait for me until dawn,” he said. “If I haven’t returned by then, I never will. Head south to your homes.”

As he climbed over the rail, his men let out a mournful wail at his departure, until he stuck his head back up to curse them into silence. Then, dropping into the boat, he grabbed the oars and sent the little craft speeding over the waves faster than its owner had ever rowed it.

The harbor of Khemi lay between two great points of land that jutted into the sea. He rounded the southern point, where the massive black fortresses rose like a man-made mountain, and entered the harbor just at dusk, when there was still enough light for the watchers to recognize a fisherman’s boat and cloak, but not enough to notice any suspicious details. Unchallenged, he moved among the great black war galleys lying silent and unlit at anchor, and pulled up to a wide flight of stone steps that rose from the water’s edge. There he tied his boat to an iron ring set in the stone, as many other small boats were tied. There was nothing unusual about a fisherman leaving his boat there. No one but a fisherman would have any use for such a craft, and they didn’t steal from each other.

No one gave him more than a passing glance as he climbed the long steps, carefully avoiding the torches that burned at intervals above the dark, rippling water. He looked like an ordinary fisherman returning empty-handed after a fruitless day along the coast. If anyone had looked closely, they might have noticed that his stride was a bit too springy and confident, his posture too upright for a humble fisherman. But he moved quickly, keeping to the shadows, and the common people of Stygia were no more given to close observation than those of any other land.

In build, he wasn’t unlike the warrior caste of the Stygians, who were tall and muscular. Sun-bronzed, he was nearly as dark as many of them. His black, square-cut hair, held by a copper band, added to the resemblance. What set him apart were the subtle differences in his gait, his foreign features, and his blue eyes.

Still, the cloak was a good disguise, and he stayed in the shadows as much as possible, turning his head away when a native passed too close.

It was a dangerous game, and he knew he couldn’t keep up the deception for long. Khemi wasn’t like the sea ports of the Hyborian lands, where people of every race crowded the docks. The only foreigners here were black and Shemite slaves, and he looked like neither, though he resembled the Stygians more than them. Strangers weren’t welcome in Stygian cities; they were tolerated only as ambassadors or licensed traders, and even those weren’t allowed ashore after dark. Now there were no Hyborian ships in the harbor at all. A strange unease filled the city—a stirring of old ambitions, a whispering that only those who whispered could understand. Conan didn’t know it consciously, but his sharpened instincts sensed the unrest all around him.
