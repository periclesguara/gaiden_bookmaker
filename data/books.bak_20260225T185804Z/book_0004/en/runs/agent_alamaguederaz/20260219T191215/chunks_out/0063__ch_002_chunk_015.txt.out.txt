It was a strong position. His flanks couldn’t be turned, because that would mean climbing the steep, wooded hills under a storm of Bossonian arrows and swords. His camp lay directly behind him, in a narrow, steep-sided valley that was really just an extension of the Valley of Lions, rising to a higher level. He didn’t fear a surprise attack from the rear, since the hills behind him were full of refugees and broken men whose loyalty to him was beyond question.

But if his position was hard to attack, it was just as hard to escape from. It was both a fortress and a trap for the defenders—a desperate last stand for men who didn’t expect to live unless they won. The only possible retreat was through the narrow valley behind them.

Xaltotun climbed a hill on the left side of the valley, near its wide mouth. This hill rose higher than the others and was called the King’s Altar, though the reason for that name had long been forgotten. Only Xaltotun remembered, and his memory stretched back three thousand years.

He wasn’t alone. His two familiars—silent, hairy, secretive, and dark—were with him, carrying a young Aquilonian girl bound hand and foot. They laid her on an ancient stone that looked strangely like an altar, crowning the top of the hill. For centuries it had stood there, worn by wind and rain until many believed it was just a naturally shaped rock. But Xaltotun knew what it really was and why it stood there. The familiars slipped away, their bent backs like silent gnomes, and Xaltotun stood alone beside the altar, his dark beard blowing in the wind as he looked down over the valley.

He could see all the way back to the winding Shirki River and up into the hills beyond the valley’s head. He could see the gleaming wedge of steel drawn up at the top of the terraces, the helmets of the archers glinting among the rocks and bushes, the silent knights motionless on their horses, their pennons streaming above their helmets, their lances rising like a forest of spears.

Looking the other way, he could see the long, tight ranks of the Nemedians moving in shining lines of steel into the mouth of the valley. Behind them stretched the bright pavilions of the lords and knights and the dull tents of the common soldiers, reaching almost to the river.

Like a river of molten steel, the Nemedian host flowed into the valley, the great scarlet dragon banner rippling above it. First came the bowmen, marching in even ranks, crossbows half raised, bolts loaded, fingers on the triggers. Behind them came the pikemen, and behind those the real strength of the army—the mounted knights, banners unfurled in the wind, lances lifted, riding their great horses forward as if they were heading to a feast.

And higher up on the slopes, the smaller Aquilonian force stood grim and silent.

There were thirty thousand Nemedian knights, and as in most Hyborian nations, the chivalry was the sword of the army. The foot soldiers were used only to clear the way for the charge of the armored knights. There were twenty-one thousand of these footmen—pikemen and archers.

The bowmen began shooting as they advanced, keeping their formation, loosing their bolts with a whir and a snap. But the bolts fell short or bounced harmlessly off the overlapping shields of the Gundermen. Before the crossbowmen could get close enough to kill, the arching arrows of the Bossonians were tearing through their ranks.

After a short, useless exchange of fire, the Nemedian bowmen began to fall back in disorder. Their armor was light, and their weapons were no match for the Bossonian longbows. The western archers were sheltered by rocks and bushes. Besides, the Nemedian foot soldiers lacked the spirit of the horsemen, knowing they were being used only to clear the way for the knights.

The crossbowmen fell back, and through the gaps in their lines the pikemen advanced. These were mostly mercenaries, and their masters didn’t hesitate to sacrifice them. They were meant to cover the advance of the knights until the latter were close enough to strike. So while the crossbowmen fired from both flanks at long range, the pikemen marched straight into the deadly storm from above, and behind them the knights came on.

When the pikemen began to waver under the savage rain of death whistling down the slopes, a trumpet sounded, their ranks split to right and left, and through them thundered the armored knights.

They charged straight into a storm of deadly arrows. The long shafts found every gap in their armor and in the coverings of their horses. Horses scrambling up the grassy terraces reared and fell backward, crushing their riders. Steel-clad bodies littered the slopes. The charge faltered and rolled back.

Down in the valley, Amalric reformed his ranks. Tarascus was fighting with drawn sword under the scarlet dragon, but it was the Baron of Tor who commanded that day. Amalric cursed as he looked at the forest of lance tips visible above and beyond the helmets of the Gundermen. He had hoped his retreat would lure the knights into charging down the slopes after him, where they could be raked from both sides by his archers and overwhelmed by his cavalry. But they hadn’t moved. Camp servants brought water skins from the river. Knights took off their helmets and drenched their sweating heads. The wounded on the slopes screamed in vain for water. In the upper valley, springs supplied the defenders. They didn’t thirst that long, hot spring day.

On the King’s Altar, beside the ancient carved stone, Xaltotun watched the steel tide surge and break. The knights came on, plumes waving, lances dipping. Through a whistling storm of arrows they charged, only to crash like a thunderous wave against the bristling wall of spears and shields. Axes rose and fell above the plumed helmets, spears thrust upward, bringing down horses and riders. The pride of the Gundermen was as fierce as that of the knights. They were not fodder to be sacrificed for the glory of others. They were the finest infantry in the world, with a tradition that made their morale unshakable. The kings of Aquilonia had long known the value of unbreakable infantry. They held their formation firm; over their shining ranks flew the great lion banner, and at the tip of the wedge a giant figure in black armor roared and struck like a storm, wielding a blood-soaked axe that split steel and bone alike.
