The servants stopped their work and stared with open mouths as Conan crossed a wide, cool balcony that overlooked the courtyard and stepped into a broad hallway where the sea breeze flowed through. Halfway down, he heard the sound of a quill scratching and turned into a large room with many wide windows that looked out over the harbor.

Publio sat at a carved teakwood desk, writing on fine parchment with a golden quill. He was a short man with a large head and sharp dark eyes. His blue robe was made of the finest watered silk, trimmed with gold cloth, and a heavy gold chain hung from his thick white neck.

As the Cimmerian entered, the merchant looked up with an annoyed gesture. He froze mid-motion. His mouth fell open; he stared as if seeing a ghost from the past. Disbelief and fear flickered in his wide eyes.

“Well,” said Conan, “have you nothing to say to me, Publio?”

Publio wet his lips. “Conan!” he whispered in shock. “Mitra! Conan! Amra!”

“Who else?” The Cimmerian unclasped his cloak and tossed it, along with his gauntlets, onto the desk. “Well, man?” he said irritably. “Can’t you at least offer me a cup of wine? My throat’s dry with road dust.”

“Yes, wine!” echoed Publio automatically. His hand reached for a gong by habit, then jerked back as if from a hot coal, and he shuddered.

While Conan watched him with a trace of grim amusement, the merchant got up and quickly shut the door, first craning his neck up and down the corridor to make sure no servant was nearby. Then he returned, took a gold vessel of wine from a nearby table, and was about to fill a slender goblet when Conan impatiently took the vessel from him and, lifting it with both hands, drank deeply and with satisfaction.

“Yes, it’s Conan, no doubt about that,” muttered Publio. “Man, are you insane?”

“By Crom, Publio,” said Conan, lowering the vessel but keeping it in his hands, “you live in finer quarters than before. Only an Argossean merchant could turn a little waterfront shop that reeked of rotten fish and cheap wine into this kind of wealth.”

“The old days are gone,” muttered Publio, pulling his robe around himself with a slight involuntary shiver. “I’ve cast off the past like a worn-out cloak.”

“Well,” said Conan, “you can’t cast me off like an old cloak. I don’t want much from you, but what I do want, I’ll have. And you can’t refuse me. We had too many dealings in the old days. Do you think I don’t know this fine mansion was built on my sweat and blood? How many cargoes from my ships passed through your shop?”

“All merchants of Messantia have done business with sea-rovers at one time or another,” mumbled Publio nervously.

“But not with the black corsairs,” said Conan grimly.

“For Mitra’s sake, be quiet!” gasped Publio, sweat breaking out on his forehead. His fingers twitched at the gold embroidery of his robe.

“I just wanted to remind you,” said Conan. “Don’t be so frightened. You took plenty of risks back when you were fighting for your life and fortune in that filthy little shop by the docks, hand-in-hand with every buccaneer, smuggler, and pirate from here to the Barachan Isles. Prosperity must have made you soft.”

“I am respectable,” began Publio.

“Meaning you’re rich as hell,” snorted Conan. “Why? Why did you get rich so much faster than the others? Was it because you did big business in ivory and ostrich feathers, copper, hides, pearls, and gold ornaments from the coast of Kush? And how did you get them so cheaply, when other merchants were paying their weight in silver to the Stygians? I’ll remind you, if you’ve forgotten—you bought them from me, for far less than they were worth, and I took them from the tribes of the Black Coast and from Stygian ships—I and the black corsairs.”

“In Mitra’s name, stop!” begged Publio. “I haven’t forgotten. But what are you doing here? I’m the only man in Argos who knew that the king of Aquilonia was once Conan the buccaneer, back in the day. But word has come south that Aquilonia has fallen and the king is dead.”

“My enemies have killed me a hundred times with rumors,” grunted Conan. “Yet here I sit, drinking Kyros wine.” And he took another long swallow.

Lowering the vessel, now nearly empty, he said, “It’s a small thing I ask, Publio. I know you keep track of everything that happens in Messantia. I want to know if a Zingaran named Beloso—though he might be using another name—is in this city. He’s tall, lean, and dark like most of his people, and he’s likely trying to sell a very rare jewel.”

Publio shook his head. “I haven’t heard of such a man. But thousands come and go in Messantia. If he’s here, my agents will find him.”

“Good. Send them to look for him. In the meantime, have my horse cared for and bring me food here in this room.”

Publio agreed quickly, and Conan emptied the wine vessel, tossed it carelessly into a corner, and strode to a nearby window, unconsciously expanding his chest as he breathed in the salty air. He looked down at the winding streets along the waterfront. His eyes swept over the ships in the harbor with appreciation, then lifted toward the horizon, far beyond the bay, where the sea met the sky in a blue haze. His thoughts drifted beyond that horizon, to the golden southern seas under blazing suns, where there were no laws and life burned fiercely. Some drifting scent of spice or palm brought back vivid memories of strange coasts where mangroves grew and drums thundered, of ships locked in battle and decks running with blood, of smoke, fire, and the cries of slaughter. Lost in thought, he barely noticed when Publio quietly slipped out of the room.
