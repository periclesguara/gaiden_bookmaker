If he were discovered, his fate would be terrible. They would kill him simply for being a stranger; if they realized he was Amra, the pirate chief who had ravaged their coasts with sword and fire—an involuntary shiver ran through Conan’s broad shoulders. He did not fear human enemies, nor any death by blade or flame. But this was a dark land of sorcery and nameless horrors. People said Set the Old Serpent, long ago banished from the Hyborian races, still lurked in the shadows of the cryptic temples, and dreadful, mysterious deeds were done in the night-shrouded shrines.

He had moved away from the waterfront streets with their wide steps leading down to the water and was entering the long, shadowy streets of the city’s main section. There was no scene like those of any Hyborian city—no blaze of lamps and torches, no brightly dressed people laughing and strolling along the pavements, no shops and stalls open wide to display their goods.

Here, the stalls were shut at dusk. The only lights along the streets were torches, flaring and smoking at wide intervals. The people walking the streets were few; they moved quickly and silently, and their numbers dwindled as the night grew later. Conan found the scene gloomy and unreal—the silence of the people, their secretive haste, the great black stone walls rising on each side of the streets. There was a grim heaviness about Stygian architecture that felt overwhelming and oppressive.

Few lights showed anywhere except in the upper parts of the buildings. Conan knew that most of the people lay on the flat roofs, among the palms of artificial gardens beneath the stars. Somewhere, strange music murmured faintly. Occasionally, a bronze chariot rumbled over the paving stones, giving a brief glimpse of a tall, hawk-faced noble with a silk cloak wrapped around him and a gold band shaped like a rearing serpent’s head holding back his black hair; of the dark, naked charioteer bracing his muscular legs against the strain of the fierce Stygian horses.

But the people still walking the streets on foot were commoners—slaves, tradesmen, prostitutes, laborers—and they grew fewer as he went on. He was heading toward the temple of Set, where he knew he was likely to find the priest he sought. He believed he would recognize Thutothmes if he saw him, even though his only glimpse had been in the half-darkness of the Messantian alley. He was certain that the man he had seen there had been the priest. Only occultists high in the ranks of the dreadful Black Ring possessed the power of the black hand that killed with a touch; and only such a man would dare to defy Thoth-Amon, whom the western world knew only as a figure of terror and legend.
