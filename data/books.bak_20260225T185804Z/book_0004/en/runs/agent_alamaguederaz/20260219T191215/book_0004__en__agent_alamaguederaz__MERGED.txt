THE HOUR OF THE DRAGON  

By Robert E. Howard  

O Sleeper, Awake!  

The tall candles flickered, sending black shadows wavering along the walls, and the velvet tapestries rippled. Yet there was no wind in the room. Four men stood around the ebony table on which lay a green sarcophagus that gleamed like carved jade. In the raised right hand of each man, a strange black candle burned with an eerie greenish light. Outside, night reigned, and a lost wind moaned among the dark trees.  

Inside the chamber, silence was tense, broken only by the flickering of shadows, while four pairs of eyes, burning with intensity, were fixed on the long green coffin, across which cryptic hieroglyphs seemed to writhe as if alive in the unsteady light. The man standing at the foot of the sarcophagus leaned over it and moved his candle as though writing in the air, tracing a mystic symbol. Then he set the candle down in its black-gold holder at the foot of the case and, mumbling a formula his companions could not understand, thrust a broad white hand into his fur-trimmed robe. When he drew it out again, it was as if he held a ball of living fire in his palm.  

The other three gasped sharply, and the dark, powerful man standing at the head of the sarcophagus whispered, “The Heart of Ahriman!” The one holding the jewel lifted a quick hand for silence. Somewhere, a dog began to howl mournfully, and stealthy footsteps padded outside the barred and bolted door. But none of the men looked away from the coffin, over which the man in the ermine-trimmed robe now moved the blazing gem while muttering an incantation that was already ancient when Atlantis sank. The glare of the jewel dazzled their eyes so that they could not be sure of what they saw; but with a splintering crash, the carved lid of the sarcophagus burst outward as if from some irresistible force within, and the four men, bending eagerly forward, saw the occupant—a shriveled, withered shape, its dried brown limbs like dead wood showing through rotting bandages.  

“Bring that thing back?” muttered the small, dark man standing on the right with a short, sardonic laugh. “It’s ready to crumble at a touch. We’re fools—”  

“Quiet!” hissed the large man holding the jewel, his tone sharp with command. Sweat stood on his broad white forehead, and his eyes were wide and dilated. He leaned forward and, without touching the thing with his hand, laid the blazing jewel on the mummy’s chest. Then he drew back and watched with fierce intensity, his lips moving in a soundless chant.  

It was as if a globe of living fire flickered and burned on the dead, withered breast. The watchers sucked in their breath through clenched teeth. For as they watched, a horrifying transformation began. The withered shape in the sarcophagus was expanding, growing, lengthening. The bandages split and fell away in brown dust. The shriveled limbs swelled and straightened. Their dark hue began to fade.  

“By Mitra!” whispered the tall, yellow-haired man on the left. “He wasn’t a Stygian. That part, at least, was true.”  

Again, a trembling finger warned for silence. The hound outside had stopped howling. It whimpered as if in a nightmare, and then that sound, too, faded into silence. In that stillness, the yellow-haired man could clearly hear the heavy door straining, as if something outside were pushing hard against it. He half turned, his hand on his sword, but the man in the ermine robe hissed urgently, “Stay! Don’t break the chain! And for your life’s sake, don’t go to the door!”  

The yellow-haired man shrugged and turned back—then froze, staring. In the jade sarcophagus lay a living man: tall, strong, naked, pale-skinned, dark of hair and beard. He lay motionless, his eyes wide open, blank and unknowing as a newborn’s. On his chest, the great jewel smoldered and sparkled.  

The man in ermine reeled as if from the release of unbearable tension.  

“Ishtar!” he gasped. “It’s Xaltotun—and he lives! Valerius! Tarascus! Amalric! Do you see? Do you see? You doubted me—but I haven’t failed! We’ve stood at the open gates of hell tonight, and the shapes of darkness gathered close around us—yes, they followed him to the very door—but we’ve brought the great magician back to life.”  

“And damned our souls to eternal torment, no doubt,” muttered the small, dark man, Tarascus.  

The yellow-haired man, Valerius, laughed harshly.  

“What torment could be worse than life itself? We’re all damned from birth anyway. And who wouldn’t sell his wretched soul for a throne?”  

“There’s no awareness in his eyes, Orastes,” said the large man.  

“He’s been dead a long time,” answered Orastes. “He’s like one newly awakened. His mind is empty after the long sleep—no, he was dead, not sleeping. We’ve brought his spirit back across the voids of night and oblivion. I’ll speak to him.”  

He bent over the foot of the sarcophagus, fixing his gaze on the wide, dark eyes of the man within, and said slowly, “Awake, Xaltotun!”  

The man’s lips moved mechanically. “Xaltotun,” he repeated in a groping whisper.  

“You are Xaltotun!” exclaimed Orastes, like a hypnotist driving home his command. “You are Xaltotun of Python, in Acheron.”  

A dim flame flickered in the dark eyes.  

“I was Xaltotun,” he whispered. “I am dead.”  

“You are Xaltotun!” cried Orastes. “You are not dead! You live!”  

“I am Xaltotun,” came the eerie whisper. “But I am dead. In my house in Khemi, in Stygia, there I died.”  

“And the priests who poisoned you mummified your body with their dark arts, keeping all your organs intact!” exclaimed Orastes. “But now you live again! The Heart of Ahriman has restored your life, drawn your spirit back from the void of eternity.”  

“The Heart of Ahriman!” The flame of memory grew stronger. “The barbarians stole it from me!”

“He remembers,” muttered Orastes. “Lift him out of the case.”

The others obeyed hesitantly, as if they were afraid to touch the man they had brought back to life, and they didn’t feel any easier when they felt the solid, muscular flesh, warm with blood and life, under their fingers. But they lifted him onto the table, and Orastes dressed him in a strange dark velvet robe decorated with gold stars and crescent moons, then tied a gold cloth band around his temples to hold back the black wavy hair that fell to his shoulders. He let them do as they wished, saying nothing, not even when they placed him in a carved, throne-like chair with a tall ebony back, wide silver arms, and feet shaped like golden claws. He sat there motionless, and slowly intelligence returned to his dark eyes, making them deep, strange, and glowing. It was as if long-buried witchlights were rising slowly through midnight pools of darkness.

Orastes cast a cautious glance at his companions, who stood staring in grim fascination at their strange guest. Their iron nerves had endured an ordeal that might have driven weaker men insane. He knew he was dealing with no cowards, but with men whose courage matched their lawless ambitions and capacity for evil. He turned his attention back to the figure in the black chair. And at last, that one spoke.

“I remember,” he said in a strong, resonant voice, speaking Nemedian with a strange, ancient accent. “I am Xaltotun, who was high priest of Set in Python, which was in Acheron. The Heart of Ahriman—I dreamed I had found it again—where is it?”

Orastes placed it in his hand, and Xaltotun drew a deep breath as he stared into the depths of the terrible jewel burning in his grasp.

“They stole it from me long ago,” he said. “It is the red heart of the night, powerful to save or to damn. It came from far away and from long ago. While I held it, none could stand against me. But it was stolen from me, and Acheron fell, and I fled in exile into dark Stygia. I remember much, but much I have forgotten. I have been in a far land, across misty voids and gulfs and unlit seas. What year is this?”

Orastes answered him. “It is the waning of the Year of the Lion, three thousand years after the fall of Acheron.”

“Three thousand years!” murmured the other. “So long? Who are you?”

“I am Orastes, once a priest of Mitra. This man is Amalric, baron of Tor, in Nemedia; this other is Tarascus, younger brother of the king of Nemedia; and this tall man is Valerius, rightful heir to the throne of Aquilonia.”

“Why have you given me life?” demanded Xaltotun. “What do you want from me?”

The man was now fully alive and alert, his sharp eyes showing the activity of a clear, powerful mind. There was no hesitation or confusion in his manner. He came straight to the point, as one who knows that no one gives something for nothing. Orastes met him with equal frankness.

“We have opened the gates of hell tonight to free your soul and return it to your body because we need your help. We want to place Tarascus on the throne of Nemedia, and to win for Valerius the crown of Aquilonia. With your sorcery, you can help us.”

Xaltotun’s mind was twisted and full of unexpected turns.

“You must be deeply skilled in the arts yourself, Orastes, to have been able to restore my life. How is it that a priest of Mitra knows of the Heart of Ahriman and the spells of Skelos?”

“I am no longer a priest of Mitra,” answered Orastes. “I was cast out of my order because of my studies in black magic. If not for Amalric there, I might have been burned as a sorcerer.

“But that left me free to pursue my studies. I traveled in Zamora, in Vendhya, in Stygia, and through the haunted jungles of Khitai. I read the iron-bound books of Skelos and spoke with unseen beings in deep wells and faceless shapes in black, stinking jungles. I caught a glimpse of your sarcophagus in the demon-haunted crypts beneath the black, giant-walled temple of Set in the wilds of Stygia, and I learned the arts that could bring life back to your shriveled corpse. From decaying manuscripts I learned of the Heart of Ahriman. Then for a year I searched for its hiding place, and at last I found it.”

“Then why bother bringing me back to life?” demanded Xaltotun, his piercing gaze fixed on the priest. “Why didn’t you use the Heart to increase your own power?”

“Because no man today knows the secrets of the Heart,” answered Orastes. “Not even in legend do the arts survive that could unlock its full power. I knew it could restore life; of its deeper powers I am ignorant. I only used it to bring you back. It’s your knowledge we need. As for the Heart, you alone know its terrible secrets.”

Xaltotun shook his head, staring broodingly into the flaming depths.

“My necromantic knowledge is greater than all other men’s combined,” he said, “yet even I do not know the full power of the jewel. I did not use it in the old days; I guarded it, lest it be turned against me. At last it was stolen, and in the hands of a feathered shaman of the barbarians it overcame all my mighty sorcery. Then it vanished, and I was poisoned by the jealous priests of Stygia before I could learn where it had been hidden.”

“It was hidden in a cavern beneath the temple of Mitra in Tarantia,” said Orastes. “By roundabout means I discovered this, after I had found your remains in Set’s underground temple in Stygia.

“Zamorian thieves, partly protected by spells I learned from sources best left unnamed, stole your mummy case from under the very claws of those who guarded it in the dark, and by camel caravan, ship, and ox wagon it finally came to this city.

“Those same thieves—or rather, those of them who still lived after their dreadful quest—stole the Heart of Ahriman from its haunted cavern beneath the temple of Mitra, and all the skill of men and the spells of sorcerers nearly failed. One of them lived long enough to reach me and place the jewel in my hands before he died, raving and gibbering about what he had seen in that cursed crypt. The thieves of Zamora are the most faithful men alive to their trust. Even with my enchantments, only they could have stolen the Heart from where it had lain in demon-guarded darkness since the fall of Acheron, three thousand years ago.”

Xaltotun lifted his lion-like head and stared far into the distance, as if he were peering back through the lost centuries.  

“Three thousand years!” he muttered. “Set! Tell me what has happened in the world.”  

“The barbarians who destroyed Acheron founded new kingdoms,” said Orastes. “Where the empire once stretched, there now stand realms called Aquilonia, Nemedia, and Argos, named after the tribes that created them. The older kingdoms of Ophir, Corinthia, and western Koth, which had once been ruled by the kings of Acheron, regained their independence when the empire fell.”  

“And what became of the people of Acheron?” demanded Xaltotun. “When I fled into Stygia, Python was in ruins, and all the great purple-towered cities of Acheron were smeared with blood and crushed under the sandals of the barbarians.”  

“In the hills, small groups of people still claim descent from Acheron,” answered Orastes. “But for the most part, the tide of my barbarian ancestors swept over them and wiped them out. They—my ancestors—had suffered greatly under the kings of Acheron.”  

A grim, terrible smile twisted the Pythonian’s lips.  

“Yes! Many a barbarian, both man and woman, died screaming on the altar beneath this hand. I’ve seen their heads piled high into a pyramid in the great square of Python when the kings returned from the west with their plunder and naked captives.”  

“Yes. And when the day of vengeance came, the sword showed no mercy. So Acheron vanished, and purple-towered Python became only a memory of forgotten days. But the younger kingdoms rose from the ruins of the empire and grew strong. And now we have brought you back to help us rule these kingdoms, which, though less strange and marvelous than Acheron of old, are still rich and powerful—worth fighting for. Look!” Orastes unrolled before the stranger a map skillfully drawn on vellum.  

Xaltotun studied it, then shook his head in confusion.  

“The very shape of the land has changed. It’s like something familiar seen in a dream, strangely distorted.”  

“Nevertheless,” said Orastes, tracing with his finger, “here is Belverus, the capital of Nemedia, where we are now. Here are the borders of Nemedia. To the south and southeast lie Ophir and Corinthia, to the east Brythunia, and to the west Aquilonia.”  

“This is the map of a world I do not know,” said Xaltotun softly, but Orastes did not miss the dark, burning hatred that flickered in his eyes.  

“It’s a map you will help us change,” said Orastes. “Our first goal is to place Tarascus on the throne of Nemedia. We want to do this without conflict, in such a way that no suspicion falls on Tarascus. We don’t want the land torn apart by civil war; we must keep our strength for the conquest of Aquilonia.  

“If King Nimed and his sons were to die naturally—say, in a plague—Tarascus would ascend the throne as the next heir, peacefully and without opposition.”  

Xaltotun nodded silently, and Orastes went on.  

“The other task will be harder. We cannot put Valerius on the Aquilonian throne without war, and that kingdom is a formidable enemy. Its people are a tough, warlike race, hardened by constant battles with the Picts, Zingarians, and Cimmerians. For five hundred years, Aquilonia and Nemedia have fought off and on, and the advantage has always gone to the Aquilonians.  

“Their current king is the most famous warrior among the western nations. He’s a foreigner, an adventurer who seized the crown by force during a time of civil unrest, strangling King Namedides with his own hands right on the throne. His name is Conan, and no man can stand against him in battle.  

“Valerius is now the rightful heir to the throne. He was driven into exile by his royal kinsman Namedides and has been away from his homeland for years, but he is of the old royal blood, and many of the barons would secretly welcome Conan’s downfall, for Conan is a commoner, with no royal or even noble lineage. But the common people are loyal to him, as are the nobles of the outer provinces. Still, if his armies were defeated in the battle that must come, and Conan himself slain, I think it would not be difficult to place Valerius on the throne. Indeed, with Conan dead, the only center of power would be gone. He is not part of a dynasty—he is only a lone adventurer.”  

“I wish I could see this king,” mused Xaltotun, glancing toward a silvery mirror that formed one of the wall panels. The mirror cast no reflection, but Xaltotun’s expression showed he understood its purpose, and Orastes nodded with the pride of a craftsman recognized by a master of his art.  

“I’ll try to show him to you,” he said. Sitting before the mirror, he stared deeply into its surface, where a dim shadow began to take shape.  

It was eerie, but those watching knew it was only the reflection of Orastes’ thoughts, made visible in the mirror as a wizard’s thoughts appear in a magic crystal. It floated hazily, then suddenly sharpened into vivid clarity—a tall man, broad-shouldered and deep-chested, with a thick, corded neck and powerful limbs. He wore silk and velvet, with the golden lions of Aquilonia embroidered on his rich tunic, and the crown of Aquilonia gleamed on his square-cut black hair. Yet the great sword at his side seemed more natural to him than the royal finery. His brow was low and broad, his eyes a volcanic blue that burned with inner fire. His dark, scarred, almost sinister face was that of a warrior, and his velvet garments could not hide the hard, dangerous lines of his body.  

“That man is no Hyborian!” exclaimed Xaltotun.  

“No; he’s a Cimmerian, one of those wild tribesmen who live in the gray northern hills.”  

“I fought his ancestors long ago,” muttered Xaltotun. “Not even the kings of Acheron could conquer them.”  

“They are still a terror to the southern nations,” said Orastes. “He is a true son of that savage race, and so far, he has proven himself unconquerable.”

Xaltotun didn’t answer; he sat staring down at the pool of living fire that shimmered in his hand. Outside, the hound howled again—long and shuddering.

2 - A BLACK WIND BLOWS  
A Black Wind Blows  

The Year of the Dragon began with war, plague, and unrest. The black plague stalked through the streets of Belverus, striking down the merchant at his stall, the serf in his hovel, and the knight at his banquet table. The healers’ arts were useless against it. People said it had come from hell itself as punishment for their pride and lust. It was as swift and deadly as a viper’s strike. The victim’s body turned purple, then black, and within minutes he collapsed, dying, the stench of his own decay filling his nostrils before death even tore his soul from his rotting flesh. A hot, roaring wind blew endlessly from the south, and the crops withered in the fields, the cattle sank and died where they stood.

People cried out to Mitra and cursed the king, for throughout the kingdom whispers spread that the king secretly indulged in vile practices and obscene debaucheries within the darkness of his palace. Then death itself stalked that palace, grinning, surrounded by the swirling vapors of the plague. In one night the king and his three sons died, and the drums that thundered their funeral dirge drowned out the grim, ominous bells that rang from the carts lumbering through the streets, collecting the rotting dead.

That night, just before dawn, the hot wind that had blown for weeks stopped rustling the silken curtains. From the north rose a great wind that roared among the towers, with crashing thunder, blinding lightning, and driving rain. But when dawn came, it shone clean, green, and clear; the scorched earth clothed itself in grass, the thirsty crops sprang up again, and the plague was gone—its foul miasma swept from the land by the mighty wind.

People said the gods were satisfied because the evil king and his offspring were dead, and when his young brother Tarascus was crowned in the great coronation hall, the people cheered until the towers shook, hailing the monarch upon whom the gods smiled.

Such waves of joy and celebration often lead to wars of conquest. So no one was surprised when it was announced that King Tarascus had declared the truce made by the late king with their western neighbors void, and was gathering his armies to invade Aquilonia. His reasoning was open; his motives, loudly proclaimed, gave his actions the glow of a holy cause. He claimed to champion Valerius, the “rightful heir to the throne.” He came, he said, not as Aquilonia’s enemy, but as its friend—to free its people from the tyranny of a usurper and a foreigner.

If there were cynical smiles in some corners, and whispers about the king’s good friend Amalric—whose vast personal fortune seemed to be pouring into the nearly empty royal treasury—they went ignored amid the general wave of zeal and Tarascus’s popularity. If any shrewd minds suspected that Amalric was the true ruler of Nemedia behind the scenes, they were careful not to voice such dangerous thoughts. And the war went forward with enthusiasm.

The king and his allies marched west at the head of fifty thousand men—knights in shining armor with pennons streaming above their helmets, pikemen in steel caps and brigandines, crossbowmen in leather jerkins. They crossed the border, captured a frontier castle, and burned three mountain villages. Then, in the valley of the Valkia, ten miles west of the border, they met the forces of Conan, King of Aquilonia—forty-five thousand knights, archers, and men-at-arms, the pride of Aquilonian strength and chivalry. Only the knights of Poitain, under Prospero, had not yet arrived, for they had far to ride from the southwestern corner of the kingdom. Tarascus had struck without warning. His invasion followed immediately after his proclamation, without any formal declaration of war.

The two armies faced each other across a wide, shallow valley, with rugged cliffs and a narrow stream winding through reeds and willows down the middle. The camp followers from both sides came to the stream for water, shouting insults and throwing stones across at each other. The last rays of the sun gleamed on the golden banner of Nemedia, with its scarlet dragon, fluttering in the breeze above King Tarascus’s pavilion on a rise near the eastern cliffs. But the shadow of the western cliffs spread like a vast purple shroud across the tents and the army of Aquilonia, and over the black banner with its golden lion that flew above King Conan’s pavilion.

All night long, fires burned the length of the valley, and the wind carried the sound of trumpets, the clash of arms, and the sharp challenges of sentries pacing their horses along both sides of the willow-lined stream.

It was in the darkness before dawn that King Conan stirred on his couch—a pile of silks and furs thrown on a dais—and woke. He sat up suddenly, crying out sharply and grabbing his sword. Pallantides, his commander, rushed in at the cry and saw his king sitting upright, hand on his hilt, sweat dripping from his strangely pale face.

“Your Majesty!” exclaimed Pallantides. “Is something wrong?”

“What of the camp?” demanded Conan. “Are the guards posted?”

“Five hundred horsemen patrol the stream, Your Majesty,” answered the general. “The Nemedians haven’t moved against us in the night. They wait for dawn, as we do.”

“By Crom,” muttered Conan. “I woke with the feeling that doom was creeping toward me in the dark.”

He stared up at the great golden lamp that cast a soft glow over the velvet hangings and carpets of the great tent. They were alone; not even a slave or page slept on the carpeted floor. But Conan’s eyes blazed as they always did in the face of great danger, and the sword trembled in his hand. Pallantides watched him uneasily. Conan seemed to be listening.

“Listen!” hissed the king. “Did you hear that? A sneaky step!”  

“Seven knights guard your tent, Your Majesty,” said Pallantides. “No one could come near without being challenged.”  

“Not outside,” growled Conan. “It sounded like it came from inside the tent.”  

Pallantides quickly looked around, startled. The velvet curtains blended with the shadows in the corners, but if anyone else had been in the pavilion, the general would have seen them. He shook his head again.  

“There’s no one here, sire. You’re sleeping in the middle of your army.”  

“I’ve seen death strike a king in the middle of thousands,” muttered Conan. “Something that walks on invisible feet and can’t be seen—”  

“Maybe you were dreaming, Your Majesty,” said Pallantides, a bit uneasy.  

“So I was,” grunted Conan. “And it was a devilish dream, too. I walked again all the long, weary roads I traveled on my way to becoming king.”  

He fell silent, and Pallantides stared at him without speaking. The king was a mystery to the general, as he was to most of his civilized subjects. Pallantides knew that Conan had walked many strange paths in his wild, eventful life, and had been many things before fate placed him on the throne of Aquilonia.  

“I saw again the battlefield where I was born,” said Conan, resting his chin moodily on a massive fist. “I saw myself in a panther-skin loincloth, throwing my spear at mountain beasts. I was a mercenary swordsman again, a hetman of the kozaki who live along the Zaporoska River, a corsair raiding the coasts of Kush, a pirate of the Barachan Isles, a chief of the Himelian hillmen. I’ve been all these things, and I dreamed of them all; all the shapes I’ve worn passed before me like an endless procession, and their feet beat out a funeral rhythm in the echoing dust.  

“But through all my dreams moved strange, veiled figures and ghostly shadows, and a distant voice mocked me. And near the end I seemed to see myself lying on this dais in my tent, and a shape bent over me, cloaked and hooded. I lay unable to move, and then the hood fell back and a rotting skull grinned down at me. That’s when I woke up.”  

“That’s an evil dream, Your Majesty,” said Pallantides, suppressing a shudder. “But nothing more.”  

Conan shook his head, more doubtful than dismissive. He came from a barbarian race, and the superstitions and instincts of his heritage lay close beneath the surface of his mind.  

“I’ve dreamed many evil dreams,” he said, “and most of them meant nothing. But by Crom, this one wasn’t like the others! I wish this battle were already fought and won, because I’ve had a grim feeling ever since King Nimed died of the black plague. Why did it stop when he died?”  

“Men say he sinned—”  

“Men are fools, as always,” grunted Conan. “If the plague struck everyone who sinned, then by Crom there wouldn’t be enough left alive to count! Why would the gods—who the priests claim are just—kill five hundred peasants, merchants, and nobles before killing the king, if the whole plague was meant for him? Were the gods striking blindly, like swordsmen in a fog? By Mitra, if I aimed my blows that poorly, Aquilonia would have had a new king long ago.  

“No! The black plague isn’t a normal disease. It hides in Stygian tombs and is only brought forth by sorcerers. I was a swordsman in Prince Almuric’s army when we invaded Stygia, and of his thirty thousand men, fifteen thousand died by Stygian arrows, and the rest by the black plague that swept over us like a wind from the south. I was the only one who lived.”  

“Yet only five hundred died in Nemedia,” argued Pallantides.  

“Whoever summoned it knew how to stop it at will,” answered Conan. “So I know it was planned and evil. Someone called it forth, someone dismissed it when the job was done—when Tarascus was safe on the throne and being hailed as the savior of the people from the wrath of the gods. By Crom, I sense a dark, cunning mind behind all this. What about this stranger people say advises Tarascus?”  

“He wears a veil,” answered Pallantides. “They say he’s a foreigner—a stranger from Stygia.”  

“A stranger from Stygia!” repeated Conan, scowling. “A stranger from hell, more like!—Ha! What’s that?”  

“The trumpets of the Nemedians!” exclaimed Pallantides. “And listen, our own sound right after them! Dawn is breaking, and the captains are forming the armies for battle! Mitra be with them, for many won’t see the sun set behind the cliffs.”  

“Send my squires to me!” exclaimed Conan, rising quickly and throwing off his velvet night-robe; he seemed to have forgotten his dark thoughts at the prospect of action. “Go to the captains and make sure everything is ready. I’ll join you as soon as I put on my armor.”  

Many of Conan’s habits were baffling to the civilized people he ruled, and one of them was his insistence on sleeping alone in his chamber or tent. Pallantides hurried from the pavilion, his armor clanking—the same armor he had put on at midnight after a few hours of sleep. He glanced swiftly over the camp, which was now coming alive with movement, mail clinking and men moving dimly in the uncertain light among the long rows of tents. Stars still glimmered faintly in the western sky, but long pink streaks stretched along the eastern horizon, and against them the dragon banner of Nemedia unfurled its rippling silk folds.  

Pallantides turned toward a smaller tent nearby, where the royal squires slept. They were already tumbling out, roused by the trumpets. And as Pallantides called to them to hurry, he froze in shock at a deep, fierce shout and the sound of a heavy blow inside the king’s tent, followed by the sickening crash of a falling body. Then came a low laugh that turned the general’s blood to ice.  

Echoing the cry, Pallantides spun around and rushed back into the pavilion. He cried out again when he saw Conan’s massive body stretched out on the carpet. The king’s great two-handed sword lay near his hand, and a shattered tent pole showed where his blow had struck. Pallantides drew his sword and glared around the tent, but nothing met his gaze. Except for the king and himself, it was empty—just as it had been when he left.

“Your Majesty!” Pallantides dropped to one knee beside the fallen giant.

Conan’s eyes were open; they blazed with full awareness and recognition. His lips twisted, but no sound came out. He seemed unable to move.

Voices sounded outside. Pallantides rose quickly and stepped to the door. The royal squires and one of the knights guarding the tent stood there.

“We heard a noise inside,” said the knight apologetically. “Is everything all right with the king?”

Pallantides studied him intently.

“No one has entered or left the pavilion tonight?”

“No one except you, my lord,” answered the knight, and Pallantides had no reason to doubt his honesty.

“The king stumbled and dropped his sword,” said Pallantides shortly. “Return to your post.”

As the knight turned away, the general discreetly motioned to the five royal squires, and when they had followed him inside, he closed the flap tightly. They turned pale at the sight of the king lying on the carpet, but Pallantides’ quick gesture stopped their cries.

The general bent over him again, and again Conan tried to speak. The veins in his temples and the cords in his neck bulged with the effort, and he lifted his head off the ground. At last a voice came, mumbling and barely understandable.

“The thing—the thing in the corner!”

Pallantides lifted his head and looked around fearfully. He saw only the pale faces of the squires in the lamplight and the velvet shadows lurking along the pavilion walls. That was all.

“There’s nothing here, Your Majesty,” he said.

“It was there, in the corner,” muttered the king, tossing his lion-maned head from side to side as he struggled to rise. “A man—or at least he looked like a man—wrapped in rags like a mummy’s bandages, with a rotting cloak around him and a hood. All I could see were his eyes as he crouched there in the shadows. I thought he was a shadow himself until I saw those eyes. They were like black jewels.

“I lunged at him and swung my sword, but I missed completely—how, only Crom knows—and shattered that pole instead. He grabbed my wrist as I stumbled off balance, and his fingers burned like hot iron. All my strength left me, and the floor rose up and hit me like a club. Then he was gone, and I was down, and—curse him!—I can’t move! I’m paralyzed!”

Pallantides lifted the giant’s hand, and his skin crawled. On the king’s wrist were the blue marks of long, thin fingers. What kind of hand could grip so hard as to leave its print on that thick wrist? Pallantides remembered the low laugh he had heard as he rushed into the tent, and cold sweat broke out on his skin. It had not been Conan who laughed.

“This is something evil!” whispered a trembling squire. “Men say the children of darkness fight for Tarascus!”

“Be silent!” ordered Pallantides sternly.

Outside, dawn was dimming the stars. A light wind rose from the peaks and carried the fanfare of a thousand trumpets. At the sound, a convulsive shudder ran through the king’s massive body. Again the veins in his temples bulged as he struggled to break the invisible chains that held him down.

“Put my armor on me and tie me into my saddle,” he whispered. “I’ll lead the charge yet!”

Pallantides shook his head, and a squire tugged at his skirt.

“My lord, we’re lost if the army learns the king has been struck down! Only he could have led us to victory today.”

“Help me lift him onto the dais,” said the general.

They obeyed, laying the helpless giant on the furs and spreading a silken cloak over him. Pallantides turned to the five squires and studied their pale faces for a long moment before speaking.

“Our lips must remain sealed forever about what happens in this tent,” he said at last. “The kingdom of Aquilonia depends on it. One of you go and bring me the officer Valannus, captain of the Pellian spearmen.”

The chosen squire bowed and hurried from the tent, while Pallantides stood staring down at the stricken king. Outside, trumpets blared, drums thundered, and the roar of the masses rose with the growing dawn. Soon the squire returned with the officer Pallantides had named—a tall, broad, powerful man, built much like the king. Like Conan, he had thick black hair, but his eyes were gray and his features were different.

“The king has been struck by a strange illness,” said Pallantides briefly. “A great honor is yours; you are to wear his armor and ride at the head of the army today. No one must know it isn’t the king who rides.”

“It’s an honor for which a man would gladly give his life,” stammered the captain, overwhelmed by the command. “Mitra grant that I don’t fail in this great trust!”

And while the fallen king stared with burning eyes that reflected the bitter rage and humiliation eating at his heart, the squires stripped Valannus of his mail shirt, helmet, and leg armor, and dressed him in Conan’s black plate armor, with the visored salade and the dark plumes nodding above the wyvern crest. Over all they placed the silken surcoat with the royal lion embroidered in gold on the chest, and they belted him with a broad gold-buckled belt that held a jewel-hilted broadsword in a cloth-of-gold scabbard. While they worked, trumpets blared outside, weapons clanged, and across the river rose a deep roar as squadron after squadron moved into position.

Fully armed, Valannus dropped to one knee and bowed his plumes before the figure lying on the dais.

“Lord King, may Mitra grant that I do not dishonor the armor I wear today!”

“Bring me Tarascus’ head and I’ll make you a baron!” In the heat of his anguish, Conan’s thin layer of civilization had fallen away. His eyes blazed, his teeth ground in fury and bloodlust, as savage as any tribesman from the Cimmerian hills.

The Aquilonian army was drawn up—long, serried lines of pikemen and horsemen in gleaming steel—when a giant figure in black armor emerged from the royal pavilion. As he swung into the saddle of the black stallion held by four squires, a roar that shook the mountains rose from the host. They raised their blades and thundered their cheers for their warrior king—knights in gold-chased armor, pikemen in mail coats and helmets, archers in leather jerkins with longbows in their left hands.

The army on the far side of the valley was on the move, trotting down the long, gentle slope toward the river; their steel gleamed through the morning mist swirling around their horses’ hooves.  

The Aquilonian army advanced at an easy pace to meet them. The steady tramp of the armored horses made the ground shake. Banners unfurled their long silk folds in the morning wind; lances swayed like a forest of spears, dipping and rising, their pennons fluttering around them.  

Ten men-at-arms—grim, silent veterans who knew how to keep their mouths shut—guarded the royal pavilion. One squire stood inside the tent, peering out through a slit in the doorway. Except for the few who were in on the secret, no one in the vast army knew that it wasn’t Conan riding the great stallion at the head of the host.  

The Aquilonian army had taken the usual formation: the strongest part was the center, made up entirely of heavily armed knights; the wings consisted of smaller groups of cavalry, mostly mounted men-at-arms, supported by pikemen and archers. The archers were Bossonians from the western borders, sturdy men of medium height, wearing leather jackets and iron helmets.  

The Nemedian army advanced in a similar formation, and the two forces moved toward the river, their wings ahead of their centers. In the middle of the Aquilonian host, the great lion banner streamed its billowing black folds over the steel-clad figure on the black stallion.  

But on his dais in the royal pavilion, Conan groaned in torment and cursed with strange heathen oaths.  

“The armies are closing in,” said the squire, watching from the doorway. “Listen to the trumpets! Ha! The rising sun flashes off the lances and helmets until I’m blinded. It turns the river crimson—aye, and it’ll be truly crimson before this day is done!  

“The enemy has reached the river. Now arrows fly between the armies like stinging clouds that dim the sun. Ha! Well shot, bowmen! The Bossonians have the upper hand! Hear them shout!”  

Faintly, above the blare of trumpets and the clash of steel, the deep, fierce shout of the Bossonians reached the king’s ears as they drew and loosed in perfect rhythm.  

“Their archers are trying to keep ours busy while their knights ride into the river,” said the squire. “The banks aren’t steep; they slope gently to the water’s edge. The knights are coming on—they crash through the willows. By Mitra, the clothyard arrows find every gap in their armor! Horses and men go down, thrashing and struggling in the water. It’s not deep, and the current isn’t strong, but men are drowning there, dragged under by their armor and trampled by the frantic horses. Now the Aquilonian knights advance. They ride into the water and clash with the Nemedian knights. The water swirls around their horses’ bellies, and the clang of sword on sword is deafening.”  

“Crom!” Conan gasped in agony. Life was slowly returning to his veins, but he still couldn’t lift his massive body from the dais.  

“The wings are closing in,” said the squire. “Pikemen and swordsmen are fighting hand to hand in the stream, and behind them the archers keep shooting.  

“By Mitra, the Nemedian crossbowmen are taking a beating, and the Bossonians are arching their arrows high to drop among the rear ranks. The enemy’s center isn’t gaining an inch, and their wings are being pushed back from the river again.”  

“Crom, Ymir, and Mitra!” Conan raged. “Gods and devils, if only I could reach the battle—even if it meant dying with the first blow!”  

* * * * *  

Outside, through the long, hot day, the battle raged and thundered. The valley shook with charge and countercharge, with the hiss of arrows, and the crash of splintering shields and breaking lances. But the Aquilonian army held firm. Once they were driven back from the bank, but a counterattack—led by the black banner flying over the black stallion—won back the lost ground. Like an iron wall, they held the right bank of the stream, and at last the squire brought Conan the news that the Nemedians were retreating from the river.  

“Their wings are in chaos!” he cried. “Their knights are reeling back from the sword fight. But wait—what’s this? Your banner is moving—the center is crossing the river! By Mitra, Valannus is leading the army across!”  

“Fool!” groaned Conan. “It could be a trap. He should hold his ground; by dawn Prospero will be here with the Poitanian troops.”  

“The knights are riding into a storm of arrows!” shouted the squire. “But they don’t falter! They press on—they’ve crossed! They’re charging up the slope! Pallantides has thrown the wings across the river to support them! It’s all he can do. The lion banner dips and sways above the chaos.  

“The Nemedian knights are making a stand. They’re breaking! They’re falling back! Their left wing is in full flight, and our pikemen are cutting them down as they run! I see Valannus, fighting like a madman. He’s lost in the fury of battle. The men no longer look to Pallantides—they follow Valannus, thinking he’s Conan, since he rides with his visor closed.  

“But look! There’s method in his madness! He’s swinging wide of the Nemedian front with five thousand knights—the best of the army. The main Nemedian host is in confusion—and look! Their flank is guarded by cliffs, but there’s a pass left unguarded! It’s like a great cleft in the wall that opens again behind the Nemedian lines. By Mitra, Valannus sees it and seizes the chance! He’s driven their wing before him, and now he’s leading his knights toward that pass. They swing wide of the main battle, cut through a line of spearmen, and charge into the defile!”  

“An ambush!” cried Conan, struggling to rise.  

“No!” shouted the squire in triumph. “The whole Nemedian army is in plain sight! They’ve forgotten the pass! They never expected to be driven back that far. Oh, fool, fool Tarascus, to make such a blunder! Ah, I see lances and pennons pouring from the far mouth of the pass, beyond the Nemedian lines. They’ll strike those ranks from behind and crush them. Mitra, what’s this?”

He stumbled as the tent walls swayed like a drunkard. Far off, above the thunder of battle, rose a deep, booming roar that sounded indescribably menacing.  

“The cliffs are shaking!” screamed the squire. “Oh gods, what’s happening? The river’s bursting from its bed, the peaks are collapsing! The ground’s shaking—horses and armored riders are being thrown down! The cliffs! The cliffs are falling!”  

As he spoke, there came a grinding rumble and a thunderous crash, and the earth quivered. Over the roar of battle came screams of insane terror.  

“The cliffs have fallen!” cried the pale-faced squire. “They’ve crashed down into the gorge and crushed every living thing in it! I saw the lion banner wave for a moment in the dust and tumbling stones, and then it was gone! Ha, the Nemedians are shouting in triumph! And no wonder—they’ve wiped out five thousand of our bravest knights in one stroke—listen!”  

To Conan’s ears came a wild, swelling roar of voices, rising higher and higher in panic: “The king is dead! The king is dead! Run! Run! The king is dead!”  

“Liars!” gasped Conan. “Dogs! Cowards! By Crom, if I could only stand—if I could just crawl to the river with my sword in my teeth! What, boy, are they running?”  

“Yes!” sobbed the squire. “They’re spurring for the river—they’re broken, swept away like foam before a storm. I see Pallantides trying to stop them—he’s down, trampled by the horses! They’re plunging into the river—knights, archers, pikemen—all mixed together in one mad flood of ruin. The Nemedians are right behind them, cutting them down like wheat.”  

“But they’ll rally on this side of the river!” shouted the king. With an effort that made sweat stream down his temples, he pushed himself up on his elbows.  

“No!” cried the squire. “They can’t! They’re shattered—routed! Oh gods, that I should live to see this day!”  

Then he remembered his duty and shouted to the men-at-arms who stood grimly watching their comrades flee. “Get a horse, quick, and help me lift the king onto it. We can’t stay here.”  

But before they could obey, the first wave of the storm broke over them. Knights, spearmen, and archers fled through the tents, tripping over ropes and baggage, and mixed among them were Nemedian riders, slashing right and left at anyone not their own. Tent ropes were cut, fires sprang up everywhere, and looting had already begun. The grim guards around Conan’s tent died where they stood, striking and stabbing, and over their torn bodies thundered the hooves of the conquerors.  

But the squire had pulled the tent flap shut, and in the chaos of slaughter no one realized the pavilion still held someone inside. The rout and pursuit swept past and roared away up the valley, and soon the squire looked out to see a group of men approaching the royal tent with clear intent.  

“Here comes the king of Nemedia with four companions and his squire,” he said. “He’ll accept your surrender, my lord—”  

“Surrender, my ass!” growled the king.  

He had forced himself up to a sitting position. He swung his legs painfully off the dais and struggled to his feet, swaying like a drunk. The squire rushed to help him, but Conan shoved him aside.  

“Give me that bow!” he snarled, pointing to a longbow and quiver hanging from a tent pole.  

“But your Majesty!” cried the squire in distress. “The battle’s lost! It would be fitting for one of royal blood to yield with dignity!”  

“I’ve got no royal blood,” Conan growled. “I’m a barbarian, the son of a blacksmith.”  

He tore the bow and an arrow from the pole and staggered toward the tent opening. His appearance was so fierce—bare except for short leather breeches and a sleeveless shirt open over his broad, hairy chest, his huge muscles bulging, his blue eyes blazing under his tangled black hair—that the squire shrank back, more afraid of his king than of the entire Nemedian army.  

Staggering on wide, unsteady legs, Conan ripped open the tent flap and lurched out under the canopy. The king of Nemedia and his men had dismounted and stopped short, staring in astonishment at the sight before them.  

“Here I am, you jackals!” roared the Cimmerian. “I’m the king! Death to you, dog-brothers!”  

He drew the arrow to the head and loosed it; the shaft buried itself in the chest of the knight standing beside Tarascus. Conan hurled the bow at the king of Nemedia.  

“Damn my shaky hand! Come in and take me if you dare!”  

Reeling backward on unsteady legs, he fell with his shoulders against a tent pole, and propped there, he lifted his great sword with both hands.  

“By Mitra, it really is the king!” swore Tarascus. He glanced quickly around and laughed. “That other one was a jackal in his armor! In with you, dogs—take his head!”  

The three soldiers—men-at-arms wearing the royal guard’s emblem—charged the king, and one struck down the squire with a mace. The other two met worse fates. As the first rushed in, sword raised, Conan met him with a sweeping blow that sliced through his mail like cloth and cut off his arm and shoulder in one stroke. The corpse fell backward across his comrade’s legs. The man stumbled, and before he could recover, Conan’s great sword drove through him.  

Conan wrenched his blade free with a gasping breath and sagged back against the tent pole. His massive limbs trembled, his chest heaved, and sweat streamed down his face and neck. But his eyes blazed with savage triumph as he panted, “Why do you hang back, dog of Belverus? I can’t reach you—come in and die!”  

Tarascus hesitated, glanced at the last man-at-arms and his squire—a tall, grim man in black armor—and took a step forward. He was far smaller and weaker than the giant Cimmerian, but he wore full armor and was known throughout the western lands as a master swordsman. Yet his squire caught his arm.

“No, your Majesty, don’t throw your life away. I’ll call the archers to shoot this barbarian, the same way we shoot lions.”

Neither of them had noticed that a chariot had approached during the fight and had now stopped before them. But Conan saw it over their shoulders, and a strange chill crept down his spine. There was something oddly unnatural about the black horses pulling the chariot, but it was the person inside who caught the king’s attention.

He was a tall, powerfully built man, dressed in a long, plain silk robe. He wore a Shemitish headdress, and its lower folds hid his face except for his dark, magnetic eyes. The hands holding the reins, pulling the rearing horses back on their haunches, were pale but strong. Conan glared at the stranger, every primal instinct on edge. He could feel an aura of danger and power radiating from the veiled figure—a threat as real as the silent ripple of tall grass that marks a serpent’s passage.

“Hail, Xaltotun!” exclaimed Tarascus. “Here is the king of Aquilonia! He didn’t die in the landslide as we thought.”

“I know,” replied the other, not bothering to explain how he knew. “What do you plan to do now?”

“I’ll call the archers to kill him,” said the Nemedian. “As long as he lives, he’s a danger to us.”

“Even a dog can be useful,” said Xaltotun. “Take him alive.”

Conan gave a harsh laugh. “Come on and try!” he challenged. “If not for my treacherous legs, I’d chop you out of that chariot like a woodsman felling a tree. But you’ll never take me alive, damn you!”

“He speaks the truth, I fear,” said Tarascus. “The man’s a barbarian, with the mindless fury of a wounded tiger. Let me summon the archers.”

“Watch and learn wisdom,” said Xaltotun.

His hand slipped into his robe and came out holding something shiny—a gleaming sphere. He suddenly threw it at Conan. The Cimmerian contemptuously struck it aside with his sword—at the moment of contact there was a sharp explosion, a burst of blinding white light, and Conan fell senseless to the ground.

“Is he dead?” Tarascus asked, more as a statement than a question.

“No. He’s only unconscious. He’ll wake in a few hours. Have your men bind his arms and legs and lift him into my chariot.”

Tarascus gestured, and his men obeyed, grunting as they lifted the limp king into the chariot. Xaltotun threw a velvet cloak over Conan’s body, completely covering him from anyone who might look inside. He gathered the reins in his hands.

“I’m going to Belverus,” he said. “Tell Amalric I’ll come if he needs me. But with Conan out of the way and his army destroyed, sword and lance should be enough to finish the conquest. Prospero can’t have more than ten thousand men, and he’ll likely retreat to Tarantia when he hears of the battle. Say nothing to Amalric, Valerius, or anyone else about our capture. Let them believe Conan died in the rockslide.”

He stared at the man-at-arms for a long moment, until the soldier shifted uneasily under his gaze.

“What’s that around your waist?” Xaltotun demanded.

“My belt, my lord!” stammered the startled guardsman.

“You lie!” Xaltotun’s laugh was as merciless as a blade’s edge. “It’s a poisonous serpent! What a fool you are, to wear a snake around your waist!”

The man’s eyes widened in horror as he looked down—and to his utter terror, he saw the buckle of his belt rise up. It was a snake’s head! He saw its evil eyes, its dripping fangs, heard its hiss, and felt the vile thing coiling around his body. He screamed horribly and struck at it with his bare hand, felt its fangs sink into his flesh—and then he stiffened and fell heavily to the ground. Tarascus looked down at him without expression. All he saw was the leather belt and the buckle, its pointed tongue embedded in the man’s palm. Xaltotun turned his hypnotic gaze on Tarascus’s squire, and the man went pale and began to tremble, but the king intervened. “No, we can trust him.”

The sorcerer tightened the reins and turned the horses.

“Make sure this stays secret. If I’m needed, let Altaro, Orastes’ servant, summon me as I’ve taught him. I’ll be in your palace at Belverus.”

Tarascus raised his hand in salute, but his expression was grim as he watched the departing sorcerer.

“Why would he spare the Cimmerian?” whispered the frightened squire.

“I’m wondering that myself,” grunted Tarascus.

Behind the rumbling chariot, the dull roar of battle and pursuit faded into the distance. The setting sun edged the cliffs in scarlet flame, and the chariot rolled into the vast blue shadows rising from the east.

4 – “FROM WHAT HELL HAVE YOU CRAWLED?”

Conan knew nothing of that long ride in Xaltotun’s chariot. He lay like a dead man while the bronze wheels clattered over the stones of mountain roads, swished through the tall grass of fertile valleys, and finally, descending from the rugged heights, rumbled steadily along the broad white road that wound through the rich meadows toward the walls of Belverus.

Just before dawn, a faint spark of life stirred in him. He heard murmuring voices, the groan of heavy hinges. Through a slit in the cloak covering him, he saw, dimly lit by the red glare of torches, the great black arch of a gateway and the bearded faces of soldiers, the torchlight flashing off their spearheads and helmets.

“How went the battle, my lord?” asked an eager voice in the Nemedian tongue.

“Very well,” came the curt reply. “The king of Aquilonia lies dead, and his army is broken.”

A babble of excited voices rose, then was drowned out by the clatter of the chariot wheels on the paving stones. Sparks flew from beneath the rims as Xaltotun drove his horses through the arch. But Conan heard one of the guards mutter, “From beyond the border to Belverus between sunset and dawn! And the horses barely sweating! By Mitra, they—” Then silence swallowed the voices, leaving only the rhythmic clatter of hooves and wheels echoing down the shadowy street.

What he had heard registered in Conan’s mind, but it meant nothing to him. He was like a mindless machine that could hear and see but not understand. Sights and sounds drifted around him without meaning. He sank again into a deep stupor, only vaguely aware when the chariot stopped in a high-walled courtyard, and many hands lifted him out, carrying him up a winding stone staircase and down a long, dim hallway. Whispers, soft footsteps, and random noises swirled around him, distant and meaningless.  

But when he finally awoke, it was sudden and crystal clear. He remembered everything about the battle in the mountains and what had happened afterward, and he had a good idea of where he was.  

He lay on a velvet couch, dressed as he had been the day before, but his limbs were bound with chains even he could not break. The room around him was darkly magnificent, its walls covered with black velvet tapestries and the floor with thick purple carpets. There was no visible door or window, and a single gold lamp, intricately carved and hanging from the decorated ceiling, cast a strange, reddish light over everything.  

In that light, the figure sitting before him in a silver, throne-like chair looked unreal and dreamlike, its outline blurred by a thin, silken robe. Yet the man’s face was sharply clear—unnaturally so in that uncertain glow. It was as if a strange halo surrounded his head, throwing his bearded face into bold relief, making it the only solid, undeniable reality in that eerie, ghostly chamber.  

It was a striking face, with strong, sculpted features of classical beauty. But there was something unsettling about its calmness, a sense of knowledge beyond human understanding, of certainty beyond mortal confidence. And at the back of Conan’s mind, a faint feeling of recognition stirred. He knew he had never seen this man before, yet something about those features reminded him of someone—or something. It was like meeting in real life a figure that had haunted his nightmares.  

“Who are you?” demanded the king harshly, struggling to sit up despite his chains.  

“Men call me Xaltotun,” came the reply, in a deep, golden voice.  

“What place is this?” Conan asked next.  

“A chamber in the palace of King Tarascus, in Belverus.”  

Conan wasn’t surprised. Belverus, the capital, was also the largest Nemedian city near the border.  

“And where’s Tarascus?”  

“With the army.”  

“Well,” Conan growled, “if you plan to kill me, why don’t you just do it and be done with it?”  

“I didn’t save you from the king’s archers just to murder you in Belverus,” said Xaltotun.  

“What in hell did you do to me?” Conan demanded.  

“I shattered your consciousness,” Xaltotun replied. “How, you wouldn’t understand. Call it black magic, if you like.”  

Conan had already guessed as much, but his mind was turning to something else.  

“I think I see why you spared me,” he rumbled. “Amalric wants to keep me alive as a counter to Valerius, in case the impossible happens and Valerius becomes king of Aquilonia. Everyone knows the baron of Tor is behind this plan to put Valerius on my throne. And if I know Amalric, he doesn’t mean for Valerius to be anything more than a puppet—just like Tarascus is now.”  

“Amalric knows nothing about your capture,” said Xaltotun. “Neither does Valerius. Both believe you died at Valkia.”  

Conan’s eyes narrowed as he stared silently at the man.  

“I knew there was a mind behind all this,” he muttered, “but I thought it was Amalric’s. Are Amalric, Tarascus, and Valerius all just puppets dancing on your strings? Who are you really?”  

“What does it matter? If I told you, you wouldn’t believe me. What if I said I could put you back on the throne of Aquilonia?”  

Conan’s eyes burned like a wolf’s.  

“What’s your price?”  

“Obedience to me.”  

“Go to hell with your offer!” Conan snarled. “I’m no puppet. I won my crown with my sword. And besides, you can’t just buy and sell the throne of Aquilonia as you please. The kingdom isn’t conquered; one battle doesn’t decide a war.”  

“You fight against more than swords,” said Xaltotun. “Was it a mortal’s blade that struck you down in your tent before the battle? No—it was a creature of darkness, a wanderer from the outer void, whose fingers burned with the frozen cold of the black gulfs, freezing the blood in your veins and the marrow in your bones. Cold so intense it seared your flesh like white-hot iron!  

“Was it mere chance that made the man wearing your armor lead his knights into the pass—chance that brought the cliffs crashing down upon them?”  

Conan glared at him without speaking, a chill creeping along his spine. Wizards and sorcerers filled his barbaric legends, and anyone could see this was no ordinary man. Conan felt something about him that set him apart—an alien aura of time and space, a sense of immense and sinister age. But his stubborn spirit refused to yield.  

“The fall of the cliffs was chance,” he said defiantly. “The charge into the pass was what any man would have done.”  

“Not so. You would never have led a charge into it. You would have suspected a trap. You would never have crossed the river at all until you were sure the Nemedian retreat was real. No hypnotic suggestion could have invaded your mind, even in the madness of battle, to drive you into the snare as it did the lesser man who pretended to be you.”  

“Then if all this was planned,” Conan grunted skeptically, “if it was all a scheme to trap my army, why didn’t your ‘creature of darkness’ just kill me in my tent?”  

“Because I wanted you alive. It took no sorcery to foresee that Pallantides would send another man out in your armor. I wanted you alive and unharmed. You may have a place in my plans. There is a living power in you greater than the skill and cunning of my allies. You are a dangerous enemy—but you might make a great servant.”

Conan spat fiercely at the word, and Xaltotun, ignoring his anger, took a crystal globe from a nearby table and set it before him. He didn’t rest it on anything, nor did he hold it up, yet it hung motionless in midair, as solid as if it stood on an iron pedestal. Conan snorted at this display of sorcery, but he couldn’t help being impressed.  

“Do you want to know what’s happening in Aquilonia?” Xaltotun asked.  

Conan didn’t answer, but the sudden stiffness in his posture betrayed his interest.  

Xaltotun gazed into the cloudy depths of the globe and spoke. “It’s now the evening of the day after the battle of Valkia. Last night the main body of the army camped near Valkia, while squadrons of knights hunted down the fleeing Aquilonians. At dawn the host broke camp and pushed westward through the mountains. Prospero, with ten thousand Poitanians, was miles from the battlefield when he met the fleeing survivors in the early morning. He had marched all night, hoping to reach the field before the battle began. Unable to rally the shattered remnants of the army, he fell back toward Tarantia. Riding hard, changing his exhausted horses for fresh ones seized from the countryside, he now approaches Tarantia.  

“I see his weary knights, their armor gray with dust, their banners drooping as they drive their tired horses across the plain. I also see the streets of Tarantia. The city is in chaos. Somehow word has reached the people of the defeat and the death of King Conan. The mob is mad with fear, crying that the king is dead and there’s no one to lead them against the Nemedians. Giant shadows rush toward Aquilonia from the east, and the sky is black with vultures.”  

Conan cursed bitterly.  

“What are these but words? The poorest beggar in the street could predict as much. If you claim you saw all that in your crystal ball, then you’re a liar as well as a scoundrel—and there’s no doubt about the latter! Prospero will hold Tarantia, and the barons will rally to him. Count Trocero of Poitain rules the kingdom in my absence, and he’ll drive those Nemedian dogs howling back to their kennels. What are fifty thousand Nemedians? Aquilonia will swallow them whole. They’ll never see Belverus again. It wasn’t Aquilonia that was defeated at Valkia—it was only Conan.”  

“Aquilonia is doomed,” said Xaltotun calmly. “Lance, axe, and torch will conquer her; and if they fail, powers from the darkness of ages will march against her. As the cliffs fell at Valkia, so will walled cities and mountains fall if the need arises, and rivers will roar from their beds to drown whole provinces.  

“Better if steel and bowstring prevail without further aid from the arts, for the constant use of great spells sometimes sets forces in motion that could shake the universe.”  

“From what hell did you crawl, you night-born dog?” muttered Conan, staring at him. The Cimmerian shivered involuntarily; he sensed something impossibly ancient, impossibly evil.  

Xaltotun lifted his head, as if listening to whispers across the void. He seemed to forget his prisoner. Then he shook his head impatiently and looked at Conan with cold detachment.  

“What? Why, if I told you, you wouldn’t believe me. But I’m tired of talking to you; it’s less exhausting to destroy a walled city than to shape my thoughts into words a brainless barbarian can understand.”  

“If my hands were free,” said Conan, “I’d soon make a brainless corpse out of you.”  

“I don’t doubt it—if I were foolish enough to give you the chance,” replied Xaltotun, clapping his hands.  

His manner had changed; there was impatience in his tone and a certain nervousness in his movements, though Conan didn’t think it had anything to do with him.  

“Think about what I’ve told you, barbarian,” said Xaltotun. “You’ll have plenty of time. I haven’t yet decided what to do with you. It depends on circumstances not yet born. But remember this: if I decide to use you in my plans, it will be better to submit without resistance than to face my wrath.”  

Conan spat a curse at him just as the hangings that covered a door swung aside and four huge black men entered. Each wore only a silk loincloth held by a belt from which hung a large key.  

Xaltotun gestured impatiently toward the king and turned away, as if dismissing the matter from his mind entirely. His fingers twitched strangely. From a carved green jade box he took a handful of shimmering black dust and dropped it into a brazier that stood on a golden tripod beside him. The crystal globe, which he seemed to have forgotten, suddenly fell to the floor, as if its invisible support had vanished.  

Then the black men lifted Conan—for he was so loaded with chains he couldn’t walk—and carried him from the chamber. A glance back, before the heavy, gold-bound teak door closed, showed him Xaltotun leaning back in his throne-like chair, arms folded, while a thin wisp of smoke curled up from the brazier. Conan’s scalp prickled. In Stygia, that ancient and evil kingdom far to the south, he had seen such black dust before. It was the pollen of the black lotus, which brings deathlike sleep and monstrous dreams; and he knew that only the grim wizards of the Black Ring, the lowest pit of evil, willingly seek the scarlet nightmares of the black lotus to renew their necromantic powers.  

The Black Ring was a myth and a lie to most people of the western world, but Conan knew its dreadful reality, and the grim devotees who practiced their vile sorceries in the black vaults of Stygia and beneath the shadowed domes of cursed Sabatea.  

He glanced back at the mysterious, gold-bound door, shuddering at what it concealed.  

Whether it was day or night, the king couldn’t tell. The palace of King Tarascus seemed a shadowy, dark place that shunned natural light. The spirit of darkness and shadow hung over it, and that spirit, Conan felt, was embodied in the stranger Xaltotun. The black men carried the king along a winding corridor so dimly lit that they moved like black ghosts bearing a dead man, and down a stone stairway that spiraled endlessly. A torch in one man’s hand cast huge, twisted shadows along the wall; it was like a descent into hell, a corpse carried by dusky demons.

At last they reached the bottom of the stairs, then walked down a long, straight hallway. On one side was a blank wall broken only by the occasional arched doorway, each with a stairway leading upward behind it. On the other side was another wall lined with heavy barred doors spaced evenly every few feet.  

They stopped in front of one of these doors. One of the black-skinned men took a key from his belt and turned it in the lock. Pushing open the grille, they entered with their prisoner. The room was a small dungeon with thick stone walls, floor, and ceiling. In the opposite wall was another barred door. Conan couldn’t tell what lay beyond it, but he doubted it was another hallway. The flickering torchlight shining through the bars hinted at a large, shadowy space beyond, echoing and deep.  

In one corner of the dungeon, near the door they had entered through, a cluster of rusty chains hung from a huge iron ring set into the stone. A skeleton dangled from those chains. Conan stared at it with grim curiosity, noticing the condition of the bare bones—most were splintered and broken. The skull, which had fallen from the spine, was crushed as if by a savage blow of enormous strength.  

One of the black men, not the one who had opened the door, silently unlocked the chains from the ring with his key and dragged the heap of rusted metal and shattered bones aside. Then they fastened Conan’s chains to that same ring. The third man turned his key in the lock of the far door, grunting once he was sure it was secure.  

Then they looked at Conan with unreadable expressions, their slit eyes gleaming, the torchlight glinting off their dark, glossy skin.  

The one holding the key to the nearer door spoke in a rough, guttural voice: “This your palace now, white dog-king! Only master and we know. All palace sleep. We keep secret. You live and die here, maybe. Like him!” He kicked the broken skull contemptuously, sending it clattering across the stone floor.  

Conan didn’t bother to answer the insult. The man, angered by his silence, muttered a curse, bent down, and spat full in the king’s face. That was a mistake. Conan sat on the floor, chained by the waist, ankles, and wrists to the ring in the wall. He couldn’t stand or move more than a yard from the wall, but there was enough slack in the chains on his wrists. Before the man could pull his bullet-shaped head out of reach, Conan gathered the slack in one powerful hand and struck him on the head. The man dropped like a slaughtered ox, and his companions stared at him lying there, his scalp split open, blood seeping from his nose and ears.  

They made no move to retaliate, nor did they accept Conan’s fierce invitation to come within reach of the bloody chain in his hand. Grunting in their ape-like language, they finally lifted the unconscious man and carried him out like a sack of grain, his arms and legs dangling. They used his key to lock the door behind them but didn’t remove it from the gold chain attached to his belt. They took the torch with them, and as they moved down the corridor, the darkness crept in behind them like a living thing. Their soft, padded footsteps faded away along with the torchlight, leaving only silence and darkness.  

Conan lay still, enduring the weight of his chains and the hopelessness of his situation with the stoic toughness of the wild lands that had shaped him. He didn’t move, because the clanking of his chains sounded unnaturally loud in the stillness, and instinct—born from countless generations of wilderness ancestors—told him not to reveal his position while helpless. It wasn’t a conscious decision; he didn’t stay quiet because he reasoned that danger might be lurking in the dark. Xaltotun had told him he wouldn’t be harmed, and Conan believed it was in the man’s interest to keep him alive, at least for now. But the instincts of the wild were still in him, the same instincts that had made him lie silent as a child while beasts prowled near his hiding place.  

Even his sharp eyes couldn’t pierce the total darkness. But after a while—he couldn’t tell how long—a faint light appeared, a slanted gray beam by which he could just make out the bars of the door beside him and even the skeleton hanging from the other grille. This puzzled him until he realized the reason. He was deep underground, in the pits beneath the palace, but for some reason a shaft had been built from somewhere above. The moon had risen outside, and its light was shining faintly down the shaft. He realized he could use that light to tell the passing of days and nights. Maybe the sun would shine down it too, though it might be closed during the day. Perhaps it was a cruel trick, allowing a prisoner only a glimpse of daylight or moonlight.  

His eyes fell on the broken bones in the far corner, dimly glimmering. He didn’t waste thought wondering who the poor soul had been or why he had died there, but he did wonder at the shattered state of the bones. They hadn’t been broken on a torture rack. Then he noticed something else disturbing—the shinbones were split lengthwise. There was only one reason for that: someone had broken them open to get at the marrow. But what creature other than a human breaks bones for marrow? Maybe those remains were silent proof of a horrible act of cannibalism, some prisoner driven mad by starvation. Conan wondered if his own bones would one day hang there, rusted chains clinking around them. He fought down the wild panic of a trapped beast.  

The Cimmerian didn’t curse, scream, or weep like a civilized man might have. But the turmoil inside him was no less fierce. His great muscles trembled with the force of his emotions. Somewhere far to the west, the Nemedian army was cutting and burning its way through the heart of his kingdom. The small Poitanian force couldn’t stand against them. Prospero might hold Tarantia for weeks or even months, but eventually, without reinforcements, he would have to surrender to superior numbers. Surely the barons would rally to him against the invaders. But meanwhile, Conan himself lay helpless in a dark cell while others led his soldiers and fought for his crown. The king ground his powerful teeth in furious rage.

Then he froze when he heard a quiet step outside the far door. Straining his eyes, he made out a bent, shadowy figure beyond the grille. There was a faint scrape of metal on metal, and he heard the click of tumblers, as if a key had turned in a lock. Then the figure moved silently out of sight. Some guard, he thought, testing the locks. After a while, he heard the same sound again, fainter and farther away, followed by the soft opening of a door and the quick retreat of softly shod feet. Then silence returned.  

Conan listened for what felt like a long time, though it couldn’t have been, since the moon still shone down the hidden shaft. He heard nothing more. At last, he shifted his position, and his chains rattled. Then he caught another sound—a lighter step, a soft footfall outside the nearer door, the one he had entered through. A moment later, a slender figure appeared faintly outlined in the gray light.  

“King Conan!” a soft, urgent voice whispered. “Oh, my lord, are you there?”  

“Where else?” he answered cautiously, turning his head to stare at the figure.  

It was a girl, gripping the bars with her slender fingers. The dim light behind her outlined her graceful body through the thin silk wrapped around her hips and glimmered faintly on jeweled breastplates. Her dark eyes shone in the shadows, and her pale limbs gleamed softly, like polished stone. Her hair was a dark, rippling mass that caught the faint light with a burnished glow.  

“The keys to your chains and the far door!” she whispered, and a slim white hand slipped through the bars, dropping three objects that clinked against the stone floor beside him.  

“What trick is this?” he demanded. “You speak the Nemedian tongue, and I have no friends in Nemedia. What devilry is your master plotting now? Did he send you here to mock me?”  

“It’s no trick!” the girl said, trembling so hard that her bracelets and breastplates clinked against the bars. “I swear by Mitra! I stole the keys from the black jailers. They guard the pits, and each carries a key that fits only one set of locks. I made them drunk. The one whose head you smashed was taken to a healer, and I couldn’t get his key. But I stole the others. Please, don’t waste time! Beyond these dungeons lie the pits that open into hell itself.”  

Somewhat impressed, Conan tried the keys, expecting failure and mocking laughter. But to his shock, one of them actually worked, freeing him from his shackles—it fit not only the lock that held them to the ring but also the locks on his limbs. Moments later, he stood upright, exulting fiercely in his partial freedom. With a quick stride, he reached the grille, his fingers closing around a bar and the slender wrist pressed against it, trapping the girl, who lifted her face bravely to meet his fierce gaze.  

“Who are you, girl?” he demanded. “Why are you doing this?”  

“I am only Zenobia,” she breathed, her voice catching as if from fear, “just a girl from the king’s harem.”  

“Unless this is some cursed trick,” Conan muttered, “I can’t see why you’d bring me these keys.”  

She bowed her dark head, then lifted it again to meet his suspicious eyes. Tears sparkled like jewels on her long lashes.  

“I am only a girl of the king’s harem,” she said, with a kind of proud humility. “He has never looked at me, and probably never will. I am less than one of the dogs that gnaw bones in his banquet hall.  

“But I am no painted toy; I am flesh and blood. I breathe, hate, fear, rejoice—and love. And I have loved you, King Conan, ever since I saw you riding at the head of your knights through the streets of Belverus when you visited King Nimed years ago. My heart wanted to leap from my chest and fall in the dust beneath your horse’s hooves.”  

Color flushed her face as she spoke, but her dark eyes did not waver. Conan didn’t answer right away; wild and untamed as he was, even he felt a kind of awe at the raw honesty of a woman’s soul laid bare before him.  

She bent her head and pressed her red lips to the fingers that held her wrist captive. Then she suddenly lifted her head, as if remembering their situation, and fear flared in her dark eyes.  

“Hurry!” she whispered urgently. “It’s past midnight. You must go.”  

“But won’t they flay you alive for stealing these keys?”  

“They’ll never know. If the black men remember in the morning who gave them the wine, they won’t dare admit the keys were stolen while they were drunk. The key I couldn’t get is the one that opens this door. You’ll have to find your way to freedom through the pits. I don’t know what horrors lie beyond that door, but staying here is even more dangerous.  

“King Tarascus has returned—”  

“What? Tarascus?”  

“Yes! He’s returned, in great secrecy. Not long ago he went down into the pits and came back pale and shaking, like a man who had faced a terrible danger. I heard him whisper to his squire, Arideus, that despite Xaltotun, you must die.”  

“What about Xaltotun?” murmured Conan.  

She shuddered.  

“Don’t speak his name!” she whispered. “Demons are often summoned by the sound of their names. The slaves say he lies in his chamber behind a bolted door, dreaming the dreams of the black lotus. I think even Tarascus secretly fears him, or he would have killed you openly. But he’s been in the pits tonight, and only Mitra knows what he did there.”  

“I wonder if that was Tarascus who was fumbling at my cell door earlier,” Conan muttered.  

“Here—a dagger!” she whispered, pushing something through the bars. His eager fingers closed around the familiar shape. “Go quickly through that door, turn left, and follow the line of cells until you reach a stone stairway. Whatever you do, don’t stray from the row of cells! Climb the stairs and open the door at the top—one of the keys will fit it. If Mitra wills it, I’ll be waiting for you there.” Then she was gone, her light, slippered footsteps fading into the darkness.

Conan shrugged and turned toward the farther grille. It might be some devilish trap set by Tarascus, but charging headfirst into danger was less hateful to Conan’s nature than sitting quietly and waiting for death. He examined the weapon the girl had given him and smiled grimly. Whatever else she might be, that dagger proved she had practical sense. It wasn’t some slim stiletto chosen for its jeweled hilt or golden guard, fit only for dainty murders in a lady’s chamber; it was a straightforward poniard, a warrior’s blade, broad and fifteen inches long, tapering to a diamond-sharp point.  

He grunted in satisfaction. The feel of the hilt lifted his spirits and filled him with confidence. Whatever webs of conspiracy surrounded him, whatever deceit and betrayal trapped him, this knife was real. The great muscles of his right arm swelled in anticipation of deadly blows.  

He tried the farther door, fumbling with the keys as he did. It wasn’t locked. Yet he remembered the black man locking it. That furtive, bent figure hadn’t been a jailer checking the bolts—it had unlocked the door instead. There was something sinister about that unlocked door. But Conan didn’t hesitate. He pushed the grille open and stepped from the dungeon into the outer darkness.  

As he had guessed, the door didn’t open into another corridor. The stone floor stretched out under his feet, and the line of cells ran away to the right and left behind him, but he couldn’t make out the other boundaries of the place he had entered. He could see neither the ceiling nor any other wall. Moonlight filtered into the vast space only through the cell grilles and was almost swallowed by the darkness. Eyes less sharp than his could hardly have made out the faint gray patches that floated before each cell door.  

Turning left, he moved quickly and silently along the row of dungeons, his bare feet making no sound on the stone. He glanced briefly into each cell as he passed. They were all empty but locked. In some, he caught the glimmer of bare white bones. These pits were relics of a darker age, built long ago when Belverus had been a fortress instead of a city. But clearly, their more recent use had been far greater than anyone suspected.  

Ahead, he soon saw the dim outline of a stairway slanting sharply upward and knew it must be the one he sought. Then he suddenly spun around, crouching in the deep shadows at its base.  

Something was moving behind him—something large and stealthy, padding on feet that weren’t human. He looked down the long row of cells, before each of which lay a square of dim gray light, little more than a patch of thinner darkness. He saw something moving across those squares. He couldn’t tell what it was, but it was huge and heavy, yet moved with more than human ease and speed. He glimpsed it as it crossed the gray patches, then lost it as it melted into the shadows between. Its stealthy advance was eerie, appearing and disappearing like a blur in his vision.  

He heard the bars rattle as it tested each door in turn. Now it had reached the cell he had just left, and the door swung open as it pulled. He saw a massive shape faintly outlined in the gray doorway, and then it vanished into the dungeon. Sweat broke out on Conan’s face and hands. Now he understood why Tarascus had come so quietly to his door and then fled so quickly. The king had unlocked his door and somewhere in these cursed pits had opened a cell or cage that held some monstrous creature.  

Now the thing was coming out of the cell again and moving up the corridor, its misshapen head close to the ground. It no longer bothered with the locked doors—it was following his scent. He could see it more clearly now; the gray light revealed a giant, manlike body, but far bulkier and thicker than any human. It walked on two legs, though hunched forward, and its gray, shaggy coat glinted with silver. Its head was a ghastly mockery of a human face, and its long arms nearly touched the ground.  

Conan recognized it at last—he understood the meaning of those crushed and broken bones in the dungeon and knew the creature that haunted the pits. It was a gray ape, one of the monstrous man-eaters from the forests along the mountainous eastern shores of the Sea of Vilayet. Half legendary and wholly terrifying, these apes were the goblins of Hyborian tales—real-world ogres, cannibals, and killers of the dark forests.  

He knew it had caught his scent, for it was coming fast now, rolling its barrel-like body forward on short, powerful, bowed legs. He cast a quick glance up the long stair but knew the creature would be on him before he could reach the distant door. He chose to face it head-on.  

Conan stepped into the nearest patch of moonlight to gain whatever advantage the light could give him, for he knew the beast could see better than he could in the dark. Instantly the brute saw him; its great yellow tusks gleamed in the shadows, but it made no sound. Creatures of the night and silence, the gray apes of Vilayet were voiceless. Yet in its dim, hideous face—a bestial parody of a man’s—there showed a ghastly joy.  

Conan stood ready, watching the approaching monster without a tremor. He knew he had to stake his life on one strike; there would be no chance for another, no time to hit and leap away. The first blow had to kill—and kill instantly—if he hoped to survive that terrible grapple. His eyes swept over the short, thick neck, the hairy belly, and the massive chest that arched like twin shields. He had to aim for the heart; better to risk the blade being turned by the heavy ribs than to strike where the wound wouldn’t be instantly fatal. Fully aware of the odds, Conan matched his speed, precision, and strength against the brute’s raw power and savagery. He would meet it chest to chest, strike a killing blow, and trust his own rugged body to endure the brief but deadly struggle that would follow.

As the ape came charging at him, swinging its massive arms wide, he dove between them and struck with all his desperate strength. He felt the blade sink to the hilt into the creature’s hairy chest, and instantly, letting go of it, he ducked his head and tightened his whole body into a compact knot of muscle. As he did, he grabbed the closing arms and drove his knee hard into the monster’s belly, bracing himself against the crushing grip.

For one dizzy moment he felt as if he were being torn apart by an earthquake; then suddenly he was free, sprawling on the floor, while the monster gasped out its life beneath him, its red eyes rolling upward, the hilt of the dagger quivering in its chest. His desperate strike had found its mark.

Conan was panting as if after a long fight, trembling in every limb. Some of his joints felt as though they had been wrenched out of place, and blood dripped from scratches where the creature’s claws had raked his skin; his muscles and tendons had been brutally strained and twisted. If the beast had lived a second longer, it would have torn him apart. But the Cimmerian’s immense strength had held out, for the brief instant it lasted, against the dying spasms of the ape that would have ripped a weaker man to pieces.

Conan bent down and pulled the knife from the monster’s chest. Then he quickly went up the stairs. He couldn’t guess what other horrors the darkness might hide, but he had no wish to meet any more. This kind of close, desperate fighting was exhausting even for the giant Cimmerian. The moonlight was fading from the floor, the darkness closing in, and something close to panic drove him up the stairway. He let out a gusty sigh of relief when he reached the top and felt the third key turn in the lock. He opened the door slightly and craned his neck to look through, half expecting an attack from some human or beastly foe.

He looked into a bare stone hallway, dimly lit, and a slender, graceful figure stood before the door.

“Your Majesty!” It was a low, vibrant cry, half relief and half fear. The girl rushed to his side, then hesitated, suddenly shy.

“You’re bleeding,” she said. “You’ve been hurt!”

He brushed off the concern with an impatient gesture.

“Just scratches that wouldn’t hurt a baby. Your little skewer came in handy, though. If not for it, Tarascus’ pet ape would be cracking my bones for the marrow right now. But what now?”

“Follow me,” she whispered. “I’ll lead you outside the city walls. I have a horse hidden there.”

She turned to lead the way down the corridor, but he placed a heavy hand on her bare shoulder.

“Walk beside me,” he said quietly, wrapping his massive arm around her lithe waist. “You’ve played fair with me so far, and I’m inclined to believe you; but I’ve survived this long only by never trusting anyone too much—man or woman. So! If you betray me, you won’t live to enjoy it.”

She didn’t flinch at the sight of the blood-stained dagger or the feel of his hard muscles pressing against her supple body.

“Strike me down without mercy if I betray you,” she said. “The very feel of your arm around me, even in threat, is like the fulfillment of a dream.”

The vaulted hallway ended at a door, which she opened. Outside lay another black man, a giant wearing a turban and silk loincloth, with a curved sword lying on the floor near his hand. He didn’t move.

“I drugged his wine,” she whispered, stepping carefully around the motionless figure. “He’s the last and outer guard of the pits. No one has ever escaped from them before, and no one has ever wanted to enter them; so only these black men guard them. Only they, of all the servants, knew it was King Conan that Xaltotun brought in his chariot as a prisoner. I was watching, sleepless, from an upper window that looked into the courtyard while the other girls slept; for I knew a battle was being fought, or had been fought, in the west, and I feared for you…

“I saw the blacks carry you up the stairs, and I recognized you in the torchlight. I slipped into this wing of the palace tonight, just in time to see them take you to the pits. I hadn’t dared come here before nightfall. You must have lain in a drugged sleep all day in Xaltotun’s chamber.

“Oh, we must be careful! Strange things are happening in the palace tonight. The slaves said Xaltotun sleeps as he often does, drugged by the lotus of Stygia, but Tarascus is in the palace. He came secretly, through the postern gate, wrapped in a cloak dusty from long travel, attended only by his squire, the lean, silent Arideus. I don’t understand it, but I’m afraid.”

They came to the foot of a narrow, winding stair, and climbing it, passed through a small panel that she slid aside. When they had gone through, she slipped it back into place, and it became just another part of the ornate wall. They were now in a larger corridor, carpeted and hung with tapestries, where golden light from hanging lamps glowed softly.

Conan listened intently, but heard no sound anywhere in the palace. He didn’t know what part of it he was in, or which direction led to Xaltotun’s chamber. The girl trembled as she drew him along the corridor, stopping beside an alcove hidden by satin drapes. Pulling these aside, she motioned for him to step into the niche and whispered, “Wait here! Beyond that door at the end of the corridor, we could meet slaves or eunuchs at any hour. I’ll go see if the way is clear before we risk it.”

At once his quick suspicions flared up.

“Are you leading me into a trap?”

Tears welled in her dark eyes. She dropped to her knees and clutched his strong hand.

“Oh, my king, don’t doubt me now!” Her voice trembled with desperate urgency. “If you hesitate, we’re doomed! Why would I bring you out of the pits just to betray you now?”

“All right,” he muttered. “I’ll trust you—though, by Crom, the habits of a lifetime aren’t easy to break. Still, I wouldn’t harm you now, even if you brought every swordsman in Nemedia down on me. If not for you, Tarascus’ cursed ape would have found me chained and unarmed. Do as you will, girl.”

Kissing his hands, she sprang up lightly and ran down the hallway, disappearing through a heavy double door.

He looked after her, wondering if he was a fool to trust her; then he shrugged his broad shoulders and pulled the satin curtains together, hiding his refuge. It wasn’t strange that a passionate young woman would risk her life to help him; such things had happened often enough before. Many women had looked on him with favor, both during his wanderings and in his days as a king.

Still, he didn’t stay motionless in the alcove waiting for her return. Following his instincts, he searched the niche for another exit and soon found one—the opening of a narrow passage hidden behind the tapestries, leading to an ornately carved door, barely visible in the dim light filtering in from the outer corridor. As he stared into it, he heard, somewhere beyond that carved door, the sound of another door opening and closing, followed by a low murmur of voices. The familiar sound of one of those voices brought a dark, dangerous look to his face. Without hesitation, he slipped down the passage and crouched beside the door like a stalking panther. It wasn’t locked, and handling it carefully, he pushed it open a crack, showing a reckless disregard for danger that only he could have explained or justified.

The other side was also covered by tapestries, but through a thin slit in the velvet he could see into a room lit by a candle on an ebony table. Two men were there. One was a scarred, sinister-looking thug in leather pants and a ragged cloak; the other was Tarascus, king of Nemedia.

Tarascus looked uneasy. He was slightly pale and kept starting and glancing around, as if expecting and dreading some sound or movement.

“Go quickly, right now,” he was saying. “He’s deep in a drugged sleep, but I don’t know when he might wake.”

“Strange to hear words of fear coming from Tarascus,” rumbled the other in a harsh, deep voice.

The king frowned.

“I fear no ordinary man, as you well know. But when I saw the cliffs fall at Valkia, I knew that the devil we had brought back to life was no fraud. I fear his powers because I don’t know their full extent. But I know they’re somehow tied to this cursed thing I stole from him. It brought him back to life, so it must be the source of his magic.

“He hid it well, but following my secret orders, a slave spied on him and saw him place it in a golden chest, and saw where he hid the chest. Even so, I wouldn’t have dared steal it if Xaltotun himself hadn’t been sunk in lotus sleep.

“I believe it’s the secret of his power. With it, Orastes brought him back to life. With it, he’ll make us all his slaves if we’re not careful. So take it and throw it into the sea as I’ve told you. Make sure you’re far enough from land that neither tide nor storm can wash it back to shore. You’ve been paid.”

“So I have,” grunted the ruffian. “And I owe you more than gold, king; I owe you a debt of gratitude. Even thieves can be thankful.”

“Whatever debt you think you owe me,” replied Tarascus, “will be paid when you’ve hurled this thing into the sea.”

“I’ll ride for Zingara and take a ship from Kordava,” promised the other. “I can’t show my face in Argos because of a murder or two—”

“I don’t care, as long as it’s done. Here it is; a horse is waiting for you in the courtyard. Go, and go quickly!”

Something passed between them, something that glowed like living fire. Conan caught only a brief glimpse of it before the ruffian pulled a slouch hat over his eyes, wrapped his cloak around his shoulders, and hurried out of the room. As the door closed behind him, Conan moved with the savage fury of unchained bloodlust. He had held himself back as long as he could. The sight of his enemy so close set his wild blood boiling and swept away all caution and restraint.

Tarascus was turning toward an inner door when Conan tore aside the hangings and leaped into the room like a blood-crazed panther. Tarascus spun around, but before he could even recognize his attacker, Conan’s dagger struck him.

But the blow wasn’t fatal, as Conan knew the instant he struck. His foot had caught in a fold of the curtains and tripped him as he leaped. The point sank into Tarascus’s shoulder and slashed down along his ribs, and the king of Nemedia screamed.

The force of the blow and Conan’s lunging body knocked him back against the table, which toppled over, snuffing out the candle. Both men were thrown to the floor by the violence of Conan’s attack, tangled in the folds of the fallen tapestry. Conan stabbed blindly in the dark while Tarascus screamed in a frenzy of panic. As if fear gave him superhuman strength, Tarascus tore free and stumbled away into the darkness, shouting, “Help! Guards! Arideus! Orastes! Orastes!”

Conan rose, kicking himself free of the tangled curtains and broken table, cursing with the bitterness of his bloodthirsty frustration. He was disoriented and didn’t know the layout of the palace. Tarascus’s screams still echoed in the distance, and a wild uproar was rising in response. The Nemedian had escaped him in the dark, and Conan didn’t know which way he had gone. His reckless strike for vengeance had failed, and now his only task was to save his own skin if he could.

Swearing fiercely, Conan ran back down the passage into the alcove, glaring out into the lit corridor just as Zenobia came running toward him, her dark eyes wide with terror.

“Oh, what’s happened?” she cried. “The whole palace is awake! I swear I didn’t betray you—”

“No, I’m the one who stirred up this hornet’s nest,” he grunted. “I tried to settle a score. What’s the quickest way out of here?”

She grabbed his wrist and ran swiftly down the corridor. But before they reached the heavy door at the far end, muffled shouts rose from behind it, and the doors began to shake under blows from the other side. Zenobia wrung her hands and whimpered.

“We’re trapped! I locked that door when I came back through it. But they’ll break it down any second. The way to the postern gate goes through there.”

Conan spun around. Up the corridor, though still out of sight, he heard a growing uproar that told him his enemies were behind him as well as ahead.

“Quick! In here!” the girl cried desperately, running across the corridor and throwing open the door to a chamber.

Conan followed her inside and snapped the gold latch shut behind them. They stood in an ornately furnished room, empty except for themselves, and she pulled him toward a window barred with gold, through which he saw trees and shrubs outside.

“You’re strong,” she gasped. “If you can tear these bars away, you might still escape. The garden is full of guards, but the bushes are thick—you might slip past them. The southern wall is also the city’s outer wall. Once you’re over that, you have a chance to get away. A horse is hidden for you in a thicket beside the road that runs west, a few hundred paces south of the Fountain of Thrallos. You know where that is?”

“Yes! But what about you? I meant to take you with me.”

A rush of joy lit up her beautiful face.

“Then my happiness is complete! But I won’t slow you down. If you take me, you’ll fail. No, don’t worry about me. They’ll never suspect I helped you willingly. Go! What you just said will make my life shine for all the years to come.”

He swept her up in his iron arms, crushed her slim, trembling body against him, and kissed her fiercely on her eyes, cheeks, throat, and lips until she lay breathless in his embrace. Wild and stormy as a gale, even his love was violent.

“I’ll go,” he muttered. “But by Crom, I’ll come back for you one day!”

Turning, he gripped the gold bars and ripped them from their sockets with one tremendous pull. He swung a leg over the sill and climbed down swiftly, clinging to the wall’s ornaments. He hit the ground running and vanished like a shadow into the maze of tall rosebushes and spreading trees. One glance back showed Zenobia leaning over the window ledge, her arms stretched toward him in silent farewell and sacrifice.

Guards were running through the garden, all heading toward the palace, where the noise grew louder by the moment—tall men in shining cuirasses and crested bronze helmets. The starlight flashed on their gleaming armor among the trees, revealing every move they made, but the sound of their approach carried far ahead of them. To Conan, raised in the wild, their rush through the shrubbery sounded like a herd of stampeding cattle. Some passed within a few feet of where he lay flat in a thick clump of bushes, never guessing he was there. Focused on the palace, they noticed nothing else. When they had gone shouting past, he rose and slipped through the garden as silently as a panther.

Soon he reached the southern wall and climbed the steps leading to the parapet. The wall was built to keep people out, not in. No sentry was in sight along the battlements. Crouching by an embrasure, he looked back at the great palace rising above the cypresses behind him. Lights blazed from every window, and he could see figures darting back and forth across them like puppets on invisible strings. He grinned grimly, shook his fist in a gesture of farewell and defiance, and lowered himself over the outer edge of the parapet.

A low tree, a few yards below, caught Conan’s weight as he dropped silently into its branches. An instant later he was running through the shadows with the long, swinging stride of a hillman that devours distance.

Gardens and pleasure villas surrounded the walls of Belverus. Drowsy slaves, sleeping beside their watchmen’s pikes, never saw the swift, stealthy figure that climbed walls, crossed alleys shaded by arching trees, and slipped noiselessly through orchards and vineyards. Watchdogs woke and raised their deep, booming cries at a gliding shadow they half-saw, half-scented—and then it was gone.

* * * * *

In a chamber of the palace, Tarascus writhed and cursed on a blood-smeared couch under the quick, skilled hands of Orastes. The palace was full of wide-eyed, trembling servants, but the room where the king lay was empty except for him and the renegade priest.

“Are you sure he’s still asleep?” Tarascus demanded again, clenching his teeth against the sting of the herbal juices with which Orastes was bandaging the long, ragged cut in his shoulder and ribs. “Ishtar, Mitra, and Set! That burns like molten pitch from hell!”

“You’d be feeling that for eternity if not for your luck,” said Orastes. “Whoever struck that blow meant to kill. Yes, I’ve told you Xaltotun still sleeps. Why are you so insistent about that? What’s he got to do with this?”

“You don’t know what’s happened in the palace tonight?” Tarascus searched the priest’s face with burning intensity.

“Nothing. As you know, I’ve been busy for months translating manuscripts for Xaltotun—copying ancient texts written in newer languages into scripts he can read. He knew all the tongues and writings of his own age, but he hasn’t yet learned all the modern ones, and to save time he has me translate them so he can see if any new knowledge has been discovered since his era. I didn’t even know he had returned last night until he sent for me and told me about the battle. Then I went back to my studies, and I didn’t know you had returned until the uproar in the palace drew me out of my cell.”

“Then you don’t know that Xaltotun brought the king of Aquilonia here as a captive?”

Orastes shook his head, not particularly surprised.

“Xaltotun only said that Conan would trouble us no more. I assumed he was dead, but I didn’t ask for details.”

“Xaltotun saved his life when I would have killed him,” snarled Tarascus. “I saw his plan right away. He meant to keep Conan alive as a weapon against us—against Amalric, against Valerius, and against me. As long as Conan lives, he’s a threat, a rallying point for Aquilonia, something that could be used to force us into actions we wouldn’t otherwise take. I don’t trust that undead sorcerer from Python. Lately, I’ve begun to fear him.”

I followed him a few hours after he had gone east. I wanted to find out what he planned to do with Conan. I discovered that he had locked him up in the pits. I meant to make sure the barbarian died, no matter what Xaltotun wanted. And I succeeded—

A careful knock came at the door.

“That’s Arideus,” grunted Tarascus. “Let him in.”

The dark, grim-faced squire entered, his eyes blazing with barely contained excitement.

“Well, Arideus?” exclaimed Tarascus. “Have you found the man who attacked me?”

“You didn’t see him, my lord?” asked Arideus, as if confirming something he already suspected. “You didn’t recognize him?”

“No. It happened so fast, and the candle went out—my only thought was that some demon had been set on me by Xaltotun’s sorcery—”

“The Pythonian sleeps in his locked and barred chamber. But I’ve been down in the pits.” Arideus’s lean shoulders twitched with excitement.

“Well, speak, man!” snapped Tarascus impatiently. “What did you find there?”

“An empty cell,” whispered the squire. “And the corpse of the great ape!”

“What?” Tarascus jerked upright, and blood gushed from his reopened wound.

“Yes! The man-eater is dead—stabbed through the heart—and Conan is gone!”

Tarascus’s face turned gray as he let Orastes push him back down and the priest resumed tending to his torn flesh.

“Conan!” he repeated. “Not a crushed corpse—escaped! Mitra! He’s no man, but a devil himself! I thought Xaltotun caused this wound. Now I see. Gods and demons! It was Conan who stabbed me! Arideus!”

“Yes, your Majesty!”

“Search every corner of the palace. He might be prowling the dark corridors right now like a hungry tiger. Leave no niche unchecked—and be careful. You’re not hunting a civilized man, but a blood-crazed barbarian with the strength and savagery of a wild beast. Search the palace grounds and the city. Surround the walls. If he’s already escaped the city, as he might have, take a troop of horsemen and pursue him. Once he’s past the walls, it’ll be like hunting a wolf through the hills. But hurry—you might still catch him.”

“This is a matter that calls for more than ordinary human skill,” said Orastes. “Perhaps we should ask Xaltotun for advice.”

“No!” shouted Tarascus violently. “Let the soldiers hunt Conan down and kill him. Xaltotun can’t blame us for killing a prisoner to stop his escape.”

“Well,” said Orastes, “I’m no Acheronian, but I know something of the arts, and how to control certain spirits that have taken on physical form. Maybe I can help you with this.”

The fountain of Thrallos stood in a cluster of oaks beside the road, a mile from the city walls. Its musical trickle reached Conan’s ears through the still starlight. He drank deeply from its icy water, then hurried south toward a small, thick grove he saw nearby. As he rounded it, he spotted a large white horse tied among the bushes. Letting out a deep, gusty sigh, he took a stride toward it—then a mocking laugh made him whirl around, glaring.

A dull gleam of mail caught the starlight as a figure stepped from the shadows. This was no plumed, polished palace guard. It was a tall man wearing a morion and gray chain mail—one of the Adventurers, a class of warriors unique to Nemedia. They were men who had never gained the wealth or rank of knighthood, or who had fallen from it; hard-bitten fighters who lived for war and adventure. They formed a class of their own, sometimes commanding troops, but answering to no one except the king. Conan knew he could not have been discovered by a more dangerous foe.

A quick glance into the shadows told him the man was alone. Conan’s great chest expanded slightly, his toes dug into the turf, and his muscles coiled tight.

“I was riding to Belverus on Amalric’s business,” said the Adventurer, moving forward cautiously. The starlight glinted along the length of the great two-handed sword he held bare in his hand. “A horse whinnied to mine from the thicket. I checked it out and thought it strange to find a steed tied here. I waited—and look, I’ve caught a rare prize!”

The Adventurers lived by their swords.

“I know you,” muttered the Nemedian. “You’re Conan, king of Aquilonia. I thought I saw you die in the valley of the Valkia, but—”

Conan leapt like a dying tiger. Skilled fighter though the Adventurer was, he didn’t realize the desperate speed that lay in barbarian muscles. He was caught off guard, his heavy sword half raised. Before he could strike or block, the king’s dagger buried itself in his throat above the gorget, angling down into his heart. With a choking gurgle he staggered and fell, and Conan ripped his blade free without mercy as the man collapsed. The white horse snorted and shied violently at the sight and smell of blood.

Glaring down at his dead enemy, dagger dripping in hand, sweat shining on his broad chest, Conan stood motionless, listening intently. The woods around him were silent except for the sleepy chirp of startled birds. But from the city, a mile away, came the harsh blare of a trumpet.

He bent quickly over the fallen man. A brief search showed that whatever message the man carried was meant to be delivered by word of mouth. But Conan didn’t stop his work. Dawn was only a few hours away. A few minutes later, the white horse was galloping west along the pale road, and its rider wore the gray mail of a Nemedian Adventurer.

Conan knew his only chance to escape was speed. He didn’t even think about hiding near Belverus until the pursuit passed by; he was sure Tarascus’s unnatural ally could track him down. Besides, hiding wasn’t in his nature—he preferred an open fight or an open chase. He knew he had a long head start. He would lead them on a brutal race for the border.

Zenobia had made a good choice in picking the white horse. Its speed, toughness, and endurance were obvious. The girl knew both weapons and horses—and, Conan thought with some satisfaction, she also knew men. He rode westward at a pace that devoured the miles.  

He traveled through a sleeping land, passing villages sheltered by groves and white-walled villas surrounded by wide fields and orchards that grew fewer as he went farther west. As the villages became scarce, the land turned rougher, and the fortresses that loomed from the hills spoke of centuries of border wars. But no one rode down from those castles to challenge or stop him. The lords of those keeps were following Amalric’s banner; the flags that once flew over their towers now waved over the Aquilonian plains.  

When the last clustered village was behind him, Conan left the road, which was beginning to curve northwest toward the distant mountain passes. Staying on the road would mean passing border towers still manned by armed guards who wouldn’t let him through without question. He knew there would be no patrols riding the borderlands on either side, as there would be in normal times, but those towers remained, and by dawn there would likely be groups of returning soldiers with wounded men in ox-carts.  

This road from Belverus was the only one crossing the border for fifty miles north to south. It wound through a series of passes in the hills, and on either side stretched a wide expanse of wild, sparsely inhabited mountains. He kept heading due west, planning to cross the border deep in the wilderness of the hills lying south of the passes. It was a shorter but harder route—safer for a hunted man. One rider on horseback could travel through terrain that an army would find impossible.  

But by dawn he still hadn’t reached the hills; they stretched ahead of him like a long, low blue wall along the horizon. There were no farms or villages here, no white-walled villas among clusters of trees. The dawn wind stirred the tall, stiff grass, and there was nothing but the long rolling swells of brown earth covered with dry grass, and in the distance, the stark walls of a fortress on a low hill. Too many Aquilonian raiders had crossed the mountains in recent years for this countryside to be as thickly settled as the lands farther east.  

Dawn spread like wildfire across the grasslands, and high overhead came a strange crying as a scattered wedge of wild geese flew swiftly south. In a grassy hollow Conan stopped and unsaddled his horse. Its sides were heaving, its coat slick with sweat. He had driven it mercilessly through the hours before dawn.  

While it grazed on the brittle grass and rolled on the ground, he lay on the crest of a low slope, staring eastward. Far to the north he could see the road he had left, stretching like a white ribbon over a distant rise. No black dots moved along that shining line. There was no sign from the far-off castle that anyone had noticed the lone traveler.  

An hour later the land still lay bare. The only signs of life were a glint of steel on the distant battlements and a raven circling in the sky, dipping and rising as if searching for something. Conan saddled up again and rode westward at a slower pace.  

As he topped the next rise, a harsh screaming burst above him, and looking up, he saw the raven flapping high overhead, cawing without pause. As he rode on, it followed him, keeping its distance but filling the morning with its grating cries, ignoring all his efforts to drive it away.  

This went on for hours, until Conan’s nerves were raw, and he felt he’d give half his kingdom just to wring that black neck.  

“Devils of hell!” he roared in helpless fury, shaking his mailed fist at the frenzied bird. “Why do you torment me with your squawking? Get away, you black spawn of damnation, and go peck for grain in the farmer’s fields!”  

He was climbing the first slope of the hills when he thought he heard an echo of the bird’s cries far behind him. Turning in his saddle, he soon spotted another black speck hanging in the blue sky. Beyond that, he caught the glint of the afternoon sun on steel. That could mean only one thing—armed men. And they weren’t riding along the main road, which lay out of sight beyond the horizon. They were following him.  

His face hardened, and he shivered slightly as he stared up at the raven circling above.  

“So it’s more than just the whim of a mindless beast?” he muttered. “Those riders can’t see you, creature of hell—but the other bird can see you, and they can see him. You follow me, he follows you, and they follow him. Are you just a cleverly trained bird, or some demon in feathered form? Did Xaltotun send you after me? Are you Xaltotun yourself?”  

Only a shrill screech answered him, vibrating with mocking laughter.  

Conan wasted no more words on his dark betrayer. Grimly, he settled into the long climb through the hills. He dared not push the horse too hard; the brief rest he’d given it hadn’t been enough to restore its strength. He was still far ahead of his pursuers, but they would steadily close the gap. Their horses were almost certainly fresher than his—they had probably changed mounts at that castle he had passed.  

The terrain grew rougher, the landscape more rugged, with steep grassy slopes rising into thickly forested mountainsides. Here, he knew, he might lose his hunters—if not for that cursed bird shrieking endlessly above him. He could no longer see his pursuers in this broken country, but he was sure they still followed, guided without fail by their feathered ally. That black shape became like a demonic shadow, hounding him through endless torment. The stones he hurled with curses missed or fell harmlessly, though in his youth he had brought down hawks in flight.  

The horse was tiring fast. Conan recognized the grim finality of his situation. He felt an unstoppable fate driving him onward. Escape was impossible. He was as much a prisoner now as he had been in the dungeons of Belverus. But he was no man of the East to surrender passively to what seemed inevitable. If he couldn’t escape, he would at least take some of his enemies with him into death. He turned into a wide thicket of larches covering a slope, searching for a place to make his stand.

Then, ahead of him, there came a strange, high-pitched scream—human, but with an eerie tone. A moment later, he pushed through a screen of branches and saw the source of that unearthly cry. In a small clearing below him, four soldiers in Nemedian chain mail were tying a noose around the neck of a thin old woman dressed like a peasant. A bundle of firewood, bound with cord and lying on the ground nearby, showed what she had been doing when these stragglers caught her.  

Conan felt a slow, burning anger rise in his chest as he silently watched the ruffians drag her toward a tree whose low, spreading branches were clearly meant to serve as a gallows. He had crossed the border an hour ago. He was standing on his own land, watching the murder of one of his own people. The old woman struggled with surprising strength and energy, and as he watched, she lifted her head and gave that same strange, far-carrying cry he had heard before. It was echoed mockingly by a raven flapping above the trees. The soldiers laughed roughly, and one of them struck her in the mouth.  

Conan swung down from his weary horse and dropped down the rocky slope, landing with a clang of metal on the grass. The four men turned at the sound and drew their swords, staring in shock at the armored giant who faced them, sword in hand.  

Conan laughed harshly. His eyes were cold as flint.  

“Dogs!” he said, without emotion or mercy. “Do Nemedian jackals think themselves executioners, hanging my subjects as they please? First, you’ll have to take the head of their king. Here I stand—come and try!”  

The soldiers stared at him uncertainly as he strode toward them.  

“Who’s this lunatic?” growled a bearded thug. “He’s wearing Nemedian armor, but he talks like an Aquilonian.”  

“Doesn’t matter,” said another. “Cut him down, then we’ll hang the old hag.”  

With that, he charged at Conan, sword raised. But before he could strike, the king’s great blade came down, splitting helmet and skull. The man fell dead at his feet, but the others were tough fighters. They howled like wolves and rushed the lone figure in gray mail, and the clash of steel drowned out the raven’s cries.  

Conan didn’t shout. His eyes burned like blue fire, and his lips curved in a grim smile as he swung his two-handed sword left and right. For all his size, he moved with the speed of a cat, constantly shifting, making himself a moving target so that most of their thrusts and swings cut only air. But when he struck, he was perfectly balanced, and his blows landed with crushing power. Three of the four were down, dying in their own blood, and the last one, bleeding from half a dozen wounds, stumbled backward in panic, parrying wildly—when Conan’s spur caught on the tunic of one of the fallen men.  

The king stumbled, and before he could recover, the Nemedian, desperate and frenzied, rushed him so savagely that Conan staggered and fell sprawling over the corpse. The Nemedian croaked in triumph and leapt forward, raising his great sword with both hands over his right shoulder, bracing his legs wide for the killing blow—when suddenly, over the fallen king, something huge and hairy shot forward like lightning, slamming into the soldier’s chest. His cry of victory turned into a scream of death.  

Conan scrambled to his feet and saw the man lying dead, his throat torn out, and a great gray wolf standing over him, its head lowered as it sniffed the blood pooling on the grass.  

The king turned as the old woman spoke to him. She stood tall and straight before him, and despite her ragged clothes, her sharp, aquiline features and piercing black eyes were not those of a common peasant. She called to the wolf, and it trotted to her side like a huge dog, rubbing its massive shoulder against her knee while staring at Conan with glowing green eyes. Absentmindedly, she rested her hand on its thick neck, and together they regarded the king of Aquilonia. Their steady gaze unsettled him, though there was no hostility in it.  

“People say King Conan died under the rocks and rubble when the cliffs collapsed near Valkia,” she said in a deep, strong, resonant voice.  

“So they say,” he growled. He wasn’t in the mood for argument, and he thought of the armored riders who were surely closing in. The raven above him cawed harshly, and he glanced up involuntarily, grinding his teeth in irritation.  

Up on the ledge, his white horse stood with its head drooping. The old woman looked at it, then at the raven, and then she lifted that same strange, eerie cry as before. As if recognizing the call, the raven turned silent and flew eastward. But before it vanished from sight, the shadow of great wings fell across it. An eagle soared up from the trees, rose above it, and struck the black messenger down to the earth. The harsh voice of betrayal was silenced forever.  

“Crom!” muttered Conan, staring at the old woman. “Are you a sorceress too?”  

“I am Zelata,” she said. “The people of the valleys call me a witch. Was that creature of the night leading armed men on your trail?”  

“Yes.” She didn’t seem to find the answer strange. “They can’t be far behind me.”  

“Bring your horse and follow me, King Conan,” she said curtly.  

Without a word, he climbed back up the rocks and led his horse down to the clearing by a winding path. As he came, he saw the eagle reappear, gliding lazily down from the sky to rest for a moment on Zelata’s shoulder, spreading its great wings lightly so as not to crush her with its weight.  

Without speaking, she led the way, the great wolf trotting beside her and the eagle soaring above. Through thick woods and along twisting ledges hanging over deep ravines, she guided him, until finally they came to a narrow, cliff-edged path that led to a strange stone dwelling—half hut, half cave—beneath a cliff hidden among the gorges and crags. The eagle flew to the top of the cliff and perched there like a motionless sentinel.  

Still silent, Zelata stabled the horse in a nearby cave, where leaves and grass were piled high for feed, and a small spring bubbled in the dim recesses.

In the hut, she seated the king on a rough bench covered with hides, and she herself sat on a low stool before the small fireplace, where she built a fire of tamarisk wood and prepared a simple meal. The great wolf lay beside her, facing the flames, his massive head resting on his paws, his ears twitching as he dreamed.

“You’re not afraid to sit in a witch’s hut?” she asked at last, breaking the silence.

Her guest only shrugged impatiently, the movement rippling through his gray mail. She handed him a wooden dish piled with dried fruits, cheese, and barley bread, along with a large pot of strong upland beer, brewed from barley grown in the high valleys.

“I’ve found the quiet of the glens more pleasing than the noise of city streets,” she said. “The children of the wild are kinder than the children of men.” Her hand brushed the ruff of the sleeping wolf. “My children were far from me today, or I wouldn’t have needed your sword, my king. They were coming when I called.”

“What grudge did those Nemedian dogs have against you?” Conan demanded.

“Stragglers from the invading army wander all over the countryside, from the frontier to Tarantia,” she replied. “The foolish villagers in the valleys told them I had gold hidden away, hoping to turn their attention from their own homes. They demanded treasure from me, and my answers angered them. But neither those stragglers, nor the men hunting you, nor any raven will find you here.”

He shook his head as he ate ravenously. “I’m heading for Tarantia.”

She shook her head. “You’re walking straight into the dragon’s jaws. You’d be wiser to seek refuge abroad. The heart has gone out of your kingdom.”

“What do you mean?” he demanded. “Battles have been lost before, yet wars still won. A kingdom isn’t lost by one defeat.”

“And you’ll go to Tarantia?”

“Aye. Prospero will be holding it against Amalric.”

“Are you sure?”

“By hell’s devils, woman!” he burst out angrily. “What else would he do?”

She shook her head. “I feel it’s otherwise. Let’s see. The veil is not easily torn—but I’ll tear it a little, and show you your capital city.”

Conan didn’t see what she threw into the fire, but the wolf whimpered in his sleep, and a green smoke gathered and swirled through the hut. As he watched, the walls and ceiling seemed to stretch and fade away, merging into endless space. The smoke rolled around him, blotting out everything. Shapes moved and shifted within it, then grew clear and distinct.

He saw the familiar towers and streets of Tarantia, where a mob raged and screamed. At the same time, he somehow saw the banners of Nemedia moving steadily westward through the smoke and flames of a ravaged land. In Tarantia’s great square, the frantic crowd surged and shouted, crying that the king was dead, that the barons were preparing to divide the land among themselves, and that even the rule of Valerius was better than chaos. Prospero, shining in his armor, rode among them, trying to calm them, urging them to trust Count Trocero, to man the walls, and to help his knights defend the city. But they turned on him, shrieking in fear and blind rage, calling him Trocero’s butcher, a worse enemy than Amalric himself. They hurled garbage and stones at his knights.

The image blurred slightly, as if time had passed, and then Conan saw Prospero and his knights riding out through the gates, spurring southward. Behind them, the city was in turmoil.

“Fools!” Conan muttered thickly. “Fools! Why couldn’t they trust Prospero? Zelata, if you’re mocking me with some trick—”

“This has already happened,” Zelata replied calmly, though her tone was grim. “It was the evening of the day just past when Prospero rode out of Tarantia, with Amalric’s army almost in sight. From the walls, men saw the glow of their burning. So I read it in the smoke. At sunset, the Nemedians rode into Tarantia unopposed. Look—right now, in the royal hall of Tarantia—”

Suddenly Conan was looking into the great coronation hall. Valerius stood on the royal dais, wearing ermine robes, while Amalric, still in his dusty, bloodstained armor, placed a rich, gleaming crown on his yellow hair—the crown of Aquilonia! The people cheered; long lines of steel-clad Nemedian soldiers looked on grimly, and nobles long out of favor at Conan’s court strutted proudly, wearing Valerius’s emblem on their sleeves.

“Crom!” Conan exploded, leaping to his feet, his huge fists clenched, the veins on his temples bulging, his face contorted with fury. “A Nemedian placing the crown of Aquilonia on that traitor—in the royal hall of Tarantia!”

As if driven away by his rage, the smoke faded, and he saw Zelata’s dark eyes gleaming at him through the thinning mist.

“You’ve seen it—the people of your capital have thrown away the freedom you won for them with your sweat and blood. They’ve sold themselves to slavers and butchers. They’ve shown they don’t trust their own destiny. Can you count on them to help you win back your kingdom?”

“They thought I was dead,” he grunted, regaining some composure. “I have no son. Men can’t be ruled by a memory. So the Nemedians have taken Tarantia—what of it? There are still the provinces, the barons, and the country folk. Valerius has won an empty glory.”

“You’re stubborn, as a warrior should be. I can’t show you the future, nor all the past. No—I show you nothing. I only let you see through windows opened in the veil by powers beyond understanding. Do you want to look into the past for a clue to the present?”

“Aye.” He sat down abruptly.

Again the green smoke rose and swirled. Again images formed before him, strange and seemingly unrelated. He saw towering black walls, pedestals half lost in shadow, holding up images of monstrous, half-beast gods. Men moved in the darkness—slim, dark men wearing red silk loincloths. They carried a green jade sarcophagus down a vast black corridor. But before he could take in much of what he saw, the scene changed. He saw a cavern, dim and shadowy, filled with a strange, formless dread. On an altar of black stone stood a curious golden vessel shaped like a scallop shell. Into this cavern came some of the same dark, wiry men who had carried the sarcophagus. They seized the golden vessel, and then the shadows swirled around them, and he could no longer tell what happened. But he saw a glimmer in the whirling darkness, like a ball of living fire. Then the smoke was only smoke again, drifting up from the tamarisk fire, thinning and fading away.

“But what does this mean?” he demanded, confused. “What I saw in Tarantia I can understand. But what is the meaning of that vision of Zamorian thieves sneaking through an underground temple of Set in Stygia? And that cavern—I’ve never seen or heard of anything like it in all my travels. If you can show me that much, those scraps of vision that make no sense, disconnected, why can’t you show me everything that’s going to happen?”

Zelata stirred the fire without answering.

“These things are ruled by unchangeable laws,” she said at last. “I can’t make you understand; I don’t fully understand myself, though I’ve sought wisdom in the silence of the high places for more years than I can remember. I can’t save you, though I would if I could. In the end, a man must find his own salvation. Still, maybe wisdom will come to me in dreams, and in the morning I might be able to give you the key to the mystery.”

“What mystery?” he demanded.

“The mystery that faces you—the reason you’ve lost your kingdom,” she answered. Then she spread a sheepskin on the floor in front of the hearth. “Sleep,” she said simply.

Without a word, he stretched out on it and sank into a restless but deep sleep, through which silent phantoms moved and monstrous, shapeless shadows crept. Once, outlined against a purple, sunless horizon, he saw the massive walls and towers of a great city unlike any that existed on the waking earth he knew. Its colossal pylons and purple minarets reached toward the stars, and above it, floating like a giant mirage, hovered the bearded face of the man Xaltotun.

Conan woke in the cold whiteness of early dawn to see Zelata crouched beside the small fire. He hadn’t woken once during the night, and the sound of the great wolf leaving or returning should have roused him. Yet the wolf was there beside the hearth, its shaggy coat wet with dew—and with more than dew. Blood glistened darkly in its thick fur, and there was a cut on its shoulder.

Zelata nodded without turning, as if she could read the thoughts of her royal guest.

“He hunted before dawn, and the hunt was red. I think the man who hunted a king will hunt no more—neither man nor beast.”

Conan stared at the great beast with strange fascination as he reached for the food Zelata offered him.

“When I come back to my throne, I won’t forget,” he said shortly. “You’ve helped me—by Crom, I can’t remember the last time I lay down and slept at the mercy of man or woman as I did last night. But what about the riddle you said you’d explain this morning?”

A long silence followed, broken only by the crackle of the tamarisks on the hearth.

“Find the heart of your kingdom,” she said at last. “There lies both your defeat and your power. You’re fighting more than mortal man. You won’t reclaim your throne unless you find the heart of your kingdom.”

“Do you mean the city of Tarantia?”

She shook her head. “I’m only an oracle, through whose lips the gods speak. They’ve sealed my lips so I won’t say too much. You must find the heart of your kingdom. I can say no more. The gods open and close my lips as they will.”

Dawn still glowed pale on the peaks when Conan rode westward. A glance back showed Zelata standing in the doorway of her hut, as unreadable as ever, the great wolf beside her.

A gray sky stretched overhead, and a moaning wind carried the chill promise of winter. Brown leaves drifted slowly down from bare branches, settling on his mailed shoulders.

All day he pushed through the hills, avoiding roads and villages. Toward nightfall he began descending from the heights, level by level, until he saw the broad plains of Aquilonia spread out below him.

Villages and farms clustered near the base of the hills on the western side of the mountains, for most of the raiding across the frontier during the past fifty years had been done by the Aquilonians. But now only embers and ashes marked where farmhouses and villas had once stood.

In the deepening dusk Conan rode slowly on. He had little fear of being discovered, whether by friend or foe. The Nemedians had settled old grudges during their westward march, and Valerius had made no effort to restrain his allies. He didn’t care about winning the love of the common people. A vast path of destruction had been carved through the land from the foothills westward. Conan cursed as he rode over blackened fields that had once been rich, and saw the gaunt, burned gables of houses jutting against the sky. He moved through an empty, abandoned land, like a ghost from a forgotten age.

The speed with which the army had crossed the land showed how little resistance it had met. Yet if Conan had been leading his Aquilonians, the invaders would have had to pay for every foot of ground with their blood. The bitter truth sank deep into his soul: he wasn’t the heir of a dynasty. He was only a lone adventurer. Even the drop of royal blood Valerius claimed gave him more hold over men’s minds than the memory of Conan and the freedom and strength he had brought to the kingdom.

No pursuers followed him down from the hills. He watched for wandering or returning Nemedian troops but saw none. Stragglers gave him a wide berth, taking him for one of the conquerors because of his armor. Groves and rivers were far more plentiful on the western side of the mountains, and there was no lack of places to hide.

So he moved across the ravaged land, stopping only to rest his horse, eating sparingly of the food Zelata had given him, until one dawn, as he lay hidden on a riverbank thick with willows and oaks, he caught sight, far across the rolling plains dotted with rich groves, of the blue and golden towers of Tarantia.

He was no longer in a deserted land, but one alive with activity. From then on, his progress was slow and cautious, through dense woods and little-used paths. It was dusk when he reached the estate of Servius Galannus.

The countryside around Tarantia had escaped the worst of the devastation that had ravaged the eastern provinces. There were signs of a conquering army’s passage—broken hedges, plundered fields, looted granaries—but fire and sword had not been unleashed on a large scale.

There was only one grim blot on the landscape—a burned stretch of ashes and blackened stone where, Conan knew, the grand villa of one of his most loyal supporters had once stood.  

The king dared not openly approach the Galannus farm, which lay only a few miles from the city. At twilight he rode through a vast forest until he spotted a gamekeeper’s lodge through the trees. Dismounting and tying his horse, he walked up to the heavy arched door, planning to send the keeper to fetch Servius. He didn’t know what enemies might be hiding in the manor house. He had seen no soldiers, but they could easily be scattered across the countryside. As he drew closer, however, he saw the door open and a compact figure in silk hose and a richly embroidered doublet stride out and take a path that wound away through the woods.  

“Servius!”  

At the low call, the master of the estate spun around with a startled cry. His hand flew to the short hunting sword at his hip, and he stepped back from the tall, gray steel figure standing in the dusk before him.  

“Who are you?” he demanded. “What is your—Mitra!”  

His breath hissed in, and his ruddy face went pale. “Go away!” he gasped. “Why have you come back from the gray lands of death to haunt me? I was always your loyal man while you lived—”  

“As I still expect you to be,” said Conan. “Stop shaking, man; I’m flesh and blood.”  

Sweating with uncertainty, Servius came closer and stared into the face of the armored giant. Then, convinced that what he saw was real, he dropped to one knee and removed his plumed cap.  

“Your Majesty! Truly, this is a miracle beyond belief! The great bell in the citadel tolled your funeral days ago. Men said you died at Valkia, crushed under a mountain of earth and shattered stone.”  

“It was another man wearing my armor,” grunted Conan. “But we’ll talk later. If you’ve got a roast on your table—”  

“Forgive me, my lord!” cried Servius, jumping to his feet. “The dust of travel is gray on your armor, and I keep you standing here without rest or food! Mitra! I see clearly now that you’re alive, but I swear, when I turned and saw you standing there all gray and ghostlike in the twilight, my knees turned to water. It’s a bad thing to meet a man you thought dead in the woods at dusk.”  

“Tell the keeper to tend my horse, tied behind that oak,” said Conan, and Servius nodded, leading the king up the path. The patrician, recovering from his supernatural fright, had become extremely nervous.  

“I’ll send a servant from the manor,” he said. “The keeper is in his lodge—but I dare not trust even my servants these days. It’s better that only I know you’re here.”  

As they approached the large house that glimmered faintly through the trees, he turned aside onto a little-used path that ran between close-set oaks whose interlocking branches formed a vault overhead, shutting out the dim light of dusk. Servius hurried through the darkness without speaking, his manner close to panic, and soon led Conan through a small side door into a narrow, dimly lit corridor. They moved quickly and silently through it, and Servius brought the king into a spacious chamber with a high, oak-beamed ceiling and richly paneled walls. Logs blazed in the wide fireplace, for the air had a frosty edge, and a great meat pie on a stone platter steamed on a broad mahogany table. Servius locked the heavy door and put out the candles that stood in a silver holder on the table, leaving the room lit only by the fire on the hearth.  

“Forgive me, your Majesty,” he said. “These are dangerous times; spies are everywhere. It’s best that no one can look through the windows and recognize you. This pie, however, just came from the oven—I was going to eat it after speaking with my keeper. If your Majesty would honor me—”  

“The light’s enough,” grunted Conan, sitting down without ceremony and drawing his dagger.  

He dug hungrily into the rich dish and washed it down with great gulps of wine from grapes grown in Servius’s own vineyards. He seemed completely unconcerned about danger, but Servius shifted uneasily on his bench by the fire, nervously fingering the heavy gold chain around his neck. He kept glancing at the diamond-paned windows gleaming faintly in the firelight and cocked his ear toward the door, as if half expecting to hear stealthy footsteps in the corridor outside.  

When he had finished eating, Conan rose and sat on another bench before the fire.  

“I won’t endanger you long by staying here, Servius,” he said abruptly. “By dawn I’ll be far from your estate.”  

“My lord—” Servius lifted his hands in protest, but Conan waved him off.  

“I know your loyalty and your courage. Both are beyond question. But if Valerius has taken my throne, it would mean death for you to shelter me if you were discovered.”  

“I’m not strong enough to defy him openly,” admitted Servius. “The fifty men-at-arms I could muster would be nothing. You saw the ruins of Emilius Scavonus’s estate?”  

Conan nodded, frowning darkly.  

“He was the strongest noble in this province, as you know. He refused to swear allegiance to Valerius. The Nemedians burned him alive in the ruins of his own villa. After that, the rest of us saw how useless resistance was—especially since the people of Tarantia refused to fight. We submitted, and Valerius spared our lives, though he imposed a tax that will ruin many. But what could we do? We thought you were dead. Many of the barons had been killed, others captured. The army was shattered and scattered. You have no heir to claim the crown. There was no one to lead us—”  

“Wasn’t there Count Trocero of Poitain?” demanded Conan harshly.  

Servius spread his hands helplessly.  

“It’s true that his general Prospero was still in the field with a small army. Retreating before Amalric, he urged men to rally to his banner. But with your Majesty dead, men remembered old wars and civil feuds, and how Trocero and his Poitanians once rode through these provinces just as Amalric rides now, with fire and sword. The barons were jealous of Trocero. Some men—perhaps spies of Valerius—shouted that the Count of Poitain meant to seize the crown for himself. Old regional hatreds flared up again. If we’d had one man with royal blood in his veins, we would have crowned him and followed him against Nemedia. But we had none.”

The barons who had followed you faithfully would never follow one of their own, since each thought himself as good as the next and feared the ambitions of the others. You were the cord that held the bundle of sticks together. When that cord was cut, the bundle fell apart. If you had had a son, the barons would have rallied to him with loyalty. But there was no one for their patriotism to center on.

The merchants and commoners, terrified of chaos and a return to the feudal days when every baron ruled by his own law, cried out that any king was better than none—even Valerius, who at least carried the blood of the old dynasty. No one stood against him when he rode up at the head of his armored hosts, the scarlet dragon of Nemedia flying above him, and struck his lance against the gates of Tarantia.

No, the people opened the gates and knelt in the dust before him. They had refused to help Prospero defend the city. They said they would rather be ruled by Valerius than by Trocero. They said—truthfully—that the barons would not rally to Trocero, but many would accept Valerius. They claimed that by surrendering to Valerius they would avoid the destruction of civil war and the wrath of the Nemedians. Prospero rode south with his ten thousand knights, and the Nemedian horsemen entered the city a few hours later. They did not follow him. They stayed to see Valerius crowned in Tarantia.

“Then the old witch’s smoke told the truth,” muttered Conan, feeling a strange chill crawl down his spine. “Amalric crowned Valerius?”

“Yes, in the coronation hall, with the blood of slaughter barely dried on his hands.”

“And do the people prosper under his kind rule?” asked Conan with bitter sarcasm.

“He lives like a foreign prince in the heart of a conquered land,” answered Servius grimly. “His court is filled with Nemedians, his palace guards are of the same breed, and a great garrison of them occupies the citadel. Yes, the Hour of the Dragon has truly come.

“Nemedians strut like lords through the streets. Women are violated, merchants robbed daily, and Valerius either cannot or will not restrain them. No, he is only their puppet, their figurehead. The wise knew he would be, and now the people are beginning to see it too.

“Amalric has marched out with a strong army to subdue the outlying provinces where some barons still defy him. But there is no unity among them. Their jealousy of one another is stronger than their fear of Amalric. He will crush them one by one. Many castles and cities, realizing this, have already surrendered. Those who resist suffer terribly. The Nemedians are gorging themselves on their long-held hatred. Their ranks are swelled by Aquilonians whom fear, greed, or the need for survival have driven into their armies. It’s the natural result.”

Conan nodded darkly, staring at the red firelight flickering across the richly carved oak panels.

“Aquilonia has a king instead of the anarchy they feared,” said Servius at last. “But Valerius does not protect his people from his allies. Hundreds who could not pay the ransom demanded of them have been sold to the Kothic slave traders.”

Conan’s head snapped up, and a deadly fire blazed in his blue eyes. He cursed fiercely, his massive hands clenching into iron fists.

“Yes, white men sell white men and white women, just as in the old feudal days. In the palaces of Shem and Turan they will live out their lives as slaves. Valerius is king, but the unity the people hoped for, even if forged by the sword, is still incomplete.

“Gunderland in the north and Poitain in the south remain unconquered, and there are still free provinces in the west, where the border barons are supported by the Bossonian bowmen. Yet these distant provinces pose no real threat to Valerius. They must stay on the defensive and will be lucky if they can keep their independence. Here, Valerius and his foreign knights rule supreme.”

“Then let him enjoy it while he can,” said Conan grimly. “His time is short. The people will rise when they learn I’m alive. We’ll retake Tarantia before Amalric can return with his army. Then we’ll drive these dogs out of the kingdom.”

Servius said nothing. The crackle of the fire filled the silence.

“Well,” demanded Conan impatiently, “why do you sit there with your head bowed, staring at the hearth? Do you doubt my words?”

Servius avoided the king’s gaze.

“What any mortal man can do, you will do, Your Majesty,” he said. “I’ve ridden behind you in battle, and I know no living man can stand before your sword.”

“Then what is it?”

Servius pulled his fur-lined tunic tighter around him and shivered despite the fire.

“Men say your downfall was caused by sorcery,” he said after a moment.

“So what?”

“What mortal can fight against sorcery? Who is this veiled man who, as they say, meets with Valerius and his allies at midnight, appearing and vanishing so mysteriously? Men whisper that he is a great magician who died thousands of years ago but has returned from the gray lands of death to overthrow the king of Aquilonia and restore the dynasty from which Valerius descends.”

“What of it?” Conan burst out angrily. “I escaped from the devil-ridden pits of Belverus and from dark magic in the mountains. If the people rise—”

Servius shook his head.

“Your strongest supporters in the eastern and central provinces are dead, fled, or imprisoned. Gunderland lies far to the north, Poitain far to the south. The Bossonians have withdrawn to their western marches. It would take weeks to gather and unite those forces, and before that could happen, Amalric would attack each levy separately and destroy them.”

“But an uprising in the central provinces would turn the tide for us!” exclaimed Conan. “We could seize Tarantia and hold it against Amalric until the Gundermen and Poitanians arrived.”

Servius hesitated, his voice dropping to a whisper.

“Men say you died under a curse. They say this veiled stranger cast a spell on you to kill you and break your army. The great bell has already tolled your funeral. People believe you are dead. And the central provinces would not rise, even if they knew you lived. They would not dare. Sorcery defeated you at Valkia. Sorcery carried the news to Tarantia, for that very night men were shouting it in the streets.”

A Nemedian priest unleashed dark magic again in the streets of Tarantia to kill men who still stayed loyal to your memory. I saw it myself. Armed men dropped like flies and died in the streets in ways no one could explain. And the thin priest laughed and said, “I am only Altaro, only a follower of Orastes, who is himself only a follower of the one who wears the veil; the power isn’t mine—it only works through me.”

“Well,” said Conan harshly, “isn’t it better to die with honor than to live in disgrace? Is death worse than oppression, slavery, and total ruin?”

“When fear of sorcery takes hold, reason disappears,” replied Servius. “The fear in the central provinces is too strong for them to rise for you. The outer provinces would fight for you—but the same sorcery that destroyed your army at Valkia would destroy you again. The Nemedians control the broadest, richest, and most heavily populated parts of Aquilonia, and they can’t be defeated by the forces you still command. You’d only be sacrificing your loyal followers for nothing. It pains me to say it, but it’s true: King Conan, you are a king without a kingdom.”

Conan stared into the fire without answering. A burning log collapsed into the flames without a burst of sparks. It could have been the sound of his kingdom falling apart.

Once again Conan felt the grim presence of a harsh reality behind the illusion of the material world. He sensed the relentless push of a merciless fate. A wave of furious panic pulled at his soul—a feeling of being trapped—and a red-hot rage that burned to destroy and kill.

“Where are the officials of my court?” he demanded at last.

“Pallantides was badly wounded at Valkia, ransomed by his family, and now lies in his castle in Attalus. He’ll be lucky if he ever rides again. Publius, the chancellor, fled the kingdom in disguise—no one knows where. The council has been dissolved. Some were imprisoned, some exiled. Many of your loyal followers have been executed. Tonight, for example, Countess Albiona dies under the executioner’s axe.”

Conan started and glared at Servius with such anger burning in his blue eyes that the patrician shrank back.

“Why?”

“Because she refused to become Valerius’s mistress. Her lands are confiscated, her followers sold into slavery, and at midnight, in the Iron Tower, her head will fall. Take my advice, my king—to me you will always be my king—and flee before you’re discovered. No one is safe these days. Spies and informers move among us, betraying even the smallest act or word of discontent as treason and rebellion. If you reveal yourself to your people, it will only lead to your capture and death.

“My horses and all the men I can trust are at your service. Before dawn we can be far from Tarantia, well on our way to the border. If I can’t help you reclaim your kingdom, I can at least follow you into exile.”

Conan shook his head. Servius looked at him uneasily as he sat staring into the fire, his chin resting on his massive fist. The firelight glowed red on his steel mail and his fierce eyes. They burned in the light like a wolf’s eyes. Servius once again felt, even more strongly than before, that there was something alien about the king. That massive body under the chain mail was too hard and flexible for a civilized man; the raw fire of the primitive burned in those smoldering eyes. Now the barbaric quality about the king was more obvious, as if in his hardship the outer layers of civilization had been stripped away, revealing the primal core beneath. Conan was reverting to his original nature. He didn’t act as a civilized man would under the same circumstances, nor did his thoughts follow the same paths. He was unpredictable. It was only a short step from the king of Aquilonia to the fur-clad warrior of the Cimmerian hills.

“I’ll ride to Poitain, if I can,” Conan said at last. “But I’ll ride alone. And I have one last duty to perform as king of Aquilonia.”

“What do you mean, Your Majesty?” asked Servius, shaken by a sense of dread.

“I’m going into Tarantia tonight for Albiona,” answered the king. “I’ve failed all my other loyal followers, it seems—if they take her head, they can take mine too.”

“This is madness!” cried Servius, jumping up and clutching his throat as if he already felt a noose tightening around it.

“There are secrets about the Tower that few know,” said Conan. “Anyway, I’d be a dog to let Albiona die for her loyalty to me. I may be a king without a kingdom, but I’m not a man without honor.”

“It will destroy us all!” whispered Servius.

“It will destroy no one but me if I fail. You’ve risked enough. I ride alone tonight. All I want you to do is get me an eye patch, a staff, and clothes like those of a traveler.”

“It is the King or His Ghost!”

Many people passed through the great arched gates of Tarantia between sunset and midnight—late travelers, merchants from far away with heavily loaded mules, free laborers from nearby farms and vineyards. Now that Valerius ruled the central provinces, there was no strict inspection of the steady flow of people through the wide gates. Discipline had slackened. The Nemedian soldiers on guard were half drunk and too busy watching for pretty peasant girls or rich merchants they could bully to notice the workers or dusty travelers, even one tall wanderer whose worn cloak couldn’t hide the hard lines of his powerful body.

This man carried himself with a straight, forceful posture that came so naturally he didn’t even realize it, much less try to hide it. A large patch covered one eye, and his leather hood, pulled low over his brow, cast his face in shadow. With a long, thick staff in his strong brown hand, he walked calmly through the arch where the torches flickered and smoked, and, ignored by the drunken guards, stepped into the wide streets of Tarantia.

On those brightly lit streets, the usual crowds went about their business, and shops and stalls stood open with their goods on display. One constant thread ran through the scene: Nemedian soldiers, alone or in groups, swaggered through the crowds, pushing their way with deliberate arrogance. Women hurried out of their path, and men stepped aside with dark looks and clenched fists. The Aquilonians were a proud people, and these were their ancestral enemies.

The tall traveler’s knuckles tightened around his staff, but like the others, he stepped aside to let the armored men pass. In the mixed and colorful crowd, his plain, dusty clothes drew little notice. But once, as he passed a sword-seller’s stall and the light from its wide doorway fell directly on him, he felt a piercing gaze fixed upon him. Turning quickly, he saw a man in the brown jerkin of a free workman staring at him intently. The man turned away too quickly and disappeared into the shifting crowd. Conan turned down a narrow side street and quickened his pace. It might have been nothing more than idle curiosity, but he wasn’t about to take any chances.

The grim Iron Tower stood apart from the citadel, surrounded by a maze of narrow streets and crowded houses. The poorer buildings, claiming space from which the more refined had withdrawn, had crept into a part of the city that had once been closed to them. The Tower itself was really a fortress—an ancient, formidable mass of heavy stone and black iron that had served as the citadel in a rougher, earlier age.

Not far from it, hidden among a tangle of half-abandoned tenements and warehouses, stood an old watchtower so ancient and forgotten that it hadn’t appeared on city maps for a hundred years. Its original purpose was long lost, and those few who noticed it at all never realized that the seemingly ancient lock keeping beggars and thieves from using it as a shelter was actually quite new and extremely strong, cleverly disguised to look like rusted relic. Fewer than half a dozen men in the entire kingdom had ever known the secret of that tower.

No keyhole showed in the massive, green-crusted lock. But Conan’s skilled fingers moved over it, pressing hidden knobs invisible to the casual eye. The door swung silently inward, and he stepped into total darkness, closing it behind him. A light would have revealed the tower to be empty—a bare, cylindrical shaft of solid stone.

Feeling his way with practiced familiarity, he found the small ridges he sought on a stone slab in the floor. He lifted it quickly and, without hesitation, lowered himself into the opening beneath. His feet found stone steps leading downward into a narrow tunnel that he knew ran straight toward the foundations of the Iron Tower, three streets away.

The bell on the citadel—rung only at midnight or for the death of a king—suddenly boomed. In a dimly lit chamber of the Iron Tower, a door opened and a figure stepped into a corridor. The interior of the Tower was as grim as its exterior. Its thick stone walls were rough and bare. The flagstones of the floor were worn deep by generations of faltering feet, and the vaulted ceiling loomed dark in the dim torchlight.

The man walking down that somber corridor matched his surroundings. He was tall and powerfully built, dressed in tight-fitting black silk. A black hood covered his head and shoulders, with two holes cut for his eyes. A loose black cloak hung from his shoulders, and over one shoulder he carried a heavy axe—neither tool nor weapon in shape.

As he walked down the corridor, a figure came hobbling toward him—a bent, surly old man stooped under the weight of his pike and the lantern he carried.

“You’re not as prompt as the last headsman,” the old man grumbled. “Midnight’s just struck, and the masked men have gone to milady’s cell. They’re waiting for you.”

“The bell’s echoes still hang among the towers,” the executioner replied. “If I’m not as quick to jump at the call of these Aquilonians as the dog who held this post before me, they’ll still find my arm just as ready. Go about your duties, old watchman, and let me tend to mine. I think mine’s the sweeter trade, by Mitra—you tramp cold corridors and stare at rusty dungeon doors, while I take off the fairest head in Tarantia tonight.”

The watchman limped away down the corridor, still muttering, and the headsman continued on at his own pace. A few strides brought him around a bend, where he noticed a door on his left standing partly open. If he’d thought about it, he would have realized that door had been opened since the watchman passed—but thinking wasn’t part of his job. He was already passing the open door before he sensed something was wrong, and by then it was too late.

A soft, catlike step and the whisper of a cloak warned him, but before he could turn, a strong arm hooked around his throat from behind, crushing his cry before it could escape. In the brief instant he had, he realized with a surge of panic the incredible strength of his attacker—strength that made his own powerful muscles useless. He sensed, without seeing, the poised dagger.

“Nemedian dog,” a voice hissed thick with fury in his ear. “You’ve taken your last Aquilonian head.”

And that was the last thing he ever heard.

* * * * * * *

In a damp dungeon lit only by a flickering torch, three men stood around a young woman kneeling on the rush-covered floor, staring up at them in terror. She wore only a thin shift; her golden hair fell in shining waves over her white shoulders, and her wrists were bound behind her. Even in the uncertain light, and despite her disheveled state and the pallor of fear, her beauty was striking. She knelt silently, wide-eyed, staring up at her tormentors. The men were masked and cloaked—such a deed required masks, even in a conquered land. She knew them all, but what she knew would die with her that night.

“Our merciful sovereign offers you one more chance, Countess,” said the tallest of the three, speaking flawless Aquilonian. “He bids me tell you that if you humble your proud, rebellious spirit, he will still welcome you into his arms. If not—” he gestured toward a grim wooden block in the center of the cell. It was darkly stained and deeply notched, as though a sharp edge had bitten into it many times.

Albiona shuddered and turned pale, shrinking back. Every fiber of her strong young body trembled with the will to live. Valerius was young too, and handsome. Many women loved him, she told herself, fighting for her life. But she couldn’t speak the word that would save her soft young body from the block and the dripping axe. She couldn’t reason it out—she only knew that when she imagined Valerius’s arms around her, her skin crawled with a revulsion stronger than her fear of death. She shook her head helplessly, driven by a force more powerful than the instinct to survive.

“Then there’s nothing more to say!” snapped one of the others impatiently, speaking with a Nemedian accent. “Where’s the executioner?”

As if summoned by the word, the dungeon door opened silently, and a huge figure appeared in the doorway, like a black shadow from the underworld.

Albiona let out a low, involuntary cry at the sight of that grim shape, and the others stared in silence for a moment, perhaps themselves struck with superstitious fear at the sight of the silent, hooded figure. Through the coif, the eyes burned like blue fire, and as those eyes rested on each man in turn, a strange chill ran down his spine.

Then the tall Aquilonian roughly grabbed the girl and dragged her to the block. She screamed uncontrollably and fought hopelessly against him, wild with terror, but he forced her to her knees and bent her golden head down to the bloodstained block.

“Why do you delay, headsman?” he shouted angrily. “Do your duty!”

He was answered by a short, harsh burst of laughter that was indescribably menacing. Everyone in the dungeon froze where they stood, staring at the hooded figure—the two cloaked men, the masked man bending over the girl, and the girl herself on her knees, twisting her trapped head to look upward.

“What’s the meaning of that laughter, dog?” demanded the Aquilonian uneasily.

The man in black tore the hood from his head and threw it to the ground; he set his back against the closed door and lifted the executioner’s ax.

“Do you know me, dogs?” he growled. “Do you know me?”

The breathless silence was broken by a scream.

“The king!” cried Albiona, wrenching herself free from her captor’s loosened grip. “Oh, Mitra, the king!”

The three men stood frozen like statues, and then the Aquilonian started and spoke, like a man doubting his own senses.

“Conan!” he gasped. “It’s the king—or his ghost! What devil’s trick is this?”

“Devil’s work to match devils!” mocked Conan, his lips smiling but his eyes blazing with fury. “Come on then, my lords. You have your swords, and I have this cleaver. No, I think this butcher’s tool suits the job at hand, my fine gentlemen!”

“Get him!” muttered the Aquilonian, drawing his sword. “It’s Conan—we must kill or be killed!”

And like men waking from a trance, the Nemedians drew their blades and rushed at the king.

The executioner’s ax wasn’t made for such fighting, but the king swung the heavy, awkward weapon as easily as a hatchet, and his quick footwork, as he constantly shifted his position, kept them from surrounding him all at once.

He caught the first man’s sword on the ax-head and crushed in his chest with a murderous counterstroke before the man could step back or parry. The next Nemedian, missing a savage swing, had his skull smashed before he could recover his balance, and an instant later the Aquilonian was backed into a corner, desperately parrying the crashing blows raining down on him, unable even to cry for help.

Suddenly Conan’s long left arm shot out and ripped the mask from the man’s head, revealing his pale face.

“Dog!” snarled the king. “I thought I knew you. Traitor! Damned turncoat! Even this filthy steel is too noble for your vile head. No—you’ll die as thieves die!”

The ax came down in a terrible arc, and the Aquilonian screamed and fell to his knees, clutching the severed stump of his right arm, from which blood spurted. It had been cut off at the elbow, and the ax, unchecked in its swing, had slashed deep into his side so that his entrails bulged out.

“Lie there and bleed to death,” grunted Conan, throwing the ax aside in disgust. “Come, Countess!”

Bending down, he slashed the cords binding her wrists and, lifting her as if she were a child, strode out of the dungeon. She was sobbing hysterically, her arms thrown around his muscular neck in a desperate embrace.

“Easy now,” he muttered. “We’re not out of this yet. If we can reach the dungeon with the secret door that opens onto the stairs leading to the tunnel—damn it, they’ve heard that noise, even through these walls.”

Down the corridor came the clatter of weapons and the tramp and shouting of men echoing under the vaulted roof. A bent figure came hobbling quickly toward them, lantern held high, and its light fell full on Conan and the girl. With a curse, the Cimmerian sprang toward him, but the old watchman, dropping both lantern and pike, scuttled away down the corridor, shrieking for help at the top of his cracked voice. Louder shouts answered him.

Conan turned quickly and ran the other way. He was cut off from the dungeon with the secret lock and the hidden door through which he had entered the Tower—and through which he had hoped to leave—but he knew this grim building well. Before he was king, he had been imprisoned here.

He turned into a side passage and quickly came out into another, wider corridor that ran parallel to the one he had just left, and which was, at the moment, empty. He followed it only a few yards before turning again down another side passage. This brought him back into the corridor he had left, but at a better position. A few feet farther up the corridor was a heavy, bolted door, and before it stood a bearded Nemedian in corselet and helmet, his back to Conan as he peered up the hall toward the growing noise and the wildly swinging lanterns.

Conan didn’t hesitate. Setting the girl down, he ran at the guard swiftly and silently, sword in hand. The man turned just as the king reached him, shouted in surprise and fear, and lifted his pike—but before he could bring the clumsy weapon into play, Conan brought his sword down on the man’s helmet with a force that would have felled an ox. Helmet and skull split together, and the guard crumpled to the floor.

In an instant, Conan had drawn the massive bolt that barred the door—too heavy for an ordinary man to move—and called quickly to Albiona, who stumbled toward him. Lifting her unceremoniously with one arm, he carried her through the door and into the outer darkness.

They had entered a narrow alley, dark as pitch, with the Tower’s wall on one side and the sheer stone backs of a row of buildings on the other. Conan, moving as quickly as he dared through the darkness, felt along the latter wall for doors or windows but found none.

Behind them, the great door clanged open, and men poured out, their torches flashing on breastplates and bare swords. They glared around, shouting, unable to pierce the darkness that their torches lit only a few feet in any direction, then charged down the alley at random—heading the opposite way from Conan and Albiona.

“They’ll realize their mistake soon enough,” he muttered, quickening his pace. “If we can just find a crack in this cursed wall—damn! The street watch!”

Ahead, a faint glow appeared where the alley opened into a narrow street, and he saw dim figures silhouetted against it, steel glinting faintly. It was indeed the street watch, investigating the noise that had echoed down the alley.

“Who goes there?” they shouted, and Conan clenched his teeth at the hated Nemedian accent.

“Stay behind me,” he told the girl. “We’ve got to cut our way through before the prison guards come back and trap us between them.”

Drawing his sword, he charged straight at the oncoming figures. The advantage of surprise was his. He could see them outlined against the distant light, while they couldn’t see him coming out of the black depths of the alley. He was among them before they realized it, striking with the silent fury of a wounded lion.

His only chance was to hack through before they could recover their senses. But there were about ten of them, in full armor, hardened veterans of the border wars, whose battle instincts could replace their stunned wits. Three went down before they realized it was only one man attacking, but even then their reaction was instant. The clash of steel rang deafeningly, sparks flying as Conan’s sword smashed against helmets and mail. He could see better than they could, and in the dim light his swiftly moving form was a hard target. Blades slashed empty air or glanced off his sword, and when he struck, it was with the force and precision of a storm.

But behind him came the shouts of the prison guards, running up the alley, and still the armored watchmen blocked his way with a wall of steel. In moments the guards would be on his back—desperate, he redoubled his blows, striking like a blacksmith at his forge. Then suddenly he sensed a change. Out of nowhere behind the watchmen rose a group of dark figures, and the sound of deadly blows filled the air. Steel flashed in the gloom, and men cried out, cut down from behind. In an instant the alley was strewn with writhing bodies. A dark, cloaked figure leapt toward Conan, who raised his sword, catching a glint of steel in the man’s right hand. But the other hand reached out empty, and a voice hissed urgently, “This way, your Majesty! Quickly!”

With a muttered curse of surprise, Conan scooped up Albiona in one massive arm and followed his unknown rescuer. He wasn’t about to hesitate, not with thirty prison guards closing in behind him.

Surrounded by the mysterious figures, he hurried down the alley, carrying the countess as if she were a child. He could tell nothing about his rescuers except that they wore dark cloaks and hoods. Doubt and suspicion flickered in his mind, but they had struck down his enemies, and he saw no better choice than to follow.

As if sensing his doubt, the leader lightly touched his arm and said, “Do not fear, King Conan; we are your loyal subjects.” The voice was unfamiliar, but the accent was Aquilonian from the central provinces.

Behind them, the guards shouted as they stumbled over the bodies in the mud, then came charging down the alley, seeing the vague dark mass moving between them and the distant streetlight. But the hooded men suddenly turned toward what looked like a blank wall, and Conan saw a door open there. He swore under his breath. He had walked that alley by day in the past and had never noticed a door. But through it they went, and it closed behind them with the click of a lock. The sound wasn’t reassuring, but his guides hurried him on, moving with practiced ease, guiding Conan with a hand on each elbow. It felt like walking through a tunnel, and Conan felt Albiona trembling in his arms. Then ahead of them a faint opening appeared—a slightly lighter arch in the darkness—and through it they passed.

After that came a confusing series of dim courtyards, shadowy alleys, and winding corridors, all crossed in complete silence, until at last they emerged into a broad, lighted chamber. Conan couldn’t even guess where they were, for their twisting route had confused even his keen sense of direction.

Not all of his guides entered the chamber. When the door closed, Conan saw only one man standing before him—a slim figure in a black cloak and hood. The man threw it back, revealing a pale, oval face with calm, finely carved features.

The king set Albiona on her feet, but she still clung to him, staring around nervously. The chamber was large, its marble walls partly covered with black velvet drapes, thick rich carpets spread over the mosaic floor, and the whole place bathed in the soft golden glow of bronze lamps.

Conan instinctively rested a hand on his sword hilt. There was blood on his hand, and dried blood around the mouth of his scabbard, for he had sheathed his blade without cleaning it.

“Where are we?” he demanded.

The stranger bowed deeply, and Conan, ever suspicious, could detect no hint of mockery in the gesture.

“In the temple of Asura, your Majesty.”

Albiona gave a faint cry and clung tighter to Conan, staring fearfully at the black arched doors as if expecting some monstrous shape to emerge from them.

“Do not be afraid, my lady,” said their guide. “There is nothing here that can harm you, despite the common superstitions. If your king was wise enough to see the innocence of our faith and protect us from the persecution of the ignorant, then surely one of his subjects has no reason to fear.”

“Who are you?” demanded Conan.

“I am Hadrathus, priest of Asura. One of my followers recognized you when you entered the city and brought me the news.”

Conan grunted a curse.

“Don’t worry that anyone else discovered who you are,” Hadrathus assured him. “Your disguise would have fooled anyone except a follower of Asura, whose faith teaches us to look beneath illusions. You were followed to the watchtower, and some of my people went into the tunnel to help you if you came back that way. Others, myself included, surrounded the tower. And now, King Conan, you command here. In the temple of Asura, you are still king.”

“Why would you risk your lives for me?” asked the king.

“You were our friend when you sat on your throne,” answered Hadrathus. “You protected us when the priests of Mitra tried to drive us from the land.”

Conan looked around curiously. He had never before entered the temple of Asura, and hadn’t even been sure one existed in Tarantia. The priests of that faith were known for hiding their temples in remarkable ways. The worship of Mitra dominated the Hyborian nations, but the cult of Asura endured despite official bans and public hatred. Conan had heard dark tales of secret temples where thick smoke rose endlessly from black altars, where kidnapped victims were sacrificed before a huge coiled serpent whose terrible head swayed forever in the haunted shadows.

Persecution had forced the followers of Asura to hide their temples with great cunning and to shroud their rituals in secrecy; and that secrecy, in turn, bred even darker suspicions and tales of evil.

But Conan had the broad tolerance of a barbarian, and he had refused to persecute the followers of Asura or allow others to do so based only on rumors and unproven accusations. “If they’re black magicians,” he had said, “how would they let you hunt them? If they’re not, then they’ve done no wrong. Crom’s devils! Let men worship whatever gods they choose.”

At Hadrathus’s respectful invitation, he sat down on an ivory chair and motioned for Albiona to take another, but she preferred to sit on a golden stool at his feet, pressing close against his leg as if seeking safety in his presence. Like most devout followers of Mitra, she had an instinctive horror of the Asurans, planted in her since childhood by wild tales of human sacrifice and monstrous gods lurking in shadowy temples.

Hadrathus stood before them, his bare head bowed.

“What do you wish, Your Majesty?”

“Food first,” he grunted, and the priest struck a golden gong with a silver wand.

The mellow notes had barely faded when four hooded figures entered through a curtained doorway carrying a large silver platter on four legs, loaded with steaming dishes and crystal vessels. They set it before Conan, bowed low, and the king wiped his hands on the fine cloth and smacked his lips with open satisfaction.

“Be careful, Your Majesty!” whispered Albiona. “These people eat human flesh!”

“I’ll bet my kingdom this is nothing but good roast beef,” Conan replied. “Come on, girl, eat! You must be starving after prison food.”

Following his lead, and trusting the man whose word was law to her, the countess obeyed and ate hungrily though delicately, while her lord tore into the meat and drank the wine with as much enthusiasm as if he hadn’t already eaten earlier that night.

“You priests are clever, Hadrathus,” he said, holding a big beef bone in his hands and speaking with his mouth full. “I’d welcome your help in my campaign to win back my kingdom.”

Hadrathus slowly shook his head, and Conan slammed the bone down on the table in a burst of anger.

“Crom’s devils! What’s wrong with the men of Aquilonia? First Servius—now you! Can none of you do anything but shake your foolish heads when I talk about driving out those dogs?”

Hadrathus sighed and answered slowly, “My lord, it pains me to say this, and I wish I could say otherwise. But Aquilonia’s freedom is gone. No—perhaps the freedom of the whole world is gone! Age follows age in the world’s history, and now we enter an age of horror and slavery, as it was long ago.”

“What are you talking about?” demanded the king uneasily.

Hadrathus sank into a chair, resting his elbows on his knees and staring at the floor.

“It’s not just the rebellious lords of Aquilonia and the armies of Nemedia that stand against you,” said Hadrathus. “It’s sorcery—horrible black magic from the dark youth of the world. A dreadful being has risen from the shadows of the past, and no one can stand before him.”

“What do you mean?” Conan repeated.

“I speak of Xaltotun of Acheron, who died three thousand years ago, yet walks the earth today.”

Conan was silent, and in his mind appeared an image—the bearded face of calm, inhuman beauty. Again he felt that strange sense of uneasy familiarity. Acheron—the very sound of the word stirred instinctive echoes of memory and association in his mind.

“Acheron,” he repeated. “Xaltotun of Acheron—man, are you insane? Acheron’s been a myth for more centuries than I can count. I’ve often wondered if it ever really existed.”

“It was grim reality,” answered Hadrathus, “an empire of black magicians steeped in evil long forgotten. It was finally destroyed by the Hyborian tribes from the west. The wizards of Acheron practiced vile necromancy, the darkest kind of sorcery, taught to them by demons. And of all those cursed sorcerers, none was greater than Xaltotun of Python.”

“Then how was he ever defeated?” asked Conan skeptically.

“By some means, a source of cosmic power that he guarded jealously was stolen and turned against him. That source has now been returned to him, and he cannot be defeated.”

Albiona, clutching the executioner’s black cloak around her, stared from the priest to the king, not understanding their words. Conan shook his head angrily.

“You’re making fun of me,” he growled. “If Xaltotun has been dead for three thousand years, how can this man be him? It’s just some scoundrel who’s taken the old one’s name.”

Hadrathus leaned over an ivory table and opened a small golden chest that stood there. From it, he took something that glimmered dully in the warm light—a broad gold coin of ancient minting.

“You’ve seen Xaltotun without his disguise? Then look at this. It’s a coin struck in ancient Acheron before its fall. That dark empire was so steeped in sorcery that even this coin still holds power for magic.”

Conan took it and frowned down at it. There was no mistaking its great age. He had handled many coins during his years of plundering and knew their kinds well. The edges were worn, and the inscription was almost gone. But the face stamped on one side was still sharp and clear. Conan sucked in his breath between his teeth. The room wasn’t cold, yet he felt his scalp prickle and his skin tighten. The face was that of a bearded man—mysterious, calm, and inhumanly beautiful.

“By Crom! It’s him!” Conan muttered. Now he understood the strange sense of familiarity that had struck him when he first saw the bearded man. He had seen a coin like this once before, long ago in a distant land.

With a shake of his shoulders, he growled, “The resemblance is just a coincidence—or if he’s clever enough to take a forgotten wizard’s name, he’s clever enough to copy his face.” But he didn’t sound convinced. The sight of that coin had shaken the foundations of his world. He felt as if reality itself were crumbling into an abyss of illusion and sorcery. A wizard he could understand—but this was madness beyond reason.

“We cannot doubt that it truly is Xaltotun of Python,” said Hadrathus. “He’s the one who brought down the cliffs at Valkia with spells that command the elementals of the earth—he’s the one who sent that creature of darkness into your tent before dawn.”

Conan scowled at him. “How do you know that?”

“The followers of Asura have secret ways of learning things. That doesn’t matter. What matters is—do you realize how useless it is to sacrifice your people in a hopeless attempt to win back your crown?”

Conan rested his chin on his fist and stared grimly into nothing. Albiona watched him anxiously, her mind lost in the maze of the problem before him.

“Isn’t there any wizard in the world who could use magic to fight Xaltotun’s magic?” he asked at last.

Hadrathus shook his head. “If there were, we of Asura would know. People say our faith is a remnant of the old Stygian serpent-worship. That’s a lie. Our ancestors came from Vendhya, beyond the Vilayet Sea and the blue Himelian mountains. We are sons of the East, not the South, and we know all the wizards of the East—who are greater than those of the West. And not one of them could stand before the black power of Xaltotun.”

“But he was defeated once,” Conan insisted.

“Yes—a cosmic power was turned against him. But now that power is in his hands again, and he’ll make sure it’s never taken from him.”

“And what is this cursed power?” Conan demanded irritably.

“It’s called the Heart of Ahriman. When Acheron fell, the primitive priest who had stolen it and used it against Xaltotun hid it in a haunted cavern and built a small temple above it. Three times afterward the temple was rebuilt, each time larger and more elaborate, but always on the same spot, though men forgot why. The memory of the hidden relic faded from common knowledge and survived only in priestly books and secret writings. No one knows where it came from. Some say it’s the actual heart of a god; others say it’s a star that fell from the sky long ago. Until it was stolen, no one had seen it for three thousand years.

“When the magic of the Mitran priests failed against the magic of Xaltotun’s servant, Altaro, they remembered the old legend of the Heart. The high priest and an acolyte went down into the dark, terrible crypt beneath the temple—a place no priest had entered for three thousand years. In the ancient iron-bound books that speak of the Heart in cryptic symbols, it’s also written that a creature of darkness was left there by the ancient priest to guard it.

“Far below, in a square chamber with arched doorways leading into endless blackness, the priest and his acolyte found a black stone altar glowing faintly with an eerie light.

“On that altar lay a strange golden vessel shaped like a double-valved seashell, clinging to the stone like a barnacle. But it was open and empty. The Heart of Ahriman was gone. As they stared in horror, the guardian of the crypt—the creature of darkness—came upon them and tore the high priest so badly that he died. But the acolyte fought off the thing—a mindless, soulless spawn from the pits, brought long ago to guard the Heart—and escaped up the long, narrow black stairs, carrying the dying priest. Before he died, the priest gasped out the news to his followers, told them to submit to a power they couldn’t defeat, and ordered silence. But the rumor spread among the priests, and we of Asura learned of it.”

“And Xaltotun draws his power from this relic?” Conan asked, still doubtful.

“No. His power comes from the black abyss. But the Heart of Ahriman came from some far universe of blazing light, and against it the powers of darkness cannot stand—when it’s in the hands of one who knows how to use it. It’s like a sword that could strike him down, not one he can wield himself. It gives life and can take it away. He stole it not to use against his enemies, but to keep them from using it against him.”

“A gold shell-shaped bowl on a black altar in a deep cavern,” Conan muttered, frowning as he tried to grasp the elusive image. “That reminds me of something I’ve heard or seen. But what, in Crom’s name, is this damned Heart?”

“It’s shaped like a great gem, like a ruby, but it pulses with a blinding fire no ruby ever held. It glows like living flame—”

But Conan suddenly leapt up and slammed his right fist into his left palm with a sound like thunder.

“Crom!” he roared. “What a fool I’ve been! The Heart of Ahriman! The heart of my kingdom! ‘Find the heart of your kingdom,’ Zelata said. By Ymir, it was the jewel I saw in the green smoke—the jewel Tarascus stole from Xaltotun while he lay in the sleep of the black lotus!”

Hadrathus was on his feet too, his calm falling away like a cloak.

“What are you saying? The Heart stolen from Xaltotun?”

“Aye!” Conan thundered. “Tarascus feared Xaltotun and wanted to weaken his power, which he thought was bound to the Heart. Maybe he believed the wizard would die if the Heart were lost. By Crom—ahhh!” With a savage grimace of frustration and disgust, he dropped his clenched hand to his side.

“I forgot. Tarascus gave it to a thief to throw into the sea. By now the man must be nearly to Kordava. Before I can follow, he’ll take ship and send the Heart to the bottom of the ocean.”

“The sea will not keep it!” exclaimed Hadrathus, trembling with excitement. “Xaltotun himself would have thrown it into the ocean long ago if he hadn’t known the first storm would cast it ashore again. But on what unknown beach might it land!”

“Well,” Conan said, regaining some of his usual confidence, “there’s no guarantee the thief will throw it away. If I know thieves—and I should, for I was one in Zamora in my youth—he won’t toss it aside. He’ll sell it to some rich merchant. By Crom!” He paced back and forth, his excitement growing. “It’s worth searching for! Zelata told me to find the heart of my kingdom, and everything else she showed me proved true. Could it be that the power to defeat Xaltotun lies in that crimson jewel?”

“Aye! I’d stake my life on it!” cried Hadrathus, his face alight with passion, his eyes blazing, his fists clenched. “With it in our hands, we can challenge Xaltotun’s powers! I swear it! If we can recover it, we have a real chance to reclaim your crown and drive the invaders from our gates. It’s not Nemedia’s swords that Aquilonia fears, but the black magic of Xaltotun.”

Conan looked at him for a moment, impressed by the priest’s intensity.

“It’s like a quest from a nightmare,” he said at last. “Yet your words echo Zelata’s, and all else she said was true. I’ll search for this jewel.”

“It holds Aquilonia’s fate,” said Hadrathus with conviction. “I’ll send men with you—”

“No!” snapped the king impatiently, unwilling to be slowed down by priests, however skilled in secret arts. “This is a job for a fighting man. I go alone. First to Poitain, where I’ll leave Albiona with Trocero. Then to Kordava, and to the sea beyond, if I must. It may be that even if the thief plans to obey Tarascus’s order, he’ll have trouble finding a ship this time of year.”

“And if you find the Heart,” cried Hadrathus, “I’ll prepare the way for your victory. Before you return to Aquilonia, I’ll spread word through secret channels that you live and are returning with a power greater than Xaltotun’s. I’ll have men ready to rise when you come back. They’ll rise if they believe they’ll be protected from Xaltotun’s black magic.

“And I’ll help you on your journey.”

He rose and struck a gong.

“A secret tunnel runs from beneath this temple to a place outside the city walls. You’ll travel to Poitain on a pilgrim’s boat. No one will dare interfere with you.”

“As you wish.” With a clear purpose driving him, Conan burned with impatience and fierce energy. “Only let it be done quickly.”

* * * * *

Meanwhile, events were moving quickly elsewhere in the city. A breathless messenger burst into the palace where Valerius was amusing himself with his dancing girls, and throwing himself to one knee, gasped out a confused story of a bloody prison break and the escape of a beautiful captive. He also brought word that Count Thespius, who had been charged with carrying out Albiona’s execution, was dying and begging to speak with Valerius before he passed.

Throwing on a cloak, Valerius followed the man through winding corridors until they reached a chamber where Thespius lay. There was no doubt the count was dying; bloody froth bubbled from his lips with every shuddering breath. His severed arm had been bound to stop the bleeding, but even without that, the wound in his side was fatal.

Alone in the chamber with the dying man, Valerius swore softly.

“By Mitra, I thought only one man alive could strike such a blow.”

“Valerius!” gasped the dying man. “He lives! Conan lives!”

“What are you saying?” exclaimed Valerius.

“I swear by Mitra!” gurgled Thespius, choking on the blood that filled his mouth. “It was he who carried off Albiona! He’s not dead—not some ghost come back from hell to haunt us. He’s flesh and blood, and more terrible than ever. The alley behind the tower is full of dead men. Beware, Valerius—he’s come back—to kill us all—”

A violent shudder ran through the blood-smeared body, and Count Thespius went limp.

Valerius frowned down at the corpse, glanced quickly around the empty chamber, then strode to the door and flung it open suddenly. The messenger and a group of Nemedian guards stood several paces down the corridor. Valerius muttered something that might have been satisfaction.

“Have all the gates been closed?” he demanded.

“Yes, your Majesty.”

“Triple the guards at each. Let no one enter or leave the city without the strictest search. Send men through the streets and into every quarter. A very valuable prisoner has escaped, aided by an Aquilonian rebel. Did any of you recognize the man?”

“No, your Majesty. The old watchman caught a glimpse of him, but all he could say was that the man was a giant, dressed in the black clothes of an executioner—the same one whose naked body we found in an empty cell.”

“He’s a dangerous man,” said Valerius. “Don’t take any chances with him. You all know Countess Albiona. Find her, and if you do, kill her and her companion immediately. Don’t try to take them alive.”

Back in his palace chamber, Valerius summoned four men who looked strange and foreign. They were tall and thin, with yellowish skin and expressionless faces. They looked almost identical, wearing long black robes that barely revealed their sandaled feet. Their hoods cast shadows over their faces. They stood before Valerius with their hands tucked into their wide sleeves, arms folded. Valerius looked at them without pleasure. In his long travels, he had met many strange peoples.

“When I found you starving in the jungles of Khitai,” he said abruptly, “exiles from your own land, you swore to serve me. You’ve served me well enough, in your own vile way. I need one more service from you, and then I’ll release you from your oath.

“Conan the Cimmerian, King of Aquilonia, still lives, despite Xaltotun’s sorcery—or maybe because of it. I don’t know. The dark mind of that resurrected devil is too twisted and cunning for any mortal to understand. But as long as Conan lives, I’m not safe. The people accepted me as the lesser of two evils when they thought he was dead. If he reappears, the throne will be shaking under me in revolt before I can even raise my hand.

“Maybe my allies plan to use him to replace me if they decide I’ve outlived my usefulness. I don’t know. But I do know this world isn’t big enough for two kings of Aquilonia. Find the Cimmerian. Use your strange powers to track him down wherever he hides or runs. He has many friends in Tarantia. He had help when he carried off Albiona. It took more than one man, even a man like Conan, to cause all that bloodshed in the alley outside the tower. But enough talk. Take your staffs and follow his trail. I don’t know where it will lead you—but find him! And when you do, kill him!”

The four Khitans bowed together, still silent, then turned and padded noiselessly out of the chamber.

11 - SWORDS OF THE SOUTH  
Swords of the South

The dawn rising over the distant hills shone on the sails of a small boat drifting down the river that curves to within a mile of Tarantia’s walls before looping southward like a great shining serpent. This boat was unlike the usual vessels that traveled the broad Khorotas—fishing boats and merchant barges loaded with goods. It was long and narrow, with a high, curving prow, and black as ebony, with white skulls painted along its sides. In the middle stood a small cabin with tightly shuttered windows. Other boats gave the ominous craft a wide berth, for it was clearly one of those “pilgrim boats” that carried a dead follower of Asura on his final mysterious journey southward, to where, far beyond the Poitanian mountains, a river finally met the blue sea. Inside that cabin surely lay the corpse of the departed worshipper. Everyone knew the sight of those grim boats, and even the most zealous follower of Mitra would never dare touch or interfere with their somber voyages.

No one knew where their final destination lay. Some said Stygia; others claimed a nameless island beyond the horizon; still others whispered it was in the wondrous and mysterious land of Vendhya, where the dead at last returned home. But no one knew for sure. They only knew that when a follower of Asura died, the body was sent southward down the great river in a black boat rowed by a giant slave—and neither boat, corpse, nor slave was ever seen again. Unless, of course, the dark rumors were true, and it was always the same slave rowing every one of those boats southward.

The man rowing this particular boat was as huge and brown as the others, though a closer look might have revealed that his skin color came from carefully applied dye. He wore only a leather loincloth and sandals, and he handled the long oars with remarkable strength and skill. But no one came near the grim vessel, for everyone knew the followers of Asura were cursed, and that these pilgrim boats carried dark magic. So other sailors steered clear and muttered protective prayers as the black craft glided by, never realizing they were helping their king and Countess Albiona escape.

It was a strange journey, that flight down the great river in the black, slender boat for nearly two hundred miles, until the Khorotas turned eastward, skirting the Poitanian mountains. The ever-changing scenery drifted past like a dream. During the day, Albiona lay quietly in the small cabin, as still as the corpse she pretended to be. Only late at night, after the pleasure boats with their noble passengers reclining on silk cushions under torchlight had left the river, and before dawn brought the fishermen back, did she dare come out. Then she took the long oar, which Conan had cleverly secured with ropes to help her manage it, while he snatched a few hours of sleep. But the king needed little rest. The fire of his purpose drove him relentlessly, and his powerful body could endure the strain. Without pause, they pressed southward.

So they fled down the river, through nights when the flowing current reflected a million stars, and through days of golden sunlight, leaving winter behind as they sped south. They passed cities at night, their skies glowing with countless lights, grand riverside villas, and fertile groves. At last, the blue mountains of Poitain rose before them, tier upon tier like the ramparts of the gods, and the great river, turning from those towering cliffs, thundered through the marching hills in rapids and foaming falls.

Conan studied the shoreline carefully, then finally swung the long oar and steered toward land at a point where a narrow tongue of earth jutted into the water, and fir trees grew in a strangely perfect circle around a gray, oddly shaped rock.

“I can’t figure out how these boats manage to ride those falls we hear roaring ahead,” he grunted. “Hadrathus said they could—but this is where we stop. He said a man would be waiting here with horses, but I don’t see anyone. I don’t even know how word of our coming could have gotten here before us.”

He steered the boat toward the shore and tied the prow to a curved root jutting from the low bank. Then he plunged into the water, washed the brown paint from his skin, and came out dripping, back in his natural color. From the cabin he brought out a suit of Aquilonian ring mail that Hadrathus had gotten for him, along with his sword. He put them on while Albiona changed into clothes fit for mountain travel. When Conan was fully armed and turned toward the shore, he froze, his hand going to his sword. Standing under the trees was a black-cloaked figure holding the reins of a white palfrey and a bay warhorse.

“Who are you?” demanded the king.

The stranger bowed low.  
“A follower of Asura. A command came. I obeyed.”

“How did it ‘come’?” Conan asked, but the man only bowed again.  
“I’ve come to guide you through the mountains to the first Poitanian stronghold.”

“I don’t need a guide,” Conan replied. “I know these hills well. I thank you for the horses, but the countess and I will draw less attention traveling alone than with an acolyte of Asura.”

The man bowed deeply, handed Conan the reins, and stepped into the boat. Casting off, he drifted down the swift current toward the distant roar of the unseen rapids. Shaking his head in confusion, Conan lifted the countess into the palfrey’s saddle, mounted the warhorse, and turned toward the peaks that crowned the sky like battlements.

The rolling land at the base of the towering mountains was now a border region in turmoil, where barons had reverted to feudal ways and bands of outlaws roamed freely. Poitain had not officially declared independence from Aquilonia, but for all practical purposes it was a self-contained kingdom ruled by its hereditary count, Trocero. The southern lowlands had nominally submitted to Valerius, but he had not dared to force the mountain passes guarded by fortresses flying the crimson leopard banner of Poitain.

The king and his fair companion rode up the long blue slopes in the soft evening light. As they climbed higher, the rolling country spread out beneath them like a vast purple cloak, streaked with the shine of rivers and lakes, the yellow gleam of wide fields, and the white sparkle of distant towers. Ahead and far above, they saw the first of the Poitanian strongholds—a mighty fortress commanding a narrow pass, its crimson banner streaming against the clear blue sky.

Before they reached it, a band of knights in shining armor rode out from the trees, and their leader sternly ordered them to halt. They were tall men, with the dark eyes and black hair of the south.

“Halt, sir, and state your business, and why you ride toward Poitain.”

“Is Poitain in revolt, then?” asked Conan, watching him closely. “That a man in Aquilonian armor is stopped and questioned like a foreigner?”

“Many rogues ride out of Aquilonia these days,” the knight answered coldly. “As for revolt—if you mean rejecting a usurper, then yes, Poitain is in revolt. We’d rather serve the memory of a dead man than the scepter of a living dog.”

Conan swept off his helmet, shook back his black mane, and stared straight at the speaker. The Poitanian’s eyes went wide and his face turned pale.

“Saints above!” he gasped. “It’s the king—alive!”

The others stared in shock, then a roar of wonder and joy burst from them. They crowded around Conan, shouting their war cries and waving their swords in wild excitement. The cheers of Poitanian warriors could have terrified a timid man.

“Oh, Trocero will weep tears of joy to see you, sire!” one cried.

“Aye, and Prospero!” shouted another. “The general’s been sunk in gloom, cursing himself day and night for not reaching Valkia in time to die beside his king!”

“Now we’ll strike for empire!” yelled another, swinging his great sword above his head. “Hail, Conan, king of Poitain!”

The clash of steel and the thunder of their cheers startled flocks of bright-colored birds into the air. The hot southern blood of the knights was aflame—they wanted nothing more than for their rediscovered sovereign to lead them to battle and plunder.

“What are your orders, sire?” they cried. “Let one of us ride ahead and spread the news of your return through Poitain! Banners will wave from every tower, roses will carpet the road before your horse, and all the beauty and chivalry of the south will honor you as you deserve—”

Conan shook his head.  
“Who could doubt your loyalty? But the winds blow over these mountains into the lands of my enemies, and I’d rather they didn’t know I’m alive—yet. Take me to Trocero, and keep my identity secret.”

So what might have been a triumphal procession became more of a secret flight. They traveled quickly, speaking to no one except for a whispered word to the captain on duty at each pass, and Conan rode among them with his visor lowered.

The mountains were empty except for outlaws and the soldiers who guarded the passes. The pleasure-loving Poitanians had no need or desire to wrest a hard living from those harsh slopes. South of the ranges stretched the rich and beautiful plains of Poitain, reaching to the river Alimane; beyond that river lay the land of Zingara.

Even now, when winter was crisping the leaves beyond the mountains, tall lush grass waved on the plains where grazed the horses and cattle that made Poitain famous. Palm trees and orange groves shone in the sun, and the brilliant purple, gold, and crimson towers of castles and cities gleamed in the golden light. It was a land of warmth and abundance, of handsome men and fierce warriors. Hard lands aren’t the only ones that breed hard men. Poitain was surrounded by greedy neighbors, and her sons had learned toughness through endless wars. To the north, the mountains guarded the land, but to the south only the Alimane separated Poitain’s plains from those of Zingara—and that river had run red not once but a thousand times. To the east lay Argos, and beyond it Ophir, proud and covetous kingdoms. The knights of Poitain held their lands by the weight and edge of their swords, and they knew little of ease or idleness.

So Conan soon came to the castle of Count Trocero.

Conan sat on a silk-covered couch in a richly decorated room, where the warm breeze stirred the thin curtains. Trocero paced the floor like a panther, a lean, restless man with a narrow waist and the broad shoulders of a swordsman, carrying his years lightly.

“Let us declare you king of Poitain!” urged the count. “Let those northern swine bear the yoke they’ve chosen for themselves. The south is still yours. Stay here and rule us, among the flowers and palms.”

But Conan shook his head. “There’s no nobler land on earth than Poitain. But it can’t stand alone, brave as its sons are.”

“It did stand alone for generations,” retorted Trocero, with the quick, proud jealousy of his people. “We weren’t always part of Aquilonia.”

“I know. But things aren’t as they were then, when every kingdom was broken into small principalities constantly at war with each other. The age of dukedoms and free cities is over; the age of empires has come. Rulers dream of empire now, and only unity brings strength.”

“Then let us unite Zingara with Poitain,” argued Trocero. “Half a dozen princes fight each other, and the land is torn apart by civil wars. We’ll conquer it, province by province, and add it to your realm. Then, with Zingaran aid, we’ll conquer Argos and Ophir. We’ll build an empire—”

Again Conan shook his head. “Let others dream of empire. I only want to hold what’s mine. I’ve no wish to rule an empire forged by blood and fire. It’s one thing to seize a throne with the help of its people and rule with their consent. It’s another to subdue a foreign land and rule it through fear. I don’t want to become another Valerius. No, Trocero, I’ll rule all Aquilonia and nothing less—or I’ll rule nothing at all.”

“Then lead us over the mountains and we’ll strike the Nemedians.”

Conan’s fierce eyes glowed with approval.

“No, Trocero. That would be a useless sacrifice. I’ve told you what I must do to win back my kingdom. I must find the Heart of Ahriman.”

“But that’s madness!” protested Trocero. “The ravings of a heretical priest, the mutterings of a mad witch.”

“You weren’t in my tent before Valkia,” answered Conan grimly, glancing unconsciously at his right wrist, where faint blue marks still showed. “You didn’t see the cliffs crash down and crush the flower of my army. No, Trocero, I’m convinced. Xaltotun isn’t a mortal man, and only with the Heart of Ahriman can I stand against him. So I’m riding to Kordava—alone.”

“But that’s dangerous,” protested Trocero.

“Life is dangerous,” rumbled the king. “I won’t go as the king of Aquilonia, or even as a knight of Poitain, but as a wandering mercenary, as I once rode in Zingara. Oh, I’ve enemies enough south of the Alimane, in the lands and seas of the south. Many who wouldn’t know me as king of Aquilonia will remember me as Conan of the Barachan pirates, or Amra of the black corsairs. But I have friends too, and men who’ll help me for their own reasons.” A faint, reminiscent grin touched his lips.

Trocero dropped his hands helplessly and looked toward Albiona, who sat on a nearby couch.

“I understand your doubts, my lord,” she said. “But I too saw the coin in the temple of Asura, and remember, Hadrathus said it was dated five hundred years before the fall of Acheron. If Xaltotun is the man pictured on that coin, as His Majesty swears he is, then he was no ordinary wizard even in his former life, for his years were counted in centuries, not as other men’s lives are.”

Before Trocero could reply, a respectful knock came at the door and a voice called, “My lord, we’ve caught a man sneaking around the castle who says he wishes to speak with your guest. I await your orders.”

“A spy from Aquilonia!” hissed Trocero, reaching for his dagger, but Conan raised his voice and called, “Open the door and let me see him.”

The door opened, and a man appeared in the doorway, held firmly by two stern-looking guards. He was a slender man, dressed in a dark hooded robe.

“Are you a follower of Asura?” asked Conan.

The man nodded, and the strong guards looked shocked and glanced uncertainly at Trocero.

“The word came south,” said the man. “Beyond the Alimane we can’t aid you, for our sect goes no farther south, but stretches eastward along the Khorotas. But this I’ve learned: the thief who stole the Heart of Ahriman from Tarascus never reached Kordava. In the mountains of Poitain he was killed by bandits. The jewel fell into the hands of their chief, who, not knowing its true nature, and being hunted after his band was destroyed by Poitanian knights, sold it to the Kothic merchant Zorathus.”

“Ha!” Conan leapt to his feet, electrified. “And what of Zorathus?”

“Four days ago he crossed the Alimane, heading for Argos, with a small group of armed servants.”

“He’s a fool to cross Zingara in such times,” said Trocero.

“Yes, times are troubled across the river. But Zorathus is a bold man, reckless in his way. He’s in great haste to reach Messantia, where he hopes to find a buyer for the jewel. Perhaps he hopes to sell it at last in Stygia. Perhaps he suspects its true nature. In any case, instead of following the long road that winds along Poitain’s borders and finally enters Argos far from Messantia, he’s struck straight across eastern Zingara, taking the shorter, more direct route.”

Conan struck the table with his clenched fist so hard that the great board shook.

“Then, by Crom, fortune has finally rolled the dice for me! A horse, Trocero, and the gear of a Free Companion! Zorathus has a long head start, but not too long for me to catch him—even if I have to follow him to the ends of the earth!”

12 - THE FANG OF THE DRAGON  
The Fang of the Dragon  

At dawn, Conan rode his horse through the shallow waters of the Alimane and reached the wide caravan road that ran southeast. Behind him, on the opposite bank, Trocero sat silently on his horse at the head of his steel-armored knights, the crimson leopard banner of Poitain fluttering in the morning breeze. The dark-haired men in shining armor sat motionless until their king’s figure disappeared into the blue haze of distance that brightened toward sunrise.  

Conan rode a massive black stallion, a gift from Trocero. He no longer wore the armor of Aquilonia. His gear marked him as a veteran of the Free Companies, men of all races. His helmet was a plain, dented, and battered morion. The leather and mail of his hauberk were worn and polished from long use, and the scarlet cloak hanging loosely from his mailed shoulders was torn and stained. He looked every bit the seasoned mercenary—one who had known every turn of fortune, rich with plunder one day, broke and hungry the next.  

And more than just looking the part, he felt it. Old memories stirred within him—the wild, reckless, glorious days before he had taken the imperial path, when he had been a wandering soldier of fortune, drinking, brawling, and adventuring without a thought for tomorrow, desiring only strong ale, red lips, and a sharp sword to swing on the battlefields of the world.  

Without realizing it, he slipped back into his old habits; a new swagger showed in his posture and the way he sat his horse. Half-forgotten oaths came naturally to his lips, and as he rode, he hummed old songs he had once bellowed with his reckless comrades in countless taverns, on dusty roads, and over blood-soaked fields.  

The land he rode through was restless. The cavalry patrols that usually guarded the river against raids from Poitain were nowhere to be seen. Civil strife had left the borders unprotected. The long white road stretched empty from horizon to horizon. No camel caravans, no rumbling wagons, no herds moved along it now—only scattered groups of horsemen in leather and steel, hawk-faced, hard-eyed men who rode close together and kept watch. They studied Conan sharply but rode on, for his worn armor promised no loot, only hard fighting.  

Villages lay burned and deserted, fields and meadows untended. Only the boldest dared to travel these roads now, and the local population had been decimated by civil wars and raids from across the river. In more peaceful times, the road had been crowded with merchants traveling between Poitain and Messantia in Argos, but now they preferred the safer route east through Poitain and then south across Argos. It was longer, but far less dangerous. Only a foolhardy man would risk his life and goods on this road through Zingara.  

At night, the southern horizon glowed with fire, and by day, thin columns of smoke rose into the sky. In the cities and plains to the south, men were dying, thrones were falling, and castles were burning. Conan felt the old pull of the professional soldier—to turn his horse southward and throw himself into the fighting, the looting, and the chaos as he had so many times before. Why should he struggle to reclaim the rule of a people who had already forgotten him? Why chase a phantom crown that was lost forever? Why not lose himself again in the red tides of war and plunder that had so often consumed him? Could he not carve out another kingdom for himself? The world was entering an age of iron, an age of war and ambition; some strong man might rise above the ruins of nations as a supreme conqueror. Why not him? So whispered his familiar inner devil, and the ghosts of his lawless, bloody past crowded his mind. But he did not turn aside; he rode on, following a quest that grew dimmer the farther he went, until it seemed at times that he was chasing a dream that had never existed.  

He pushed the black stallion as hard as he dared, but the long white road stretched endlessly before him. Zorathus had a long head start, but Conan rode steadily, knowing he was traveling faster than the burdened merchants could. And so he came to the castle of Count Valbroso, perched like a vulture’s nest on a bare hill overlooking the road.  

Valbroso rode down with his men-at-arms—a lean, dark man with glittering eyes and a sharp, predatory nose. He wore black plate armor and was followed by thirty spearmen, black-mustached hawks of the border wars, as greedy and ruthless as their lord. Lately, the caravans had been few, and Valbroso cursed the civil wars that had emptied the roads of rich prey, even as he blessed them for giving him a free hand with his neighbors.  

He had not expected much from the lone rider he had spotted from his tower, but anything that came his way was worth investigating. With a practiced eye, he took in Conan’s worn mail and scarred, dark face, and came to the same conclusion as the other riders who had passed the Cimmerian on the road—an empty purse and a ready sword.  

“Who are you, knave?” he demanded.  

“A mercenary, riding for Argos,” Conan replied. “What does it matter what my name is?”  

“You’re riding the wrong way for a Free Companion,” Valbroso grunted. “The fighting—and the plunder—are to the south. Join my company. You won’t go hungry. The road’s empty of fat merchants to rob, but I plan to take my men south and sell our swords to whichever side looks strongest.”  

Conan didn’t answer right away, knowing that if he refused outright, Valbroso’s men might attack him on the spot. Before he could decide, the Zingaran spoke again.  

“You Free Company rogues always know ways to make men talk. I’ve got a prisoner—the last merchant I caught, by Mitra, and the only one I’ve seen in a week—and the dog is stubborn. He’s got an iron box that defies us, and I haven’t been able to make him open it. By Ishtar, I thought I knew every method of persuasion there was, but maybe you, as a veteran Free Companion, know some I don’t. Come with me and see what you can do.”

Valbroso’s words immediately made up Conan’s mind. That sounded a lot like Zorathus. Conan didn’t know the merchant personally, but any man stubborn enough to try traveling the Zingaran road in times like these was probably stubborn enough to resist torture.  

He fell in beside Valbroso and rode up the uneven road to the top of the hill where the grim castle stood. As a man-at-arms, he should have ridden behind the count, but habit made him careless, and Valbroso didn’t seem to care. Years of life on the border had taught the count that the frontier wasn’t the royal court. He knew how independent mercenaries were—many a king had reached his throne by following their swords.  

There was a dry moat, half filled with rubble in some places. They clattered across the drawbridge and through the gate arch. Behind them, the portcullis dropped with a heavy clang. They entered a bare courtyard overgrown with scraggly grass, with a well in the center. Shacks for the men-at-arms were scattered along the bailey wall, and women—some slovenly, others dressed in cheap finery—peered out from the doors. Fighters in rusty mail tossed dice on the flagstones under the arches. The place looked more like a bandit’s fortress than a nobleman’s castle.  

Valbroso dismounted and motioned for Conan to follow him. They went through a doorway and down a vaulted corridor, where they met a scarred, hard-faced man in armor coming down a stone staircase—clearly the captain of the guard.  

“Well, Beloso,” said Valbroso. “Has he talked?”  

“He’s stubborn,” muttered Beloso, shooting a suspicious glance at Conan.  

Valbroso swore violently and stormed up the winding stairs, followed by Conan and the captain. As they climbed, the groans of a man in mortal agony reached their ears. Valbroso’s torture chamber was high above the courtyard instead of in a dungeon below. In that room, where a gaunt, hairy brute in leather breeches squatted gnawing a beef bone greedily, stood the instruments of torment—racks, boots, hooks, and all the devices human cruelty had invented to tear flesh, break bones, and rip veins and ligaments.  

On a rack a man was stretched naked, and one glance told Conan he was dying. The unnatural length of his limbs and body showed that his joints were dislocated and his body horribly torn. He was dark-skinned, with an intelligent, hawk-like face and quick dark eyes. Now they were glazed and bloodshot with pain, and sweat from agony glistened on his face. His lips were drawn back from blackened gums.  

“There’s the box.” Valbroso viciously kicked a small but heavy iron chest standing nearby. It was intricately carved with tiny skulls and writhing dragons woven together, but Conan saw no latch or hinge that could open the lid. Marks of fire, axe, sledge, and chisel showed on it only as scratches.  

“This is the dog’s treasure chest,” said Valbroso angrily. “Everyone in the south knows of Zorathus and his iron box. Mitra alone knows what’s inside it. But he won’t give up its secret.”  

Zorathus! So it was true—the man he sought lay before him. Conan’s heart pounded painfully as he leaned over the writhing figure, though he showed no sign of his intense eagerness.  

“Loosen those ropes, you dog!” he ordered the torturer sharply, and Valbroso and his captain stared. In his moment of forgetfulness, Conan had used his kingly tone, and the brute in leather instinctively obeyed the commanding edge in that voice. He loosened the ropes slowly, for a sudden release would have tortured the torn joints as much as further stretching.  

Grabbing a jug of wine that stood nearby, Conan held it to the dying man’s lips. Zorathus gulped convulsively, the liquid spilling over his heaving chest.  

A flicker of recognition came into the bloodshot eyes, and the foam-smeared lips parted. From them came a broken whisper in the Kothic tongue.  

“Is this death, then? Is the long torment over? For this is King Conan, who died at Valkia, and I am among the dead.”  

“You’re not dead,” said Conan. “But you’re dying. You’ll be tortured no more—I’ll see to that. But I can’t help you further. Before you die, tell me how to open your iron box.”  

“My iron box,” mumbled Zorathus in delirious, broken phrases. “The chest forged in unholy fires among the flaming mountains of Khrosha; the metal no chisel can cut. How many treasures it has carried across the world! But none like the one it holds now.”  

“Tell me how to open it,” urged Conan. “It can’t help you anymore, but it might help me.”  

“Yes, you are Conan,” muttered the Kothian. “I’ve seen you sitting on your throne in the great hall of Tarantia, with your crown on your head and your scepter in your hand. But you’re dead—you died at Valkia. So I know my own end is near.”  

“What’s the dog saying?” demanded Valbroso impatiently, not understanding Kothic. “Will he tell us how to open the box?”  

As if the voice sparked a last flicker of life, Zorathus rolled his bloodshot eyes toward the speaker.  

“Only Valbroso will I tell,” he gasped in Zingaran. “Death is upon me. Lean close, Valbroso!”  

The count did so, his dark face glowing with greed; behind him his grim captain, Beloso, pressed closer.  

“Press the seven skulls on the rim, one after another,” gasped Zorathus. “Then press the head of the dragon that coils across the lid. Then press the sphere in the dragon’s claws. That will release the secret catch.”  

“Quick, the box!” shouted Valbroso with an oath.  

Conan lifted it and set it on a dais, and Valbroso shoved him aside.  

“Let me open it!” cried Beloso, stepping forward.  

Valbroso cursed him back, greed blazing in his black eyes.  

“No one but me will open it!” he shouted.

Conan, whose hand had instinctively gone to his sword hilt, glanced at Zorathus. The man’s eyes were glazed and bloodshot, but they were locked on Valbroso with burning intensity. Was that the shadow of a grim, twisted smile on the dying man’s lips? Only when the merchant knew he was dying had he given up the secret. Conan turned to watch Valbroso, just as the dying man was watching him.

Along the edge of the lid, seven skulls were carved among twisting branches of strange trees. An inlaid dragon coiled across the top of the lid amid elaborate designs. Valbroso pressed the skulls in frantic haste, and when he jammed his thumb down on the carved head of the dragon, he cursed sharply and jerked his hand away, shaking it in irritation.

“A sharp point on the carvings,” he snarled. “I’ve pricked my thumb.”

He pressed the golden sphere clutched in the dragon’s talons, and the lid suddenly flew open. Their eyes were blinded by a golden blaze. It seemed to their stunned minds that the carved box was full of glowing fire that spilled over the rim and drifted through the air in trembling flakes. Beloso cried out, and Valbroso sucked in his breath. Conan stood speechless, his mind caught in the brilliance.

“Mitra, what a jewel!” Valbroso’s hand plunged into the chest and came out holding a great pulsing crimson sphere that filled the room with a flickering glow. In its light, Valbroso looked like a corpse. And the dying man on the loosened rack suddenly laughed wildly.

“Fool!” he screamed. “The jewel is yours! I give you death with it! The scratch on your thumb—look at the dragon’s head, Valbroso!”

They all turned and stared. Something tiny and dull gleamed from the gaping, carved mouth.

“The dragon’s fang!” shrieked Zorathus. “Soaked in the venom of the black Stygian scorpion! Fool, fool to open the box of Zorathus with your bare hand! Death! You’re a dead man now!”

And with bloody foam on his lips, he died.

Valbroso staggered, crying out. “Ah, Mitra, I burn!” he screamed. “My veins are filled with liquid fire! My joints are bursting apart! Death! Death!” He reeled and crashed headlong to the floor. There was a moment of horrible convulsions, his limbs twisting into grotesque, unnatural shapes, and then he froze in that position, his glassy eyes staring blankly upward, his lips drawn back from blackened gums.

“Dead,” muttered Conan, bending to pick up the jewel where it had rolled from Valbroso’s stiff hand. It lay on the floor like a trembling pool of sunset fire.

“Dead,” muttered Beloso, madness in his eyes. Then he moved.

Conan was caught off guard, his eyes dazzled, his mind dazed by the gem’s blaze. He didn’t realize Beloso’s intent until something crashed down with terrible force on his helmet. The jewel’s glow was splashed with redder flame, and he dropped to his knees under the blow.

He heard rushing footsteps, a bellow of animal-like pain. He was stunned but not completely senseless, and he realized Beloso had grabbed the iron box and smashed it down on his head as he bent over. Only his helmet had saved his skull. He staggered up, drawing his sword, trying to shake the fog from his eyes. The room swam before his dizzy gaze. But the door was open, and quick footsteps were fading down the winding stair. On the floor, the brutish torturer was gasping out his life with a great wound under his chest. And the Heart of Ahriman was gone.

Conan stumbled out of the chamber, sword in hand, blood streaming down his face from under his helmet. He ran unsteadily down the steps, hearing the clash of steel in the courtyard below, shouts, then the frantic pounding of hooves. Rushing into the bailey, he saw the men-at-arms milling about in confusion while women screamed. The postern gate stood open, and a soldier lay across his pike with his head split open. Horses, still bridled and saddled, ran neighing around the courtyard, Conan’s black stallion among them.

“He’s gone mad!” screamed a woman, wringing her hands as she ran about in panic. “He came out of the castle like a mad dog, hacking at everyone! Beloso’s gone mad! Where’s Lord Valbroso?”

“Which way did he go?” roared Conan.

Everyone turned and stared at the stranger’s blood-smeared face and naked sword.

“Through the postern!” shrieked a woman, pointing eastward, and another shouted, “Who is this man?”

“Beloso has killed Valbroso!” yelled Conan, leaping forward and grabbing his stallion’s mane as the men-at-arms hesitated. A wild outcry rose at his words, but their reaction was exactly what he expected. Instead of closing the gates to capture him or chasing the fleeing killer to avenge their lord, they fell into even greater confusion. Bound together only by fear of Valbroso, they had no loyalty to the castle or to each other.

Swords clashed in the courtyard, and women screamed. In the chaos, no one noticed Conan as he shot through the postern gate and thundered down the hill. The wide plain spread before him, and beyond the hill the caravan road split—one branch running south, the other east. On the eastern road he saw another rider, bent low and spurring hard. The plain swam before Conan’s eyes, the sunlight a thick red haze, and he swayed in the saddle, gripping the flowing mane with one hand. Blood streamed down onto his armor, but grimly he urged the stallion on.

Behind him, smoke began to pour from the castle on the hill where the count’s body lay forgotten beside that of his prisoner. The sun was setting; against a blood-red sky, the two black figures fled.

The stallion was not fresh, but neither was the horse Beloso rode. Yet the great beast responded with deep reserves of strength. Why the Zingaran fled from a single pursuer, Conan did not trouble his bruised mind to guess. Perhaps blind panic drove Beloso, born of the madness lurking in that blazing jewel. The sun was gone; the white road was a faint glimmer through the ghostly twilight fading into purple darkness far ahead of him.

“If you call your servant, he will die,” warned one of them, which only terrified Publio even more.

Quick footsteps sounded in the corridor outside, and Gebal burst into the room—a Shemite of medium height and powerful build, his curled blue-black beard bristling, a short leaf-shaped sword in his hand.

The tall Khitan lifted his head and stared at Publio until the merchant broke into a cold sweat.

The Return of the Corsair

Conan’s first feeling as he regained consciousness was motion; there was no solid ground beneath him, only constant rolling and pitching. Then he heard the wind whistling through ropes and spars and knew he was on a ship even before his blurred vision cleared. He heard voices muttering, and then a splash of water drenched him, jerking him fully awake. He sprang up with a curse, braced his legs, and glared around him, hearing coarse laughter and smelling the stench of unwashed bodies.

He was standing on the poop deck of a long galley running before the northern wind, its striped sail swelling tight against the ropes. The sun was just rising in a blaze of gold, blue, and green. To the left, the shoreline was a faint purple shadow; to the right stretched the open sea. Conan took all this in at a glance, along with the ship itself.

It was long and narrow, a typical southern trading vessel, high at both bow and stern, with cabins at each end. Conan looked down into the open middle deck, from which came that sickening, familiar stench. He knew it well—it was the smell of the chained oarsmen. They were all black men, forty to a side, each shackled by a chain around his waist, the other end welded to a heavy iron ring fixed deep in the central beam that ran the ship’s length. Life for a slave on an Argossean galley was a hell beyond imagining. Most were Kushites, but about thirty of the blacks now resting on their oars and staring up at the stranger with dull curiosity were from the far southern islands—the homeland of the corsairs. Conan recognized them by their straighter features, their hair, and their leaner, stronger builds. Among them he saw men who had once followed him.

But all this he noticed in one swift, sweeping glance as he rose, before turning his attention to the men around him. Still unsteady on his feet, fists clenched in anger, he glared at the sailors gathered nearby. The one who had thrown the water stood grinning, the empty bucket still in his hand, and Conan cursed him furiously, instinctively reaching for his sword hilt—only to realize he was unarmed and wearing nothing but short leather breeches.

‘What cursed tub is this?’ he roared. ‘How did I end up aboard?’

The sailors laughed mockingly—stocky, bearded Argosseans, every one of them—and one, whose finer clothes and commanding air marked him as captain, folded his arms and said arrogantly, ‘We found you lying on the beach. Someone had cracked your skull and taken your clothes. We needed another man, so we brought you aboard.’

‘What ship is this?’ Conan demanded.

‘The Venturer, out of Messantia, carrying mirrors, scarlet silk cloaks, shields, gilded helmets, and swords to trade with the Shemites for copper and gold ore. I’m Demetrio, captain of this ship—and your master from now on.’

‘Then I’m headed the way I wanted after all,’ Conan muttered, ignoring that last remark. They were racing southeast, following the long curve of the Argossean coast. These trading ships never strayed far from shore. Somewhere ahead, he knew, the dark Stygian galley was speeding south.

‘Have you seen a Stygian galley—’ Conan began, but the burly, brutal-faced captain’s beard bristled. He wasn’t interested in any questions from his new hand and decided it was time to put this unruly stranger in his place.

‘Get forward!’ he roared. ‘I’ve wasted enough time on you! I did you the honor of bringing you up here to revive you and answered enough of your damned questions. Get off this deck! You’ll earn your keep aboard this galley—’

‘I’ll buy your ship—’ Conan started to say, before remembering he was penniless.

A roar of laughter met his words, and the captain’s face turned purple, thinking he was being mocked.

‘You insolent dog!’ he bellowed, stepping forward with his hand on the knife at his belt. ‘Get forward before I have you flogged! Keep a civil tongue, or by Mitra, I’ll have you chained with the blacks to pull an oar!’

Conan’s volcanic temper, never long restrained, exploded. Not in years, not even before he was king, had anyone spoken to him like that and lived.

‘Don’t raise your voice to me, you tar-soaked cur!’ he roared, his voice booming like the sea wind while the sailors stared in shock. ‘Draw that toy and I’ll feed you to the sharks!’

‘Who do you think you are?’ gasped the captain.

“I’ll show you!” roared the enraged Cimmerian, spinning around and leaping toward the rail where weapons hung in their brackets.

The captain drew his knife and charged at him, bellowing, but before he could strike, Conan seized his wrist and twisted so hard that the arm tore clean out of its socket. The captain screamed like an ox in pain and rolled across the deck as Conan contemptuously hurled him aside. Conan snatched a heavy axe from the rail and spun like a cat to face the oncoming sailors. They rushed in, shouting like hounds, clumsy and slow compared to the panther-like Cimmerian. Before they could reach him with their knives, he sprang among them, striking right and left too fast for the eye to follow. Blood and brains splattered as two bodies hit the deck.

Knives slashed wildly through the air as Conan broke through the stumbling, gasping mob and bounded onto the narrow bridge that spanned the waist from poop to forecastle, just out of reach of the slaves below. Behind him, the few sailors on the poop were stumbling after him, shaken by the slaughter of their comrades, while the rest of the crew—about thirty in all—came running across the bridge toward him, weapons in hand.

Conan leapt out onto the bridge and stood poised above the upturned black faces, axe raised, black hair whipping in the wind.

“Who am I?” he shouted. “Look, you dogs! Look, Ajonga, Yasunga, Laranga! Who am I?”

And from the waist rose a shout that swelled into a mighty roar: “Amra! It’s Amra! The Lion has returned!”

The sailors who caught and understood the meaning of that terrifying cry went pale and shrank back, staring in sudden fear at the wild figure on the bridge. Could this truly be that bloodthirsty demon of the southern seas who had vanished mysteriously years ago, but still lived on in bloody legends? The blacks were now in a frenzy, shaking and tearing at their chains, screaming the name of Amra like a prayer. Kushites who had never seen Conan before took up the cry. The slaves in the pen under the after-cabin began pounding at the walls, shrieking like the damned.

Demetrio, dragging himself along the deck on one hand and his knees, his face gray with the pain of his dislocated arm, screamed, “Get in there and kill him, dogs, before the slaves break loose!”

Driven to desperation by that word—the most dreaded by all galley men—the sailors charged onto the bridge from both ends. But with a lion-like leap, Conan left the bridge and landed lightly on his feet on the runway between the benches.

“Death to the masters!” he thundered, and his axe came down hard on a shackle chain, slicing it apart like kindling. Instantly a screaming slave was free, smashing his oar into a club. Men were racing frantically along the bridge above, and chaos exploded aboard the Venturer. Conan’s axe rose and fell without pause, and with every stroke another screaming black giant broke free, mad with hatred and the wild fury of freedom and revenge.

Sailors who jumped down into the waist to fight or strike at the naked white giant hacking like a madman at the shackles found themselves dragged down by the hands of slaves still chained, while others, their broken chains whipping around their limbs, surged up from the waist like a blind, black flood, screaming like demons, striking with broken oars and bits of iron, clawing and biting with teeth and nails. In the middle of the chaos, the slaves in the pen smashed down the walls and came flooding up onto the deck, and with fifty blacks freed from their benches, Conan left off breaking chains and bounded up onto the bridge to add his notched axe to the weapons of his allies.

Then came the slaughter. The Argosseans were strong, sturdy, and fearless, like all their race, hardened by the brutal life of the sea. But they could not stand against these raging giants led by the tigerish barbarian. Blows, abuse, and years of torment were repaid in one red storm of fury that swept like a typhoon from one end of the ship to the other. When it was over, only one white man still lived aboard the Venturer—the blood-smeared giant around whom the chanting blacks threw themselves, prostrate on the bloody deck, beating their heads against the boards in wild worship.

Conan, his massive chest heaving and glistening with sweat, the red axe gripped in his blood-covered hand, glared around him like the first chief of men might have glared in the dawn of time, shaking back his black mane. In that moment, he was not the king of Aquilonia; he was once again the lord of the black corsairs, who had fought his way to power through fire and blood.

“Amra! Amra!” chanted the delirious blacks, those still alive to chant. “The Lion has returned! Now the Stygians will howl like dogs in the night, and the black dogs of Kush will howl! Now villages will burn and ships will sink! Aie, there will be the wailing of women and the thunder of spears!”

“Stop that racket, dogs!” Conan roared, his voice drowning out the snap of the sail in the wind. “Ten of you go below and free the oarsmen still chained. The rest of you man the sweeps and get to the oars and rigging. Crom’s devils, can’t you see we’ve drifted inshore during the fight? Do you want to run aground and be taken again by the Argosseans? Throw these corpses overboard. Move it, you rogues, or I’ll mark your hides myself!”

With shouts, laughter, and wild singing, they rushed to obey. The bodies, white and black alike, were tossed overboard, where triangular fins were already slicing through the water.

Conan stood on the poop, frowning down at the black men watching him expectantly. His heavy brown arms were folded, his long black hair, grown wild during his wanderings, blowing in the wind. A wilder, more savage figure had never stood on a ship’s bridge, and few of the courtiers of Aquilonia would have recognized their king in this fierce corsair.

“There’s food in the hold!” he roared. “Plenty of weapons for you, for this ship carried blades and armor for the Shemites along the coast. There are enough of us to sail her—and to fight! You rowed in chains for the Argossean dogs; will you row as free men for Amra?”

“Aye!” they shouted. “We are your children! Lead us wherever you wish!”

“Then get to work and clean out that waist,” he ordered. “Free men don’t live in such filth. Three of you come with me and bring out food from the after-cabin. By Crom, I’ll put some meat on your bones before this voyage is over.”

Another roar of approval answered him as the half-starved black men hurried to obey. The sail swelled as the wind swept over the waves with renewed strength, and the white crests danced along the rolling sea. Conan braced his feet against the deck’s rise, breathed deeply, and spread his powerful arms. He might no longer be King of Aquilonia, but he was still king of the blue ocean.

The Venturer sped southward like a living creature, her oars now pulled by free and willing hands. She had been changed from a peaceful merchant ship into a war galley, as much as such a change was possible. Men now sat at the benches with swords at their sides and gilded helmets on their curly heads. Shields hung along the rails, and bundles of spears, bows, and arrows decorated the mast. Even the elements seemed to favor Conan now; the broad purple sail filled with a steady wind that held day after day, needing little help from the oars.

But though Conan kept a lookout on the mast day and night, they never saw the long, low, black galley fleeing south ahead of them. Day after day the blue waters stretched empty before them, broken only by fishing boats that fled like frightened birds at the sight of the shields hanging along the rail. The trading season was nearly over for the year, and they saw no other ships.

When the lookout finally spotted a sail, it was to the north, not the south. Far on the horizon behind them appeared a racing galley with a full spread of purple sail. The black crew urged Conan to turn and attack it, but he shook his head. Somewhere to the south, a slim black galley was racing toward the ports of Stygia. That night, before darkness fell, the lookout’s last glimpse showed the racing galley still on the horizon, and at dawn it was still there, far behind, tiny in the distance. Conan wondered if it was following him, though he could think of no reason why. Still, he paid it little attention.

Each day that carried him farther south filled him with growing impatience. Doubt never touched him. As surely as he believed in the rising and setting of the sun, he believed that a priest of Set had stolen the Heart of Ahriman. And where else would a priest of Set take it but to Stygia? The black sailors sensed his urgency and worked harder than they ever had under the lash, though they didn’t know his goal. They expected a bloody campaign of raiding and plunder and were satisfied. The men of the southern islands knew no other trade, and the Kushites among the crew eagerly joined in the thought of looting their own people, with the cold indifference of their race. Blood ties meant little; a victorious leader and personal gain meant everything.

Soon the coastline began to change. They no longer sailed past steep cliffs with blue hills rising behind them. Now the shore was the edge of wide meadows that barely rose above the water and stretched endlessly into the hazy distance. There were few harbors and even fewer ports, but the green plain was dotted with the cities of the Shemites; green sea lapping at the rim of green plains, and the ziggurats of the cities gleaming white in the sun, some small and distant.

Across the grazing lands moved herds of cattle, and short, broad riders with cylindrical helmets and curled blue-black beards, bows in their hands. This was the coast of Shem, where there was no law except what each city-state could enforce for itself. Far to the east, Conan knew, the meadows gave way to desert, where there were no cities and only nomadic tribes roamed freely.

Still they rowed southward, past the unchanging view of meadowlands dotted with cities, until at last the scenery began to shift again. Clumps of tamarind appeared, palm groves grew thicker. The shoreline became more broken, a marching wall of green fronds and trees, and behind them rose bare, sandy hills. Streams poured into the sea, and along their moist banks vegetation grew dense and varied.

At last they passed the mouth of a wide river that mixed its waters with the ocean and saw the great black walls and towers of Khemi rising against the southern horizon.

The river was the Styx, the true border of Stygia. Khemi was Stygia’s greatest port and, at that time, its most important city. The king lived in the older city of Luxur, but in Khemi ruled the priesthood—though people said the heart of their dark religion lay far inland, in a mysterious, deserted city near the Styx’s bank. This river, springing from some unknown source far in the unexplored lands south of Stygia, ran north for a thousand miles before turning westward for several hundred more, finally emptying into the ocean.

The Venturer, showing no lights, slipped past the port in the night, and before dawn she lay anchored in a small bay a few miles south of the city. It was surrounded by marsh—a green tangle of mangroves, palms, and vines—swarming with crocodiles and snakes. Discovery was highly unlikely. Conan knew the place well; he had hidden there before, in his corsair days.

As they glided silently past the city, whose great black bastions rose on the jutting points of land that enclosed the harbor, torches flared and glowed redly, and they heard the low thunder of drums. The port was not crowded with ships as the harbors of Argos were. The Stygians did not base their power and glory on ships and fleets. They had trading vessels and war galleys, yes, but not in proportion to their inland strength. Many of their ships traveled up and down the great river rather than along the sea coasts.

The Stygians were an ancient race, dark, mysterious, powerful, and merciless. Long ago their rule had stretched far north of the Styx, beyond the meadows of Shem, into the fertile uplands now inhabited by the peoples of Koth, Ophir, and Argos. Their borders had once met those of ancient Acheron. But Acheron had fallen, and the barbaric ancestors of the Hyborians had swept southward in wolfskins and horned helmets, driving the ancient rulers of the land before them. The Stygians had not forgotten.

In build, he wasn’t unlike the warrior caste of the Stygians, who were tall and muscular. Sun-bronzed, he was nearly as dark as many of them. His black, square-cut hair, held by a copper band, added to the resemblance. What set him apart were the subtle differences in his gait, his foreign features, and his blue eyes.

Still, the cloak was a good disguise, and he stayed in the shadows as much as possible, turning his head away when a native passed too close.

It was a dangerous game, and he knew he couldn’t keep up the deception for long. Khemi wasn’t like the sea ports of the Hyborian lands, where people of every race crowded the docks. The only foreigners here were black and Shemite slaves, and he looked like neither, though he resembled the Stygians more than them. Strangers weren’t welcome in Stygian cities; they were tolerated only as ambassadors or licensed traders, and even those weren’t allowed ashore after dark. Now there were no Hyborian ships in the harbor at all. A strange unease filled the city—a stirring of old ambitions, a whispering that only those who whispered could understand. Conan didn’t know it consciously, but his sharpened instincts sensed the unrest all around him.

If he were discovered, his fate would be terrible. They would kill him simply for being a stranger; if they realized he was Amra, the pirate chief who had ravaged their coasts with sword and fire—an involuntary shiver ran through Conan’s broad shoulders. He did not fear human enemies, nor any death by blade or flame. But this was a dark land of sorcery and nameless horrors. People said Set the Old Serpent, long ago banished from the Hyborian races, still lurked in the shadows of the cryptic temples, and dreadful, mysterious deeds were done in the night-shrouded shrines.

He had moved away from the waterfront streets with their wide steps leading down to the water and was entering the long, shadowy streets of the city’s main section. Here, the stalls were shut at dusk. The only lights along the streets were torches, flaring and smoking at wide intervals. The people walking the streets were few; they moved quickly and silently, and their numbers dwindled as the night grew later. Conan found the scene gloomy and unreal—the silence of the people, their secretive haste, the great black stone walls rising on each side of the streets. Somewhere, strange music murmured faintly. But the people still walking the streets on foot were commoners—slaves, tradesmen, prostitutes, laborers—and they grew fewer as he went on. He was heading toward the temple of Set, where he knew he was likely to find the priest he sought. He was certain that the man he had seen there had been the priest. Only occultists high in the ranks of the dreadful Black Ring possessed the power of the black hand that killed with a touch; and only such a man would dare to defy Thoth-Amon, whom the western world knew only as a figure of terror and legend.

Then from the shocked worshippers came a terrible cry: “Blasphemer! He has killed the sacred son of Set! Kill him! Kill! Kill!”  

Stones flew past him as the crazed Stygians rushed forward, screaming hysterically, while others poured from their houses on all sides, taking up the cry. Cursing, Conan spun around and darted into the black mouth of an alley. He heard the slap of bare feet on the paving stones behind him as he ran, guided more by instinct than sight, the walls echoing with the vengeful shouts of his pursuers. Then his left hand brushed against a break in the wall, and he turned sharply into another, narrower alley. On both sides rose sheer black stone walls. High above, he could see only a thin strip of stars. These towering walls, he knew, belonged to temples. Behind him, he heard the mob rush past the alley’s mouth, still shouting. Their cries grew fainter and faded away—they had missed the smaller passage and kept running straight ahead in the dark. Conan continued forward, though the thought of meeting another of Set’s “sons” in the darkness made him shudder.  

Then, somewhere ahead, he caught sight of a faint moving glow, like that of a crawling firefly. He stopped, pressed himself against the wall, and gripped his knife. He knew what it was—a man carrying a torch. Now it was close enough for him to see the dark hand holding it and the dim oval of a dark face. A few more steps and the man would surely see him. Conan crouched low like a tiger ready to spring—the torch stopped moving. A door appeared briefly in the glow as the torchbearer fumbled with it. Then it opened, the tall figure slipped inside, and darkness swallowed the alley again. There was something furtive and sinister about that shadowy figure sneaking through the door in the dark—a priest, perhaps, returning from some secret errand.  

Conan moved toward the door. If one man had come down this alley with a torch, others might come at any time. To go back the way he came could mean running straight into the mob still hunting him. At any moment they might return, find the narrow alley, and come charging down it. Hemmed in by those sheer, unscalable walls, he longed for escape—even if it meant entering some unknown building.  

The heavy bronze door wasn’t locked. It opened under his hand, and he peered through the crack. He was looking into a large square chamber of massive black stone. A torch burned low in a wall niche. The room was empty. He slipped through the lacquered door and closed it behind him.  

His sandaled feet made no sound as he crossed the black marble floor. A teak door stood partly open, and gliding through it, knife in hand, he entered a vast, dim, shadowy space whose high ceiling was only a suggestion of darkness far above, where the black walls rose upward. On all sides, black-arched doorways opened into the still hall. It was lit by strange bronze lamps that gave off a dim, eerie glow. On the far side of the hall, a broad black marble staircase without a railing climbed upward into the gloom, and above him, dim galleries hung like black stone ledges.  

Conan shivered; he was inside a temple of some Stygian god—if not Set himself, then one nearly as grim. And the shrine was not empty. In the center of the great hall stood a black stone altar, massive and somber, without carvings or decoration, and upon it coiled one of the great sacred serpents, its iridescent scales shimmering in the lamplight. It did not move, and Conan remembered hearing that the priests kept these creatures drugged part of the time. The Cimmerian took a cautious step out from the doorway, then suddenly shrank back—not into the room he had just left, but into a velvet-curtained alcove. He had heard a soft footstep nearby.

From one of the dark arches stepped out a tall, strong man wearing sandals and a silk loincloth, with a wide cloak flowing from his shoulders. His face and head were hidden by a huge mask, half-beast and half-human, with a crest from which a cluster of ostrich feathers waved.  

In certain rituals, the Stygian priests wore masks. Conan hoped the man wouldn’t notice him, but some instinct warned the Stygian. He suddenly turned away from the stairs, which had seemed to be his destination, and walked straight toward the alcove. As he yanked aside the velvet curtain, a hand shot out from the shadows, crushed the cry in his throat, dragged him headfirst into the recess, and drove a knife into him.  

Conan’s next move was the obvious one that logic suggested. He pulled off the grinning mask and put it over his own head. He threw the fisherman’s cloak over the priest’s body, hid it behind the curtains, and wrapped the priest’s mantle around his own broad shoulders. Fate had handed him a disguise. All of Khemi might now be searching for the blasphemer who had dared to defend himself against a sacred serpent, but who would ever think to look for him beneath a priest’s mask?  

He strode confidently out of the alcove and headed toward one of the arched doorways at random. But he had taken barely a dozen steps when he spun around, every sense alert to danger.  

A group of masked figures was coming down the stairs, dressed exactly as he was. He froze, caught in the open, and stood still, trusting his disguise, though cold sweat gathered on his forehead and the backs of his hands. No one spoke. Like ghosts they descended into the great hall and moved past him toward a black archway. The leader carried a black staff topped with a grinning white skull, and Conan realized it was one of those ritual processions that were impossible for outsiders to understand, yet played a powerful—and often sinister—role in Stygian religion. The last figure turned his head slightly toward the motionless Cimmerian, as if expecting him to follow. To refuse what was clearly expected of him would instantly raise suspicion. Conan fell in behind the last man and matched his stride to their slow, measured pace.  

They moved through a long, dark, vaulted hallway where, Conan noticed uneasily, the skull on the staff glowed with a ghostly light. A wave of wild, animal panic surged through him, urging him to draw his knife and slash at these eerie figures, to run madly from the grim, dark temple. But he forced himself to stay calm, fighting down the vague monstrous instincts that rose in his mind and filled the shadows with imagined horrors. He barely suppressed a sigh of relief when they finally passed through a massive double door, three times the height of a man, and stepped out into the starlight.  

Conan wondered if he dared slip away into some dark alley, but he hesitated, unsure. Down the long, dim street they padded silently, while the few people they met turned their faces away and fled. The procession kept well away from the walls; to suddenly dart into one of the alleys they passed would draw too much attention. While he fumed inwardly and cursed his luck, they reached a low-arched gate in the southern wall and passed through it. Ahead and around them lay clusters of low, flat-roofed mud houses and palm groves, shadowy under the stars. Now, if ever, Conan thought, was the time to escape his silent companions.  

But as soon as they left the gate behind, the silence ended. The masked figures began to mutter excitedly among themselves. Their slow, ritual pace was abandoned, the staff with its skull was tucked carelessly under the leader’s arm, and the whole group broke formation and hurried forward. Conan hurried with them, for in their low conversation he had caught a word that electrified him. The word was: “Thutothmes!”  

Conan stared with burning curiosity at his masked companions. One of them might be Thutothmes himself, or else they were heading to meet the man he sought. And he knew where they were going when, beyond the palms, he glimpsed a black triangular shape rising against the dark sky.  

They passed through the belt of huts and groves, and if anyone saw them, he made sure not to show himself. The huts were dark. Behind them, the black towers of Khemi loomed against the starry sky, their reflections trembling in the harbor waters; ahead stretched the dim desert, and somewhere a jackal yelped. The soft tread of their sandals made no sound in the sand. They might have been ghosts moving toward that colossal pyramid rising from the desert gloom. The whole sleeping land lay silent.  

Conan’s heart beat faster as he looked at the grim black wedge outlined against the stars. His eagerness to confront Thutothmes—whatever that meeting might bring—was mixed with a deep unease. No man could approach one of those dark stone pyramids without a sense of dread. Their very name was a symbol of horror among the northern peoples, and legends whispered that the Stygians had not built them at all; that they had already stood there when the dark-skinned race first came to the land of the great river, in ages beyond memory.  

As they neared the pyramid, he saw a faint glow near its base, which soon revealed itself as a doorway flanked by stone lions with women’s heads—cryptic, unreadable, nightmares turned to stone. The leader of the group walked straight toward the doorway, in whose deep shadow Conan saw a dim figure standing.  

The leader paused briefly beside this shadowy figure, then vanished into the dark interior, and one by one the others followed. As each masked priest passed through the gloomy entrance, he was stopped for a moment by the mysterious guardian, and something passed between them—a word or a gesture Conan couldn’t make out. Seeing this, the Cimmerian deliberately hung back, pretending to fumble with the strap of his sandal. Only when the last of the masked figures had gone inside did he straighten and approach the doorway.  

He wondered uneasily whether the temple’s guardian was even human, remembering the stories he had heard. But his doubts were soon eased. A dim bronze lamp glowing just inside the door lit a long, narrow corridor stretching into darkness, and a man stood silent at its entrance, wrapped in a wide black cloak. No one else was in sight. Clearly, the masked priests had already gone down the corridor.

Over the cloak that covered the lower part of his face, the Stygian’s sharp eyes studied Conan intently. With his left hand, he made a strange gesture. Taking a chance, Conan copied it. But apparently, another gesture was expected; the Stygian’s right hand flashed out from beneath his cloak, steel glinting, and his deadly thrust would have pierced the heart of an ordinary man.  

But he was facing someone whose muscles were trained to the speed of a jungle cat. Even as the dagger gleamed in the dim light, Conan caught the dark wrist and smashed his clenched right fist into the Stygian’s jaw. The man’s head slammed back against the stone wall with a dull crunch that told of a broken skull.  

For a moment Conan stood over him, listening intently. The cresset burned low, throwing vague shadows around the doorway. Nothing moved in the darkness beyond, though far away and below, it seemed he caught the faint, muffled sound of a gong.  

He bent down and dragged the body behind the great bronze door, which stood wide open inward, and then the Cimmerian moved cautiously but quickly down the corridor, toward whatever fate awaited him—something he didn’t even try to guess.  

He hadn’t gone far before he stopped, uncertain. The corridor split into two branches, and he had no way of knowing which path the masked priests had taken. Taking a chance, he chose the left. The floor sloped slightly downward and was worn smooth by many feet. Here and there a dim cresset cast a faint, eerie twilight. Conan wondered uneasily what purpose these massive structures had once served, and in what forgotten age they had been built. This was an ancient land—so ancient that no one knew how many ages the black temples of Stygia had stood beneath the stars.  

Narrow black arches opened occasionally to the right and left, but he kept to the main corridor, though a growing conviction told him he had taken the wrong path. Even with their head start, he should have caught up with the priests by now. He was growing uneasy. The silence felt almost solid, and yet he sensed he wasn’t alone. More than once, as he passed a dark archway, he felt the weight of unseen eyes watching him. He paused, half tempted to turn back to where the corridor had first divided. Suddenly he spun around, knife raised, every nerve alert.  

A girl stood at the mouth of a smaller tunnel, staring straight at him. Her ivory skin marked her as a Stygian of some ancient noble blood, and like all such women, she was tall, graceful, and voluptuous, her hair a great mass of black waves, with a sparkling ruby gleaming among them. Except for her velvet sandals and the wide, jewel-studded belt around her supple waist, she was completely naked.  

“What are you doing here?” she demanded.  

To answer would reveal that he was a foreigner. He stayed silent, a grim, shadowy figure in the grotesque mask with its floating plumes. His sharp eyes searched the shadows behind her and found them empty—but there could easily be armed men within her call.  

She stepped toward him, showing no fear, though suspicion flickered in her gaze.  

“You’re not a priest,” she said. “You’re a warrior. Even with that mask, it’s obvious. There’s as much difference between you and a priest as between a man and a woman. By Set!” she suddenly exclaimed, eyes widening. “I don’t believe you’re even a Stygian!”  

With a movement too fast to follow, his hand closed around her smooth throat, light as a caress.  

“Not a sound,” he muttered.  

Her cool ivory skin felt like marble, yet there was no fear in the wide, dark, mesmerizing eyes that met his.  

“Don’t be afraid,” she said calmly. “I won’t betray you. But are you insane, coming here—a stranger, a foreigner—into the forbidden temple of Set?”  

“I’m looking for the priest Thutothmes,” he said. “Is he in this temple?”  

“Why do you seek him?” she countered.  

“He has something of mine that was stolen.”  

“I’ll take you to him,” she offered so quickly that his suspicion flared.  

“Don’t play games with me, girl,” he growled.  

“I’m not playing. I have no love for Thutothmes.”  

He hesitated, then made up his mind; after all, he was as much in her power as she was in his.  

“Walk beside me,” he ordered, shifting his grip from her throat to her wrist. “But move carefully. If you make one suspicious move—”  

She led him down the sloping corridor, farther and farther, until there were no more cressets, and he had to feel his way in the dark, aware of her presence more by touch and instinct than by sight. Once, when he spoke to her, she turned her head toward him, and he was startled to see her eyes glowing like golden fire in the blackness. Vague doubts and monstrous suspicions haunted his mind, but he followed her through a twisting maze of dark corridors that even confused his primitive sense of direction. He silently cursed himself for a fool, letting himself be led into this black lair of mystery—but it was too late to turn back now. Again he sensed life and movement in the darkness around him, felt danger and hunger lurking impatiently in the shadows. Unless his ears deceived him, he heard a faint sliding sound that stopped and retreated at a low command from the girl.  

At last she brought him into a chamber lit by a strange seven-branched candelabrum, where black candles burned with an eerie glow. He knew they were deep beneath the earth. The room was square, with walls and ceiling of polished black marble, furnished in the style of ancient Stygia; there was an ebony couch covered in black velvet, and on a black stone platform lay a carved mummy case.  

Conan stood waiting, tense, watching the various black arches that opened into the chamber. But the girl made no move to go farther. Stretching out on the couch with feline grace, she clasped her hands behind her sleek head and looked at him from under long, drooping lashes.  

“Well?” he demanded impatiently. “What are you doing? Where’s Thutothmes?”  

“There’s no hurry,” she said lazily. “What’s an hour—or a day, or a year, or even a century, for that matter? Take off your mask. Let me see your face.”  

With a grunt of irritation, Conan pulled off the heavy headpiece, and the girl nodded approvingly as she studied his dark, scarred face and blazing eyes.

“There’s power in you—great power; you could strangle an ox.”  

He shifted uneasily, his suspicion growing. With his hand on his sword hilt, he peered into the shadowy arches.  

“If you’ve led me into a trap,” he said, “you won’t live to enjoy it. Are you going to get up from that couch and do what you promised, or do I have to—”  

His voice faded. He was staring at the mummy case, on which the face of the occupant was carved in ivory with the startling realism of a forgotten art. There was something disturbingly familiar about that carved mask, and with a jolt he realized what it was: it looked shockingly like the face of the girl lounging on the black couch. She could have been the model for it, but he knew the portrait was at least centuries old. Ancient hieroglyphs were scrawled across the lacquered lid, and, digging into his memory for scraps of learning picked up here and there in his adventurous life, he sounded them out and said aloud: “Akivasha!”  

“You’ve heard of Princess Akivasha?” asked the girl on the couch.  

“Who hasn’t?” he grunted. The name of that ancient, wicked, beautiful princess still lived across the world in song and legend, though ten thousand years had passed since the daughter of Tuthamon had feasted in purple splendor in the black halls of old Luxur.  

“Her only sin was that she loved life and everything it meant,” said the Stygian girl. “To win life, she courted death. She couldn’t bear the thought of growing old, shriveled, and worn, and dying at last like an old crone. She wooed Darkness like a lover, and his gift was life—life that, not being mortal life, can never age or fade. She went into the shadows to cheat age and death—”  

Conan glared at her, his eyes suddenly narrowing to burning slits. He spun around and ripped the lid from the sarcophagus. It was empty. Behind him the girl was laughing, and the sound froze his blood. He whirled back toward her, the short hairs on his neck bristling.  

“You are Akivasha!” he growled.  

She laughed, tossing back her shining hair, and spread her arms seductively.  

“I am Akivasha! I am the woman who never died, who never grew old! Fools say the gods lifted me from the earth, in the full bloom of my youth and beauty, to reign forever in some heavenly realm! No—it’s in the shadows that mortals find immortality! Ten thousand years ago I died to live forever! Give me your lips, strong man!”  

She rose gracefully and came to him, standing on tiptoe to fling her arms around his massive neck. Scowling down into her upturned, beautiful face, he felt both a terrible fascination and a cold dread.  

“Love me!” she whispered, head thrown back, eyes closed, lips parted. “Give me your blood to renew my youth and keep my eternal life! I’ll make you immortal too! I’ll teach you the wisdom of all ages, the secrets that have endured through the eons in the darkness beneath these temples. I’ll make you king of the shadowy host that revels among the tombs of the ancients when night covers the desert and bats cross the moon. I’m tired of priests and magicians, and of captive girls dragged screaming through the gates of death. I want a man. Love me, barbarian!”  

She pressed her dark head against his powerful chest, and he felt a sharp sting at the base of his throat. With a curse he tore her away and flung her sprawling across the couch.  

“Damn vampire!” Blood was trickling from a tiny wound in his neck.  

She reared up on the couch like a serpent ready to strike, the golden fires of hell blazing in her wide eyes. Her lips drew back, showing sharp white teeth.  

“Fool!” she screamed. “Do you think you can escape me? You’ll live and die in darkness! I’ve brought you far below the temple. You’ll never find your way out alone. You’ll never cut your way through those who guard the tunnels. Without my protection, the sons of Set would have devoured you long ago. Fool, I’ll still drink your blood!”  

“Stay back or I’ll cut you in half,” he growled, his skin crawling with disgust. “You may be immortal, but steel will still tear you apart.”  

As he backed toward the archway he had entered through, the light went out suddenly. All the candles were extinguished at once, though he couldn’t tell how—it wasn’t Akivasha who had touched them. But her mocking laughter rose behind him, sweet and poisonous like the music of hell, and he broke into a sweat as he groped through the darkness for the archway, nearly panicking. His fingers found an opening and he plunged through it. Whether it was the same arch he had entered by, he didn’t know, and didn’t care. His only thought was to escape the haunted chamber that had housed that beautiful, hideous, undead creature for so many centuries.  

His wandering through those black, twisting tunnels was a sweating nightmare. Behind and around him he heard faint slithering and gliding sounds, and once the echo of that sweet, hellish laughter he had heard in Akivasha’s chamber. He slashed wildly at noises and movements he sensed—or imagined—near him in the dark, and once his sword cut through something soft and thin that might have been cobwebs. He had a desperate feeling that he was being toyed with, lured deeper and deeper into endless night before being attacked by demonic claws and fangs.  

And through his fear ran a sickening disgust at what he had discovered. The legend of Akivasha was ancient, and among the evil tales told of her ran a thread of beauty and idealism—of eternal youth. To so many dreamers, poets, and lovers, she had not been just the wicked princess of Stygian legend, but the symbol of everlasting youth and beauty, shining forever in some distant realm of the gods. And this was the horrible truth. This vile corruption was the reality behind that “eternal life.” Through his physical revulsion ran the sense of a shattered dream of human worship, its glittering gold revealed as slime and cosmic filth. A wave of futility swept over him—a dim fear of the falseness of all human dreams and idols.

And now he knew his ears weren’t deceiving him. He was being followed, and whoever or whatever was after him was getting closer. In the darkness came shuffling and sliding sounds that no human feet could have made—nor the feet of any normal animal. Maybe the underworld had its own monstrous life. They were behind him. He turned to face them, though he couldn’t see a thing, and slowly backed away. Then the sounds stopped, even before he turned his head and saw, somewhere down the long corridor, a faint glow of light.

19 - IN THE HALL OF THE DEAD  
In the Hall of the Dead  

Conan moved carefully toward the light he had seen, listening intently behind him, but there was no further sound of pursuit, though he could feel the darkness alive with awareness.  

The glow wasn’t still; it moved, bobbing eerily along. Then he saw what caused it. The tunnel he was in crossed another, wider corridor some distance ahead. Along that corridor moved a strange procession—four tall, thin men in black hooded robes, leaning on staffs. The leader held a torch above his head—a torch that burned with a steady, unnatural light. Like ghosts they passed across his limited view and vanished, leaving only a fading glow to mark their passing. Their appearance was indescribably uncanny. They weren’t Stygians, nor like anything Conan had ever seen. He doubted they were even human. They looked like black phantoms, stalking like ghouls through the haunted tunnels.  

But his situation couldn’t be any worse than it already was. Before the inhuman feet behind him could start slithering forward again when the distant light faded, Conan was running down the corridor. He plunged into the other tunnel and saw, far ahead, small and distant, the strange procession moving within the glowing sphere. He crept silently after them, then suddenly pressed himself against the wall as he saw them stop and gather together as if discussing something. They turned as if to go back, and he slipped into the nearest archway. Feeling his way in the darkness—darkness he had grown so used to that he could almost see through it—he found that the tunnel curved rather than ran straight, and he moved back beyond the first bend so the strangers’ light wouldn’t fall on him as they passed.  

But as he stood there, he became aware of a low hum of sound from somewhere behind him, like the murmur of human voices. Moving down the corridor toward it, he confirmed what he had first suspected. Giving up his plan to follow the ghostly travelers to whatever destination they sought, he turned toward the voices instead.  

Soon he saw a glimmer of light ahead, and turning into the corridor from which it came, he saw a wide arch filled with a dim glow at the far end. On his left, a narrow stone staircase led upward, and instinctively cautious, he turned and climbed the stairs. The voices he heard came from beyond that glowing arch.  

The sounds faded below him as he climbed, and soon he emerged through a low arched doorway into a vast open space glowing with an eerie light.  

He was standing on a shadowy balcony looking down into a broad, dimly lit hall of enormous size. It was a hall of the dead, seen only by the silent priests of Stygia. Along the black walls rose tier upon tier of carved and painted sarcophagi. Each stood in a niche in the dark stone, and the tiers climbed higher and higher until they were lost in the shadows above. Thousands of carved faces stared down impassively at the group in the center of the hall, making them seem small and meaningless in the presence of so many dead.  

There were ten priests in the group, and though they had removed their masks, Conan recognized them as the same priests he had accompanied to the pyramid. They stood before a tall, hawk-faced man beside a black altar on which lay a mummy wrapped in rotting bandages. The altar seemed to stand in the heart of a living fire that pulsed and shimmered, dripping flakes of trembling golden flame onto the black stones around it. This dazzling light came from a great red jewel lying on the altar, and in its reflection the priests’ faces looked pale and corpse-like. As he watched, Conan felt the weight of all the long miles and weary days and nights of his quest pressing down on him, and he trembled with the wild urge to rush among those silent priests, cut his way through with mighty blows of his sword, and seize the red gem with desperate hands. But he forced himself to stay still, crouching in the shadow of the stone railing. A quick glance showed him that a stairway led down from the balcony into the hall, hugging the wall and half hidden in shadow. He peered into the dimness of the vast chamber, searching for other priests or worshipers, but saw only the group around the altar.  

In that great emptiness, the voice of the man beside the altar sounded hollow and ghostly:  

“…And so the word came southward. The night wind whispered it, the ravens croaked it as they flew, and the grim bats told it to the owls and the serpents that hide in ancient ruins. Werewolves and vampires knew, and the black-bodied demons that prowl by night. The sleeping Night of the World stirred and shook its heavy mane, and there began a beating of drums in deep darkness, and the echoes of strange cries frightened men who walked at dusk. For the Heart of Ahriman had come again into the world to fulfill its mysterious destiny.  

“Ask me not how I, Thutothmes of Khemi and the Night, heard the word before Thoth-Amon, who calls himself prince of all wizards. There are secrets not meant for ears even such as yours, and Thoth-Amon is not the only master of the Black Ring.  

“I knew, and I went to meet the Heart as it came southward. It was like a magnet drawing me without fail. From death to death it came, riding on a river of human blood. Blood feeds it, blood calls to it. Its power is greatest when there is blood on the hands that hold it, when it is taken by slaughter from its owner. Wherever it shines, blood is spilled and kingdoms fall, and the forces of nature are thrown into chaos.  

“And here I stand, master of the Heart, and I have summoned you secretly, my faithful ones, to share in the dark kingdom that is to come. Tonight you shall witness the breaking of Thoth-Amon’s chains that enslave us, and the birth of an empire.”

Who am I, even I, Thutothmes, to know what powers lie hidden and dreaming in those crimson depths? It holds secrets forgotten for three thousand years. But I will learn them. These will tell me!  

He gestured toward the silent figures lining the hall.  

See how they sleep, staring through their carved masks! Kings, queens, generals, priests, wizards—the dynasties and nobility of Stygia for ten thousand years! The touch of the Heart will awaken them from their long sleep. For ages the Heart throbbed and pulsed in ancient Stygia. Here was its home in the centuries before it journeyed to Acheron. The ancients knew its full power, and they will tell me, once I use its magic to restore them to life to serve me.  

I will rouse them, awaken them, learn their forgotten wisdom—the knowledge locked inside those withered skulls. With the lore of the dead, we will enslave the living! Yes, kings and generals and wizards of old will be our helpers and our slaves. Who will stand against us?  

Look! This dried, shriveled thing on the altar was once Thothmekri, a high priest of Set, who died three thousand years ago. He was a master of the Black Ring. He knew of the Heart. He will tell us of its powers.  

Lifting the great jewel, the speaker placed it on the mummy’s withered chest and raised his hand to begin an incantation. But the spell was never finished. With his hand raised and his lips parted, he froze, staring past his acolytes, who turned to look in the same direction.  

Through the black arch of a doorway, four gaunt, black-robed figures entered the great hall. Their faces were dim yellow ovals beneath their hoods.  

Who are you? demanded Thutothmes, his voice as dangerous as a cobra’s hiss. Are you insane, to invade the holy shrine of Set?  

The tallest of the strangers spoke, his voice flat and hollow like a Khitan temple bell.  

We follow Conan of Aquilonia.  

He is not here, said Thutothmes, shaking back his mantle from his right hand in a threatening gesture, like a panther unsheathing its claws.  

You lie. He is in this temple. We tracked him from a corpse behind the bronze door of the outer portal, through a maze of corridors. We were following his winding trail when we became aware of this gathering. We go now to take it up again. But first, give us the Heart of Ahriman.  

Death is the fate of madmen, murmured Thutothmes, stepping closer to the speaker. His priests closed in silently, but the strangers did not seem to notice.  

Who can look upon it without desire? said the Khitan. In Khitai we have heard of it. It will give us power over the people who cast us out. Glory and wonder dream in its crimson depths. Give it to us, before we kill you.  

A fierce cry rang out as a priest leaped forward with a flash of steel. Before he could strike, a scaly staff darted out and touched his chest, and he fell like a dead man. In an instant, the mummies were staring down on a scene of blood and horror. Curved knives flashed and turned red, snakelike staffs struck in and out, and whenever they touched a man, he screamed and died.  

At the first blow, Conan had leapt up and was racing down the stairs. He caught only glimpses of that brief, hellish fight—men swaying, locked in combat, streaming blood; one Khitan hacked nearly to pieces yet still standing and killing, until Thutothmes struck him on the chest with his open, empty hand, and he dropped dead, though naked steel had not been enough to destroy his unnatural life.  

By the time Conan’s pounding feet reached the floor, the fight was nearly over. Three of the Khitans were down, slashed, cut to ribbons, and disemboweled, but of the Stygians only Thutothmes remained standing.  

He rushed at the last Khitan, his empty hand raised like a weapon, and that hand was black as that of a dark-skinned man. But before he could strike, the staff in the tall Khitan’s hand darted out, seeming to lengthen as the yellow man thrust. The point touched Thutothmes’s chest, and he staggered; again and again the staff struck, and Thutothmes reeled and fell dead, his features swallowed in a spreading blackness that turned his whole body the same color as his enchanted hand.  

The Khitan turned toward the jewel burning on the mummy’s chest, but Conan was already there.  

In tense silence, the two faced each other amid the carnage, with the carved mummies staring down upon them.  

Far have I followed you, oh king of Aquilonia, said the Khitan calmly. Down the long river, over the mountains, across Poitain and Zingara, through the hills of Argos, and down the coast. It was not easy to pick up your trail from Tarantia, for the priests of Asura are cunning. We lost it in Zingara, but we found your helmet in the forest below the border hills, where you fought the ghouls of the woods. We almost lost the trail again tonight among these labyrinths.  

Conan realized he had been lucky to return from the vampire’s chamber by a different route than the one he had been led there by. Otherwise, he would have run straight into these yellow fiends instead of spotting them from afar as they tracked his scent like human bloodhounds, using whatever strange gift they possessed.  

The Khitan shook his head slightly, as if reading his thoughts.  

That doesn’t matter; the long hunt ends here.  

Why have you hunted me? demanded Conan, ready to move in any direction with the speed of a drawn bowstring.  

It was a debt to pay, answered the Khitan. To you, who are about to die, I will not withhold the truth. We were vassals of the king of Aquilonia, Valerius. Long we served him, but that service is ended now—my brothers by death, and I by fulfilling my duty. I will return to Aquilonia with two hearts: for myself, the Heart of Ahriman; for Valerius, the heart of Conan. A kiss from the staff that was cut from the living Tree of Death—

The staff shot out like a viper’s strike, but Conan’s knife was faster. The staff split into two writhing pieces, and then the flash of his sharp steel flared again like lightning, and the Khitan’s head rolled across the floor.  

Conan spun around and reached for the jewel—but then he recoiled, his hair standing on end, his blood freezing cold.  

The withered brown corpse no longer lay on the altar. The jewel now gleamed on the full, arched chest of a naked, living man lying among the rotting bandages. Living? Conan couldn’t tell. The eyes were like dark, cloudy glass, beneath which burned strange, inhuman fires.  

Slowly the man rose, taking the jewel in his hand. He stood beside the altar, dusky and naked, his face like a carved statue. Without a word, he stretched out his hand toward Conan, the jewel pulsing like a living heart within it. Conan took it, feeling a chilling sense that he was accepting a gift from the dead. He somehow knew that the proper spells had not been spoken—the ritual was incomplete—life had not been fully restored to the corpse.  

“Who are you?” demanded the Cimmerian.  

The reply came in a flat, lifeless tone, like water dripping from stalactites in a deep cave. “I was Thothmekri; I am dead.”  

“Well, then lead me out of this damned temple, will you?” Conan said, his skin crawling.  

With slow, mechanical steps, the dead man moved toward a black archway. Conan followed. A glance back showed him the vast, shadowy hall again, with its rows of sarcophagi, the corpses sprawled around the altar; the severed head of the Khitan he had killed stared blankly upward into the sweeping shadows.  

The glow of the jewel lit the black tunnels like an enchanted lamp, dripping golden fire. Once, Conan caught a glimpse of pale flesh in the shadows—he thought he saw the vampire Akivasha shrinking away from the jewel’s light; and with her, other, less human shapes scurried or lurched into the darkness.  

The dead man walked straight ahead, never turning his head, his pace steady and relentless as doom itself. Cold sweat gathered thick on Conan’s skin. Icy doubts gnawed at him. How could he know this dreadful being from the past was really leading him to freedom? Yet he knew that alone, he could never find his way through this bewitched maze of corridors and tunnels. He followed his ghastly guide through the blackness that surrounded them, filled with lurking shapes of horror and madness that shrank from the blinding glow of the Heart.  

Then the bronze doorway appeared before him, and Conan felt the night wind blowing across the desert. He saw the stars, and the starlit sands stretching out, the great black shadow of the pyramid lying across them. Thothmekri silently pointed into the desert, then turned and walked soundlessly back into the darkness. Conan watched that silent figure fade into the blackness, moving on noiseless, inevitable feet, like one returning to eternal sleep—or to a doom already known.  

With a curse, the Cimmerian leapt from the doorway and fled into the desert as if chased by demons. He didn’t look back at the pyramid or the dark towers of Khemi looming dimly across the sands. He ran south toward the coast, running like a man driven by uncontrollable panic. The fierce effort cleared his mind of dark cobwebs; the clean desert wind blew the nightmares from his soul, and his horror turned into a wild surge of triumph before the desert gave way to tangled swampland, where he saw black water ahead—and the Venturer anchored there.  

He crashed through the undergrowth, wading hip-deep through the marsh; then dove headfirst into the deep water, not caring about sharks or crocodiles, and swam to the galley. He was climbing up the chain onto the deck, dripping and exultant, before the watch even saw him.  

“Wake up, you dogs!” roared Conan, knocking aside the spear the startled lookout thrust at his chest. “Raise the anchor! Set the sails! Give that fisherman a helmet full of gold and put him ashore! Dawn’s coming soon, and before sunrise we’ll be racing for the nearest Zingaran port!”  

He swung the great jewel above his head, and it scattered bursts of light that splashed the deck with golden fire.  

Out of the Dust Shall Acheron Arise  

Winter had passed from Aquilonia. Leaves sprouted on the trees, and fresh grass smiled under the touch of warm southern winds. But many fields lay empty and untended, and many blackened heaps of ashes marked where proud villas or thriving towns had once stood. Wolves prowled openly along the overgrown highways, and bands of gaunt, lawless men skulked through the forests. Only in Tarantia were there feasts, wealth, and pageantry.  

Valerius ruled like a man touched by madness. Even many barons who had once welcomed his return finally cried out against him. His tax collectors crushed both rich and poor alike; the wealth of a plundered kingdom poured into Tarantia, which now looked less like the capital of a realm and more like the garrison of conquerors in a conquered land. Its merchants grew rich, but it was a dangerous prosperity; no one knew when he might be accused of treason on false charges, his property seized, and he himself thrown into prison or sent to the executioner’s block.  

Valerius made no effort to win over his subjects. He held power only through the Nemedian soldiers and desperate mercenaries. He knew he was Amalric’s puppet. He knew he ruled only by the Nemedian’s permission. He knew he could never hope to unite Aquilonia under his rule and throw off his masters’ yoke, for the outer provinces would resist him to the last drop of blood. And besides, the Nemedians would throw him from the throne if he tried to strengthen his kingdom. He was trapped in his own snare. The bitterness of defeated pride ate at his soul, and he plunged into a life of debauchery, living only for the moment, caring nothing for tomorrow.  

Yet there was cunning in his madness, so deep that even Amalric never suspected it. Maybe the wild, chaotic years of exile had bred in him a bitterness beyond normal understanding. Maybe his hatred of his current position had twisted that bitterness into a kind of insanity. Whatever the reason, he lived with one purpose: to bring ruin to everyone connected with him.

He knew his rule would end the moment he had fulfilled Amalric’s purpose. He also knew that as long as he kept oppressing his own kingdom, the Nemedian would allow him to remain on the throne, because Amalric wanted to crush Aquilonia into complete submission, destroy its last trace of independence, and then seize it for himself. He planned to rebuild it in his own way with his immense wealth and use its people and resources to take the crown of Nemedia from Tarascus. Amalric’s ultimate goal was to become emperor, and Valerius knew it. Valerius didn’t know whether Tarascus suspected this, but he did know that the king of Nemedia approved of his ruthless actions. Tarascus hated Aquilonia with a hatred born from old wars. He wanted nothing but the destruction of the western kingdom.  

Valerius planned to ruin the country so completely that not even Amalric’s wealth could ever rebuild it. He hated the baron just as much as he hated the Aquilonians, and he only hoped to live long enough to see Aquilonia in total ruin, with Tarascus and Amalric locked in a hopeless civil war that would destroy Nemedia as well.  

He believed that conquering the still-defiant provinces of Gunderland, Poitain, and the Bossonian Marches would mark the end of his reign. Once that was done, he would have served Amalric’s purpose and could be discarded. So he delayed the conquest of those provinces, limiting his efforts to pointless raids and skirmishes, meeting Amalric’s demands for action with all sorts of excuses and delays.  

His life became a cycle of feasts and wild debauchery. He filled his palace with the most beautiful girls in the kingdom, whether they were willing or not. He cursed the gods and sprawled drunkenly on the banquet hall floor, wearing the golden crown and staining his royal purple robes with spilled wine. In fits of bloodlust, he decorated the gallows in the market square with hanging corpses, fed the headsmen’s axes, and sent his Nemedian horsemen thundering across the land to plunder and burn. Driven mad, the land was in constant turmoil, with desperate revolts savagely crushed. Valerius plundered, raped, looted, and destroyed until even Amalric protested, warning him that he would bankrupt the kingdom beyond repair—unaware that this was exactly what Valerius intended.  

But while people in both Aquilonia and Nemedia spoke of the king’s madness, in Nemedia they also spoke often of Xaltotun, the masked one. Yet few ever saw him on the streets of Belverus. People said he spent much of his time in the hills, meeting in strange gatherings with the surviving remnants of an ancient race—dark, silent people who claimed descent from a forgotten kingdom. There were whispers of drums beating far up in the dreaming hills, of fires glowing in the night, and of strange chants carried on the wind—chants and rituals forgotten for centuries, remembered only as meaningless words muttered beside mountain hearths in villages whose people seemed oddly different from those of the valleys.  

No one knew the reason for these gatherings, unless it was Orastes, who often accompanied the Pythonian and whose face had grown increasingly haunted.  

Then, in the full bloom of spring, a sudden whisper swept across the dying kingdom and stirred the land to life. It came like a murmuring wind rising from the south, awakening men sunk in despair. No one could say exactly how it began. Some spoke of a strange, grim old woman who came down from the mountains, her hair streaming in the wind, with a great gray wolf following her like a dog. Others whispered of priests of Asura who slipped like shadows from Gunderland to Poitain and into the forest villages of the Bossonians.  

However the rumor spread, rebellion flared like wildfire along the borders. Isolated Nemedian garrisons were stormed and slaughtered, foraging parties were wiped out; the west was in arms again. But this uprising was different—it burned with fierce determination and inspired fury, not the desperate hopelessness of earlier revolts. It wasn’t just the common people; barons were fortifying their castles and defying the provincial governors. Bands of Bossonians were seen moving along the edges of the marches—sturdy, determined men in brigandines and steel caps, longbows in hand. From the stagnant decay of ruin, the realm was suddenly alive again—vibrant and dangerous. Amalric, alarmed, sent urgently for Tarascus, who came with an army.  

In the royal palace at Tarantia, the two kings and Amalric met to discuss the uprising. They had not summoned Xaltotun, who was absorbed in his mysterious studies in the Nemedian hills. Since that bloody day in the valley of the Valkia, they had not called on his magic, and he had withdrawn, speaking little to them and seeming indifferent to their schemes.  

They hadn’t called for Orastes either, but he came anyway, pale as sea foam before a storm. He stood in the gold-domed chamber where the kings met, and they stared in shock at his haggard face, seeing a fear they had never imagined Orastes could feel.  

“You look exhausted, Orastes,” said Amalric. “Sit on this couch, and I’ll have a slave bring you wine. You’ve ridden hard—”  

Orastes waved off the offer.  

“I’ve killed three horses on the road from Belverus. I can’t drink wine, I can’t rest, until I’ve said what I must say.”  

He paced back and forth as if some inner fire wouldn’t let him stand still, then stopped before his astonished companions.  

“When we used the Heart of Ahriman to bring a dead man back to life,” Orastes said abruptly, “we didn’t think about the consequences of meddling with the black dust of the past. The fault is mine—the sin is mine. We thought only of our own ambitions, forgetting what ambitions that man might have himself. And we’ve unleashed a demon upon the world, a fiend beyond human understanding. I’ve delved deeply into evil, but there’s a limit to how far I—or any man of my time and race—can go. My ancestors were pure men, untouched by demonic corruption; only I have sunk into the pits of darkness, and even my sins are bound by my own humanity. But behind Xaltotun lie a thousand centuries of black magic and devil worship, an ancient legacy of evil. He’s beyond our comprehension not only because he’s a sorcerer himself, but because he’s the descendant of a race of sorcerers.”

"I’ve seen things that have shattered my soul. In the heart of the sleeping hills, I’ve watched Xaltotun speak with the souls of the damned and summon the ancient demons of forgotten Acheron. I’ve seen the cursed descendants of that cursed empire worship him and hail him as their high priest. I’ve seen what he plans—and I tell you, it’s nothing less than the resurrection of the ancient, dark, terrible kingdom of Acheron!"

"What are you saying?" demanded Amalric. "Acheron is dust. There aren’t enough survivors left to form an empire. Not even Xaltotun can rebuild the dust of three thousand years."

"You know little of his dark powers," answered Orastes grimly. "I’ve seen the very hills take on a strange, ancient look under the spell of his incantations. I’ve glimpsed, like shadows behind reality, the faint shapes and outlines of valleys, forests, mountains, and lakes—not as they are now, but as they were in that dim past—and I’ve even sensed, more than seen, the purple towers of forgotten Python shimmering like misty figures in the twilight.

"And in the last gathering I attended with him, I finally understood his sorcery, while the drums thundered and the beast-like worshippers howled with their faces in the dust. I tell you, he intends to restore Acheron through his magic—through a blood sacrifice so vast the world has never seen its equal. He would enslave the world and, with a flood of blood, wash away the present and bring back the past!"

"You’re insane!" exclaimed Tarascus.

"Insane?" Orastes turned a haunted stare on him. "Can any man see what I’ve seen and stay completely sane? Yet I speak the truth. He plans the return of Acheron, with its towers, wizards, kings, and horrors, just as it was long ago. The descendants of Acheron will serve as the foundation on which he’ll rebuild, but it’s the blood and bodies of the people of today that will provide the mortar and stones for that rebuilding. I can’t tell you how. My mind reels when I try to understand. But I have seen! Acheron will be Acheron again, and even the hills, forests, and rivers will take back their ancient forms. Why not? If I, with my small knowledge, could bring back to life a man dead three thousand years, why couldn’t the greatest wizard in the world bring back to life a kingdom dead three thousand years? From the dust, Acheron will rise again at his command."

"How can we stop him?" asked Tarascus, shaken.

"There’s only one way," answered Orastes. "We must steal the Heart of Ahriman!"

"But I—" began Tarascus involuntarily, then quickly shut his mouth.

No one noticed him, and Orastes went on.

"It’s a power that can be used against him. With it in my hands, I might defy him. But how can we steal it? He’s hidden it in some secret place, one even a Zamorian thief couldn’t find. I can’t discover where it’s hidden. If only he would sleep again under the black lotus—but the last time he did was after the battle of Valkia, when he was exhausted from the great magic he had worked, and—"

The door, though locked and bolted, swung silently open, and Xaltotun stood before them—calm, composed, stroking his long beard—but the flickering lights of hell burned in his eyes.

"I’ve taught you too much," he said calmly, pointing a finger like a weapon of doom at Orastes. Before anyone could move, he threw a handful of dust on the floor near the priest’s feet. Orastes stood frozen like a statue. The dust flared and smoldered; a thin blue smoke rose and coiled upward around Orastes in a slender spiral. When it reached above his shoulders, it whipped around his neck with the speed of a striking snake. Orastes’ scream was cut off in a choking gurgle. His hands flew to his throat, his eyes bulged, his tongue protruded. The smoke looked like a blue rope tightening around his neck; then it faded and vanished, and Orastes collapsed to the floor, dead.

Xaltotun clapped his hands, and two men entered—men often seen with him—small, vile-looking, dark-skinned, with red slanted eyes and sharp, rat-like teeth. They said nothing. Lifting the corpse, they carried it away.

With a dismissive wave of his hand, Xaltotun sat down at the ivory table where the pale kings were gathered.

"Why are you meeting in secret?" he demanded.

"The Aquilonians have risen in the west," answered Amalric, recovering from the shock of Orastes’ death. "The fools believe Conan is alive and coming at the head of a Poitanian army to reclaim his kingdom. If he had reappeared right after Valkia, or if a rumor had spread that he lived, the central provinces wouldn’t have dared rise against us—they feared your powers too much. But they’ve grown so desperate under Valerius’ misrule that they’ll follow any man who can unite them against us, preferring sudden death to endless torture and misery.

"Of course, the rumor has long persisted that Conan wasn’t really killed at Valkia, but only recently have the masses begun to believe it. Pallantides has returned from exile in Ophir, swearing that the king was sick in his tent that day, and that a man-at-arms wore his armor. A squire who only lately recovered from a mace blow at Valkia confirms his story—or pretends to.

"An old woman with a pet wolf has wandered across the land, proclaiming that King Conan still lives and will return one day to reclaim his crown. And lately, the cursed priests of Asura are preaching the same thing. They claim they’ve received word by some mysterious means that Conan is returning to reconquer his realm. I can’t catch her or them. It’s obviously one of Trocero’s tricks. My spies tell me there’s undeniable proof that the Poitanians are gathering to invade Aquilonia. I believe Trocero will present some impostor and claim he’s King Conan."

Tarascus laughed, but there was no real conviction in his laughter. He secretly touched a scar beneath his tunic and remembered the ravens that had followed a fugitive’s trail; remembered the body of his squire, Arideus, brought back from the border mountains horribly mangled—by a great gray wolf, his terrified soldiers had said. But he also remembered a red jewel stolen from a golden chest while a wizard slept, and he said nothing.

Valerius remembered a dying nobleman who had gasped out a tale of terror, and he remembered four Khitans who had vanished into the southern labyrinths and never returned. But he kept silent, for hatred and suspicion of his allies gnawed at him like a worm, and he wanted nothing more than to see both the rebels and the Nemedians destroyed together in a death grip.  

But Amalric burst out, “It’s ridiculous to think Conan is still alive!”  

In response, Xaltotun tossed a roll of parchment onto the table.  

Amalric snatched it up and glared at it. A furious, incoherent cry escaped his lips. He read:  

To Xaltotun, grand fakir of Nemedia: Dog of Acheron, I’m coming back to my kingdom, and I plan to hang your hide on a bramble.  

Conan  

“A fake!” shouted Amalric.  

Xaltotun shook his head.  

“It’s real. I’ve compared it with the signature on the royal documents kept in the court archives. No one could copy that bold scrawl.”  

“Then if Conan truly lives,” muttered Amalric, “this rebellion won’t be like the others, because he’s the only man alive who can unite the Aquilonians. But,” he protested, “this doesn’t sound like Conan. Why would he warn us with his boasting? You’d think he’d strike without warning, like the barbarians do.”  

“We’re already warned,” said Xaltotun. “Our spies have reported preparations for war in Poitain. He couldn’t cross the mountains without us knowing, so he sends me his challenge in his usual manner.”  

“Why to you?” demanded Valerius. “Why not to me, or to Tarascus?”  

Xaltotun turned his unreadable gaze on the king.  

“Conan is wiser than you,” he said finally. “He already knows what you kings have yet to learn—that it’s not Tarascus, nor Valerius, nor Amalric, but Xaltotun who is the true master of the western nations.”  

They didn’t answer; they sat staring at him, struck by the chilling realization of the truth in his words.  

“There’s no path for me but the imperial road,” said Xaltotun. “But first we must destroy Conan. I don’t know how he escaped me at Belverus, for what happened while I lay in the sleep of the black lotus is hidden from me. But he’s in the south, raising an army. It’s his last desperate effort, made possible only by the despair of the people who have suffered under Valerius. Let them rise; I hold them all in the palm of my hand. We’ll wait until he moves against us, and then we’ll crush him once and for all.  

“Then we’ll crush Poitain and Gunderland and the foolish Bossonians. After them, Ophir, Argos, Zingara, Koth—all the nations of the world will be forged into one vast empire. You’ll rule as my satraps, and as my captains you’ll be greater than kings are now. I’m invincible, for the Heart of Ahriman is hidden where no man can ever use it against me again.”  

Tarascus turned his eyes away so Xaltotun wouldn’t read his thoughts. He knew the wizard hadn’t looked inside the golden chest with its carved serpents that seemed to sleep since he had placed the Heart within it. Strangely, Xaltotun didn’t know the Heart had been stolen; the strange jewel was beyond or outside the reach of his dark knowledge; his uncanny powers didn’t warn him that the chest was empty. Tarascus didn’t believe Xaltotun knew the full extent of Orastes’ revelations, for the Pythonian had spoken only of building a new earthly empire, not restoring Acheron. Tarascus didn’t think Xaltotun was yet completely confident in his power; if they needed his help for their ambitions, he needed theirs just as much. Magic still depended, to some degree, on sword strikes and lance thrusts. The king caught the meaning in Amalric’s secret glance—let the wizard use his sorcery to help them defeat their most dangerous enemy. There would be time enough later to turn on him. There might still be a way to outwit the dark power they had unleashed.  

21 - DRUMS OF PERIL  

The confirmation of war came when the army of Poitain, ten thousand strong, marched through the southern passes with banners flying and steel gleaming. And at their head, the spies swore, rode a giant figure in black armor, with the royal lion of Aquilonia embroidered in gold on the chest of his rich silken surcoat. Conan lived! The king lived! There was no doubt left in anyone’s mind, friend or foe alike.  

Along with the news of the southern invasion came word, brought by hard-riding messengers, that a host of Gundermen was moving south, joined by the barons of the northwest and the northern Bossonians. Tarascus marched with thirty-one thousand men to Galparan, on the river Shirki, which the Gundermen would have to cross to reach the towns still held by the Nemedians. The Shirki was a swift, turbulent river rushing southwest through rocky gorges and canyons, and there were few places where an army could cross at that time of year, when the stream was nearly full from the melting snows. All the land east of the Shirki was in Nemedian hands, and it was logical to assume that the Gundermen would try to cross either at Galparan or at Tanasul, which lay south of Galparan. Reinforcements were expected daily from Nemedia, until word came that the king of Ophir was making hostile moves on Nemedia’s southern border, and sending more troops would risk an invasion from the south.  

Amalric and Valerius marched out from Tarantia with twenty-five thousand men, leaving as large a garrison as they dared to prevent uprisings in the cities while they were gone. They wanted to meet and crush Conan before he could join forces with the kingdom’s rebels.  

The king and his Poitanians had crossed the mountains, but there had been no real battles, no attacks on towns or fortresses. Conan had appeared and vanished again. Apparently, he had turned west through the wild, sparsely populated hill country and entered the Bossonian marches, gathering recruits as he went. Amalric and Valerius, with their army of Nemedians, Aquilonian traitors, and savage mercenaries, moved through the land in frustrated anger, searching for an enemy who never appeared.

Amalric found it impossible to get anything more than vague, general news about Conan’s movements. His scouting parties often rode out and never came back, and it wasn’t unusual to find a spy nailed to an oak tree. The countryside was rising up in rebellion the way peasants do—savagely, murderously, and in secret. All Amalric knew for sure was that a large force of Gundermen and northern Bossonians was somewhere north of him, beyond the Shirki River, and that Conan, with a smaller force of Poitanians and southern Bossonians, was somewhere to the southwest.  

He began to fear that if he and Valerius advanced farther into the wild country, Conan might completely slip past them, circle around, and invade the central provinces behind them. Amalric pulled back from the Shirki valley and set up camp on a plain a day’s ride from Tanasul. There he waited. Tarascus stayed at Galparan, afraid that Conan’s maneuvers were meant to lure him south and let the Gundermen cross into the kingdom from the north.  

* * * * *  

Xaltotun came to Amalric’s camp in his chariot drawn by those eerie, tireless horses. He entered Amalric’s tent, where the baron was conferring with Valerius over a map spread out on an ivory camp table.  

Xaltotun crumpled the map and tossed it aside.  

“What your scouts can’t find out,” he said, “my spies tell me—though their reports are strangely unclear and incomplete, as if unseen forces were working against me.  

“Conan is advancing along the Shirki River with ten thousand Poitanians, three thousand southern Bossonians, and several western and southern barons with their retainers, totaling about five thousand more. An army of thirty thousand Gundermen and northern Bossonians is pushing south to join him. They’re in contact through secret channels used by those cursed priests of Asura, who seem to be working against me—and whom I’ll feed to a serpent when the battle’s done, I swear it by Set!  

“Both armies are heading for the crossing at Tanasul, but I don’t believe the Gundermen will cross the river. I think Conan will cross instead and join them.”  

“Why would Conan cross the river?”  

“Because it benefits him to delay the battle. The longer he waits, the stronger he becomes, and the more dangerous our position grows. The hills on the far side of the river are full of people fiercely loyal to him—outlaws, refugees, fugitives from Valerius’s cruelty. From all over the kingdom, men are hurrying to join his army, alone or in groups. Every day, our patrols are ambushed and wiped out by the locals. Rebellion is spreading through the central provinces and will soon break into open revolt. The garrisons we left there aren’t enough, and we can’t expect reinforcements from Nemedia anytime soon. I see Pallantides’s hand in this unrest along the Ophirean border—he has family in Ophir.  

“If we don’t catch and crush Conan quickly, the provinces behind us will erupt in flames. We’ll have to fall back to Tarantia to defend what we’ve taken, maybe even fight our way through a rebellious countryside with Conan’s entire army chasing us, and then endure a siege in the city itself, with enemies both inside and outside the walls. No, we can’t wait. We must destroy Conan before his army grows too large, before the central provinces rise up. Once his head hangs above the gate at Tarantia, you’ll see how fast the rebellion collapses.”  

“Why don’t you just cast a spell on his army and kill them all?” Valerius asked, half mocking.  

Xaltotun stared at the Aquilonian as if he could see the full measure of the madness behind those mocking eyes.  

“Don’t worry,” he said at last. “My sorcery will crush Conan in the end, like a lizard underfoot. But even magic works best when aided by pikes and swords.”  

“If he crosses the river and takes position in the Goralian hills, he might be hard to drive out,” said Amalric. “But if we catch him in the valley on this side, we can wipe him out. How far is Conan from Tanasul?”  

“At his current pace, he should reach the crossing sometime tomorrow night. His men are tough, and he’s pushing them hard. He should arrive at least a day before the Gundermen.”  

“Good!” Amalric slammed his fist on the table. “I can reach Tanasul before he does. I’ll send a rider to Tarascus, ordering him to follow me there. By the time he arrives, I’ll have cut Conan off from the crossing and destroyed him. Then our combined forces can cross the river and deal with the Gundermen.”  

Xaltotun shook his head impatiently.  

“That plan might work against anyone but Conan. But your twenty-five thousand men aren’t enough to destroy his eighteen thousand before the Gundermen arrive. They’ll fight like wounded panthers. And what if the Gundermen show up while the battle’s raging? You’ll be trapped between two armies and wiped out before Tarascus can reach you. He’ll get to Tanasul too late to help.”  

“Then what do you suggest?” demanded Amalric.  

“Move with your full strength against Conan,” said the man from Acheron. “Send a rider to tell Tarascus to join us here. We’ll wait for him, then march together to Tanasul.”  

“But while we wait,” protested Amalric, “Conan will cross the river and join the Gundermen.”  

“Conan will not cross the river,” said Xaltotun.  

Amalric’s head snapped up, and he stared into the dark, unreadable eyes.  

“What do you mean?”  

“Suppose there were torrential rains far to the north, at the source of the Shirki. Suppose the river flooded so badly that the crossing at Tanasul became impassable. Then we could bring up our entire force at our leisure, trap Conan on this side of the river, and crush him. And when the flood subsides—which I think would happen the next day—we could cross and destroy the Gundermen. That way, we could use our full strength against each smaller army in turn.”  

Valerius laughed, as he always did at the thought of anyone’s ruin, friend or foe, and ran a restless hand through his wild yellow hair. Amalric stared at the man from Acheron with a mix of fear and admiration.

“If we could catch Conan in the Shirki Valley, with the hill ridges on his right and the river in flood on his left,” he admitted, “with our full force we could wipe him out completely. Do you think—are you sure—do you believe such rains will fall?”  

“I’m going to my tent,” replied Xaltotun, standing up. “Necromancy isn’t done with a wave of the hand. Send a rider to Tarascus. And let no one come near my tent.”  

That last order was unnecessary. No man in that army could have been bribed to approach that eerie black silk pavilion, whose door flaps were always tightly closed. No one but Xaltotun ever entered it, yet voices were often heard coming from within; its walls sometimes billowed though there was no wind, and strange music drifted out. Sometimes, deep in the night, its silken walls glowed red from flickering flames inside, casting distorted shadows that moved back and forth.  

Lying in his own tent that night, Amalric heard the steady beat of a drum coming from Xaltotun’s tent; through the darkness it thudded rhythmically, and at times the Nemedian could have sworn he heard a deep, croaking voice blending with the drum’s pulse. He shuddered, for he knew that voice didn’t belong to Xaltotun. The drum murmured and rolled on like distant thunder, and before dawn Amalric, glancing from his tent, caught the red flicker of lightning far off on the northern horizon. In every other part of the sky the great stars shone white and steady. But that distant lightning flashed without pause, like the crimson gleam of firelight on a tiny spinning blade.  

* * * * *  

At sunset the next day, Tarascus arrived with his army, dusty and exhausted from hard marching, the foot soldiers straggling hours behind the cavalry. They camped on the plain near Amalric’s position, and at dawn the combined army moved westward.  

A swarm of scouts rode ahead, and Amalric waited impatiently for them to return with news of the Poitanians trapped beside a raging flood. But when the scouts met the column, they brought word that Conan had already crossed the river.  

“What?” exclaimed Amalric. “Did he cross before the flood?”  

“There was no flood,” said the scouts, confused. “Late last night he reached Tanasul and sent his army across.”  

“No flood?” said Xaltotun, startled for the first time since Amalric had known him. “Impossible! There were heavy rains on the headwaters of the Shirki last night and the night before!”  

“That may be true, my lord,” said the scout. “The water was muddy, and the people of Tanasul said the river rose maybe a foot yesterday, but not enough to stop Conan’s crossing.”  

Xaltotun’s magic had failed! The thought pounded in Amalric’s mind. His dread of this strange man from the past had grown steadily since that night in Belverus when he had seen a brown, shriveled mummy swell and transform into a living man. And the death of Orastes had turned that lurking horror into active fear. In his heart was a grim certainty that the man—or demon—was unbeatable. Yet now he had undeniable proof of his failure.  

Still, even the greatest sorcerers might fail at times, the baron thought. In any case, he dared not defy the man from Acheron—at least not yet. Orastes was dead, writhing in some nameless hell only Mitra might know, and Amalric knew his sword would hardly succeed where the black wisdom of the renegade priest had failed. Whatever monstrous thing Xaltotun was plotting lay in the uncertain future. Conan and his army were a threat of the present, one that Xaltotun’s sorcery might yet be needed to overcome before the game was finished.  

* * * * *  

They reached Tanasul, a small fortified village where a ridge of rocks formed a natural bridge across the river, passable except during the worst floods. Scouts brought word that Conan had taken position in the Goralian Hills, which began to rise a few miles beyond the river. And just before sundown, the Gundermen had joined his camp.  

Amalric looked at Xaltotun, whose face was unreadable and alien in the torchlight. Night had fallen.  

“What now? Your magic has failed. Conan faces us with an army nearly as strong as ours, and he holds the better ground. We have two bad choices: stay here and wait for his attack, or fall back toward Tarantia and wait for reinforcements.”  

“If we wait, we’re finished,” said Xaltotun. “Cross the river and camp on the plain. We’ll attack at dawn.”  

“But his position is too strong!” protested Amalric.  

“Fool!” A burst of fury cracked through the wizard’s calm. “Have you forgotten Valkia? Because some obscure elemental force stopped the flood, do you think I’m powerless? I had planned for your spears to destroy our enemies, but don’t fear—it will be my arts that crush their army. Conan is trapped. He’ll never see another sunset. Cross the river!”  

They crossed by torchlight. The horses’ hooves clinked on the rocky bridge and splashed through the shallows. The torchlight glinted red on shields and breastplates, reflecting in the black water. The rock bridge was wide enough, but even so, it was past midnight before the army was camped on the plain beyond. Above them, they could see fires burning red in the distance. Conan had turned to make his stand in the Goralian Hills, which had more than once served as the last refuge of an Aquilonian king.  

Amalric left his pavilion and paced restlessly through the camp. A strange glow flickered inside Xaltotun’s tent, and from time to time a demonic cry tore through the silence, accompanied by the low, sinister rustle of a drum.  

Amalric, his instincts sharpened by the night and the tension, felt that Xaltotun was facing more than physical opposition. Doubts about the wizard’s power gnawed at him. He looked up at the fires high above, and his face hardened. He and his army were deep in hostile territory. Up there in those hills lurked thousands of savage figures whose hearts and souls had been stripped of all emotion and hope except a burning hatred for their conquerors, a mad thirst for revenge. Defeat would mean annihilation, retreat through a land swarming with blood-crazed enemies. And tomorrow he must throw his army against the fiercest warrior in the western world and his desperate horde. If Xaltotun failed them now—

Half a dozen armored soldiers stepped out of the shadows. The firelight reflected off their breastplates and helmet crests. Between them, they half-led, half-dragged a gaunt figure dressed in filthy rags.  

Saluting, they said, “My lord, this man came to the outposts and said he wanted to speak with King Valerius. He’s an Aquilonian.”  

He looked more like a wolf—a wolf scarred by traps. Old wounds from shackles showed on his wrists and ankles. A large brand, the mark of a hot iron, marred his face. His eyes glared through the tangle of his matted hair as he crouched before the baron.  

“Who are you, you filthy dog?” demanded the Nemedian.  

“Call me Tiberias,” the man replied, his teeth clicking in an involuntary spasm. “I’ve come to tell you how to catch Conan.”  

“A traitor, huh?” rumbled the baron.  

“People say you have gold,” the man muttered, shivering under his rags. “Give me some! Give me gold and I’ll show you how to defeat the king!” His eyes were wide and glassy, his outstretched hands trembling like claws.  

Amalric shrugged in disgust. But no tool was too low for his use.  

“If you’re telling the truth, you’ll have more gold than you can carry,” he said. “If you’re lying or spying, I’ll have you crucified upside down. Bring him along.”  

In Valerius’s tent, the baron pointed to the man crouching and shivering before them, clutching his rags around him.  

“He says he knows a way to help us tomorrow. We’ll need help if Xaltotun’s plan keeps failing. Speak, dog.”  

The man’s body twisted in strange spasms. His words came out in a stumbling rush:  

“Conan’s camp is at the head of the Valley of Lions. It’s shaped like a fan, with steep hills on both sides. If you attack tomorrow, you’ll have to march straight up the valley. You can’t climb the hills on either side. But if King Valerius will accept my service, I’ll guide him through the hills and show him how to come up behind King Conan. But if we’re going to do it, we must leave soon. It’s many hours of riding—you have to go miles west, then miles north, then turn east to come into the Valley of Lions from behind, like the Gundermen did.”  

Amalric hesitated, stroking his chin. In these chaotic times, it wasn’t unusual to find men willing to sell their souls for a few gold coins.  

“If you lead me astray, you’ll die,” said Valerius. “You understand that, don’t you?”  

The man shivered, but his wide eyes didn’t waver.  

“If I betray you, kill me!”  

“Conan won’t dare split his forces,” mused Amalric. “He’ll need every man to repel our attack. He can’t spare any to set ambushes in the hills. Besides, this fellow knows his life depends on leading you as he promised. Would a dog like him sacrifice himself? Nonsense! No, Valerius, I think the man’s telling the truth.”  

“Or he’s an even bigger thief than most, since he’d sell out his own liberator,” laughed Valerius. “Very well. I’ll follow the dog. How many men can you spare me?”  

“Five thousand should be enough,” answered Amalric. “A surprise attack on their rear will throw them into confusion, and that’ll be enough. I’ll expect your attack around noon.”  

“You’ll know when I strike,” said Valerius.  

As Amalric returned to his pavilion, he was pleased to note that Xaltotun was still in his tent—judging by the blood-chilling cries that occasionally pierced the night air. When he later heard the clink of steel and the jingle of bridles in the darkness, he smiled grimly. Valerius had nearly outlived his usefulness. The baron knew Conan was like a wounded lion—deadly even in his final moments. When Valerius struck from behind, the Cimmerian’s desperate counterattack might well destroy his rival before he himself fell. All the better. Amalric felt he could easily do without Valerius once the path to a Nemedian victory was cleared.  

* * * * *  

The five thousand horsemen who rode with Valerius were mostly hardened Aquilonian deserters. In the still starlight, they moved out of the sleeping camp, following the westward line of the dark hills rising against the stars ahead. Valerius rode at the front, with Tiberias beside him, a leather thong tied to the prisoner’s wrist and held by a soldier riding on his other side. Others followed close behind with drawn swords.  

“Try to trick us and you die instantly,” Valerius warned. “I don’t know every sheep path in these hills, but I know enough about the land to know which direction we must take to come in behind the Valley of Lions. Don’t lead us astray.”  

The man ducked his head, his teeth chattering as he babbled assurances of loyalty, staring stupidly up at the banner above him—the golden serpent of the old dynasty.  

Skirting the outer edges of the hills that enclosed the Valley of Lions, they swung wide to the west. After an hour’s ride, they turned north, pushing through wild, rugged hills, following faint trails and twisting paths. Sunrise found them several miles northwest of Conan’s position, and there the guide turned east, leading them through a maze of ridges and crags. Valerius nodded, judging their position by the peaks jutting above the others. He had kept his bearings well enough to know they were still heading in the right direction.  

But suddenly, without warning, a gray, misty mass came rolling down from the north, covering the slopes and spreading through the valleys. It blotted out the sun; the world became a blind gray void where they could see only a few yards ahead. Their advance turned into a stumbling, groping confusion. Valerius cursed. He could no longer see the peaks that had guided him. He had to rely completely on the treacherous guide. The golden serpent banner hung limp in the windless air.

At that moment, Tiberias seemed confused; he stopped and looked around uncertainly.  

“Are you lost, dog?” Valerius demanded harshly.  

“Listen!”  

Somewhere ahead of them, a faint vibration began—the rhythmic rumble of a drum.  

“Conan’s drum!” exclaimed the Aquilonian.  

“If we’re close enough to hear the drum,” said Valerius, “why don’t we hear the shouting and the clash of weapons? Surely the battle has begun.”  

“The gorges and the winds play strange tricks,” answered Tiberias, his teeth chattering with the fever that often afflicts men who have spent too much time in damp underground dungeons.  

“Listen!”  

Faintly, a low muffled roar reached their ears.  

“They’re fighting down in the valley!” cried Tiberias. “The drum is beating on the heights. Let’s hurry!”  

He rode straight toward the sound of the distant drum, as if he finally knew where he was going. Valerius followed, cursing the fog. Then it occurred to him that the fog would hide his approach. Conan wouldn’t see him coming. He would be at the Cimmerian’s back before the midday sun burned away the mist.  

Right now, he couldn’t tell what lay on either side—whether cliffs, thickets, or gorges. The drum throbbed steadily, growing louder as they advanced, but they heard no more sounds of battle. Valerius had no idea which direction they were heading. He started when he saw gray rock walls looming through the smoky haze on both sides and realized they were riding through a narrow pass. But the guide showed no sign of fear, and Valerius sighed in relief when the walls widened and disappeared again into the fog. They were through the pass; if an ambush had been planned, it would have been there.  

But now Tiberias stopped again. The drum was louder, and Valerius couldn’t tell where the sound was coming from. Now it seemed ahead, now behind, now to one side or the other. Valerius glared around impatiently, sitting on his warhorse with wisps of mist curling around him and moisture glistening on his armor. Behind him, the long lines of steel-clad riders faded away into the fog like ghosts.  

“Why do you hesitate, dog?” he demanded.  

The man seemed to be listening to the ghostly drum. Slowly, he straightened in his saddle, turned his head, and faced Valerius, and the smile on his lips was terrible to see.  

“The fog is thinning, Valerius,” he said in a strange new voice, pointing a bony finger. “Look!”  

The drum was silent. The fog was fading. First, the tops of cliffs appeared above the gray clouds, tall and ghostly. Lower and lower the mists sank, shrinking and vanishing. Valerius rose in his stirrups with a cry that the horsemen behind him echoed. All around them, cliffs towered high. They were not in a wide open valley as he had thought—they were in a dead-end gorge, walled by sheer cliffs hundreds of feet high. The only way in or out was the narrow pass they had ridden through.  

“Dog!” Valerius struck Tiberias full in the mouth with his mailed fist. “What devil’s trick is this?”  

Tiberias spat out blood and shook with terrible laughter.  

“A trick that will rid the world of a beast! Look, dog!”  

Again Valerius cried out, more in rage than fear.  

The pass was blocked by a wild and terrible band of men who stood silent as statues—ragged, wild-haired men with spears in their hands—hundreds of them. And on the cliffs above appeared other faces—thousands of faces—wild, gaunt, savage faces, marked by fire, steel, and starvation.  

“A trick of Conan’s!” shouted Valerius furiously.  

“Conan knows nothing of it,” laughed Tiberias. “This was the plan of broken men—men you ruined and turned into beasts. Amalric was right. Conan hasn’t divided his army. We are the rabble who followed him, the wolves who hid in these hills, the homeless, the hopeless. This was our plan, and the priests of Asura helped us with their mist. Look at them, Valerius! Each one bears your mark—on his body or on his heart!  

“Look at me! You don’t recognize me, do you? What about this scar your hangman burned into me? Once you knew me. Once I was Lord of Amilius—the man whose sons you murdered, whose daughter your mercenaries violated and killed. You said I wouldn’t sacrifice myself to trap you? By the gods, if I had a thousand lives, I’d give them all to buy your doom!  

“And I have bought it! Look at the men you destroyed—dead men who once played the king! Their hour has come! This gorge is your tomb. Try to climb the cliffs—they’re steep, they’re high. Try to fight your way back through the pass—spears will block your path, boulders will crush you from above! Dog! I’ll be waiting for you in hell!”  

Throwing back his head, he laughed until the rocks echoed. Valerius leaned from his saddle and slashed down with his great sword, cutting through shoulder and chest. Tiberias sank to the ground, still laughing horribly through the gurgle of his own blood.  

The drums began again, surrounding the gorge with guttural thunder; boulders came crashing down; above the screams of dying men, arrows shrieked in blinding clouds from the cliffs.  

Dawn was just whitening the east when Amalric drew up his forces at the mouth of the Valley of Lions. This valley was flanked by low, rolling but steep hills, and the ground rose in a series of uneven natural terraces. On the highest of these terraces, Conan’s army held its position, waiting for the attack. The host that had joined him, marching down from Gunderland, hadn’t been made up only of spearmen. With them had come seven thousand Bossonian archers and four thousand barons and their retainers from the north and west, swelling the ranks of his cavalry.  

The pikemen were drawn up in a tight wedge-shaped formation at the narrow head of the valley. There were nineteen thousand of them, mostly Gundermen, though about four thousand were Aquilonians from other provinces. On either side of them stood five thousand Bossonian archers. Behind the pikemen, the knights sat motionless on their horses, lances raised: ten thousand knights of Poitain, nine thousand Aquilonians—barons and their retainers.

It was a strong position. His flanks couldn’t be turned, because that would mean climbing the steep, wooded hills under a storm of Bossonian arrows and swords. His camp lay directly behind him, in a narrow, steep-sided valley that was really just an extension of the Valley of Lions, rising to a higher level. He didn’t fear a surprise attack from the rear, since the hills behind him were full of refugees and broken men whose loyalty to him was beyond question.

But if his position was hard to attack, it was just as hard to escape from. It was both a fortress and a trap for the defenders—a desperate last stand for men who didn’t expect to live unless they won. The only possible retreat was through the narrow valley behind them.

Xaltotun climbed a hill on the left side of the valley, near its wide mouth. This hill rose higher than the others and was called the King’s Altar, though the reason for that name had long been forgotten. Only Xaltotun remembered, and his memory stretched back three thousand years.

He wasn’t alone. His two familiars—silent, hairy, secretive, and dark—were with him, carrying a young Aquilonian girl bound hand and foot. They laid her on an ancient stone that looked strangely like an altar, crowning the top of the hill. For centuries it had stood there, worn by wind and rain until many believed it was just a naturally shaped rock. But Xaltotun knew what it really was and why it stood there. The familiars slipped away, their bent backs like silent gnomes, and Xaltotun stood alone beside the altar, his dark beard blowing in the wind as he looked down over the valley.

He could see all the way back to the winding Shirki River and up into the hills beyond the valley’s head. He could see the gleaming wedge of steel drawn up at the top of the terraces, the helmets of the archers glinting among the rocks and bushes, the silent knights motionless on their horses, their pennons streaming above their helmets, their lances rising like a forest of spears.

Looking the other way, he could see the long, tight ranks of the Nemedians moving in shining lines of steel into the mouth of the valley. Behind them stretched the bright pavilions of the lords and knights and the dull tents of the common soldiers, reaching almost to the river.

Like a river of molten steel, the Nemedian host flowed into the valley, the great scarlet dragon banner rippling above it. First came the bowmen, marching in even ranks, crossbows half raised, bolts loaded, fingers on the triggers. Behind them came the pikemen, and behind those the real strength of the army—the mounted knights, banners unfurled in the wind, lances lifted, riding their great horses forward as if they were heading to a feast.

And higher up on the slopes, the smaller Aquilonian force stood grim and silent.

There were thirty thousand Nemedian knights, and as in most Hyborian nations, the chivalry was the sword of the army. The foot soldiers were used only to clear the way for the charge of the armored knights. There were twenty-one thousand of these footmen—pikemen and archers.

The bowmen began shooting as they advanced, keeping their formation, loosing their bolts with a whir and a snap. But the bolts fell short or bounced harmlessly off the overlapping shields of the Gundermen. Before the crossbowmen could get close enough to kill, the arching arrows of the Bossonians were tearing through their ranks.

After a short, useless exchange of fire, the Nemedian bowmen began to fall back in disorder. Their armor was light, and their weapons were no match for the Bossonian longbows. The western archers were sheltered by rocks and bushes. Besides, the Nemedian foot soldiers lacked the spirit of the horsemen, knowing they were being used only to clear the way for the knights.

The crossbowmen fell back, and through the gaps in their lines the pikemen advanced. These were mostly mercenaries, and their masters didn’t hesitate to sacrifice them. They were meant to cover the advance of the knights until the latter were close enough to strike. So while the crossbowmen fired from both flanks at long range, the pikemen marched straight into the deadly storm from above, and behind them the knights came on.

When the pikemen began to waver under the savage rain of death whistling down the slopes, a trumpet sounded, their ranks split to right and left, and through them thundered the armored knights.

They charged straight into a storm of deadly arrows. The long shafts found every gap in their armor and in the coverings of their horses. Horses scrambling up the grassy terraces reared and fell backward, crushing their riders. Steel-clad bodies littered the slopes. The charge faltered and rolled back.

Down in the valley, Amalric reformed his ranks. Tarascus was fighting with drawn sword under the scarlet dragon, but it was the Baron of Tor who commanded that day. Amalric cursed as he looked at the forest of lance tips visible above and beyond the helmets of the Gundermen. He had hoped his retreat would lure the knights into charging down the slopes after him, where they could be raked from both sides by his archers and overwhelmed by his cavalry. But they hadn’t moved. Camp servants brought water skins from the river. Knights took off their helmets and drenched their sweating heads. The wounded on the slopes screamed in vain for water. In the upper valley, springs supplied the defenders. They didn’t thirst that long, hot spring day.

On the King’s Altar, beside the ancient carved stone, Xaltotun watched the steel tide surge and break. The knights came on, plumes waving, lances dipping. Through a whistling storm of arrows they charged, only to crash like a thunderous wave against the bristling wall of spears and shields. Axes rose and fell above the plumed helmets, spears thrust upward, bringing down horses and riders. The pride of the Gundermen was as fierce as that of the knights. They were not fodder to be sacrificed for the glory of others. They were the finest infantry in the world, with a tradition that made their morale unshakable. The kings of Aquilonia had long known the value of unbreakable infantry. They held their formation firm; over their shining ranks flew the great lion banner, and at the tip of the wedge a giant figure in black armor roared and struck like a storm, wielding a blood-soaked axe that split steel and bone alike.

The Nemedians fought as bravely as their proud traditions demanded. But they couldn’t break through the iron wedge, and from the wooded hills on both sides, arrows tore mercilessly into their tightly packed ranks. Their own archers were useless, their pikemen unable to climb the slopes and engage the Bossonians. Slowly, stubbornly, grimly, the hardened knights fell back, counting the empty saddles among them. Above them, the Gundermen made no shout of victory. They closed their ranks, filling the gaps left by the fallen. Sweat ran into their eyes beneath their steel helmets. They gripped their spears and waited, their fierce hearts swelling with pride that their king fought beside them on foot. Behind them, the Aquilonian knights had not moved. They sat their horses, grim and motionless.  

A knight spurred his sweating horse up the hill called the King’s Altar and glared at Xaltotun with bitter eyes.  

“Amalric sends word that it’s time to use your magic, wizard,” he said. “We’re dying like flies down there in the valley. We can’t break their lines.”  

Xaltotun seemed to grow taller, more terrible, and filled with power.  

“Go back to Amalric,” he said. “Tell him to reform his ranks for a charge, but to wait for my signal. Before that signal comes, he will see something he’ll remember until the day he dies.”  

The knight saluted, as if compelled against his will, and galloped down the hill at breakneck speed.  

Xaltotun stood beside the dark altar stone and looked across the valley—at the dead and wounded lying on the terraces, at the grim, blood-stained band holding the slopes, at the dusty, steel-clad ranks reforming in the valley below. He looked up at the sky, then down at the slim white figure lying on the dark stone. Lifting a dagger engraved with ancient hieroglyphs, he began to chant an age-old invocation:  

“Set, god of darkness, scaly lord of shadows, by the blood of a virgin and the sevenfold symbol I call to your sons beneath the black earth! Children of the depths, beneath the red earth, under the black earth, awaken and shake your dreadful manes! Let the hills tremble and the stones fall upon my enemies! Let the sky darken above them, the ground quake beneath their feet! Let a wind from the deep black earth rise beneath them, to blacken and shrivel them—”  

He stopped suddenly, dagger raised. In the tense silence, the roar of the armies below drifted up on the wind.  

On the other side of the altar stood a man in a black hooded robe, his cowl shadowing pale, delicate features and calm, dark eyes.  

“Dog of Asura!” hissed Xaltotun, his voice like an angry serpent. “Are you mad, that you come seeking your death? Ho, Baal! Chiron!”  

“Call again, dog of Acheron!” said the other, laughing. “Call loudly. They won’t hear you unless your cries echo in hell.”  

From a thicket at the edge of the ridge came an old woman in peasant clothes, her gray hair flowing over her shoulders, a great gray wolf padding at her heels.  

“Witch, priest, and wolf,” muttered Xaltotun grimly, and laughed. “Fools, to pit your cheap tricks against my power! With a wave of my hand I could sweep you from my path!”  

“Your power is like straw in the wind, dog of Python,” replied the Asurian. “Have you wondered why the Shirki didn’t flood and trap Conan on the far bank? When I saw the lightning last night, I guessed your plan, and my spells scattered the clouds you summoned before they could release their torrents. You didn’t even know your rain-magic had failed.”  

“You lie!” shouted Xaltotun, though his voice had lost its confidence. “I felt the clash of a strong sorcery against mine—but no man alive could undo the rain-magic once it’s cast, unless he holds the very heart of sorcery itself.”  

“But the flood you planned never came,” said the priest. “Look at your allies in the valley, Pythonian! You’ve led them to slaughter! They’re caught in the jaws of the trap, and you can’t help them. Look!”  

He pointed. Out of the narrow gorge of the upper valley, behind the Poitanians, a horseman came racing, swinging something above his head that flashed in the sunlight. Recklessly he charged down the slopes, through the ranks of the Gundermen, who roared deeply and clashed their spears and shields like thunder rolling through the hills. On the terraces between the armies, the sweat-lathered horse reared and plunged, while its wild rider shouted and waved the thing in his hands like a madman. It was the torn remnant of a scarlet banner, and the sun glinted on the golden scales of a serpent that coiled upon it.  

“Valerius is dead!” cried Hadrathus, his voice ringing. “A fog and a drum lured him to his doom! I gathered that fog, dog of Python, and I scattered it! I, with my magic that is greater than yours!”  

“What does it matter?” roared Xaltotun, a terrible sight with blazing eyes and twisted features. “Valerius was a fool. I don’t need him. I can crush Conan without any mortal aid!”  

“Then why have you delayed?” mocked Hadrathus. “Why have you let so many of your allies fall, pierced by arrows and impaled on spears?”  

“Because blood feeds great sorcery!” thundered Xaltotun, his voice making the rocks tremble. A fiery aura glowed around his dreadful head. “Because no wizard wastes his strength foolishly. Because I would save my power for the great days to come, rather than spend it on a skirmish in the hills. But now, by Set, I’ll unleash it to the fullest! Watch, dog of Asura, false priest of a dead god, and see a sight that will shatter your mind forever!”  

Hadrathus threw back his head and laughed, and there was hell in his laughter.  

“Look, black devil of Python!”  

His hand came from beneath his robe holding something that blazed and burned in the sunlight, turning the light into a pulsing golden glow that made Xaltotun’s flesh look like that of a corpse.

Xaltotun cried out as if he had been stabbed.  

“The Heart! The Heart of Ahriman!”  

“Yes! The one power greater than yours!”  

Xaltotun seemed to shrink, to wither, to grow old before their eyes. Suddenly his beard turned white as snow, and gray streaks appeared in his hair.  

“The Heart!” he muttered. “You stole it! You dog! You thief!”  

“Not I! It has traveled far to the south for a long time. But now it’s in my hands, and your dark magic can’t stand against it. Just as it brought you back to life, it will now cast you back into the darkness from which it pulled you. You’ll walk the dark road to Acheron, the road of silence and night. The black empire that never rose again will remain only a legend and a dark memory. Conan will rule once more. And the Heart of Ahriman will return to the cavern beneath the temple of Mitra, to burn as a symbol of Aquilonia’s power for a thousand years!”  

Xaltotun screamed like something not human and rushed around the altar, dagger raised. But from somewhere—maybe the sky, maybe from the great jewel blazing in Hadrathus’s hand—a jet of blinding blue light shot forth. It struck Xaltotun full in the chest, and the hills echoed with the explosion. The wizard of Acheron fell as if struck by lightning, and before he hit the ground, he changed horribly. Beside the altar stone lay not a freshly killed body, but a shriveled mummy—a dry, brown, unrecognizable corpse sprawled among rotting wrappings.  

Old Zelata looked down grimly.  

“He wasn’t truly alive,” she said. “The Heart gave him only the illusion of life, enough to fool even himself. I never saw him as anything but a mummy.”  

Hadrathus bent to untie the fainting girl on the altar when a strange sight appeared among the trees—Xaltotun’s chariot, drawn by those eerie horses. Silently they came forward to the altar and stopped, the wheel almost touching the brown, withered thing on the grass. Hadrathus lifted the wizard’s body and placed it in the chariot. Without hesitation, the ghostly steeds turned and moved off to the south, down the hill. Hadrathus, Zelata, and the gray wolf watched them go—down the long road to Acheron, beyond the reach of mortal men.  

* * * * *  

Down in the valley, Amalric stiffened in his saddle when he saw that wild rider galloping and wheeling on the slopes, waving the bloodstained serpent banner. Then some instinct made him turn his head toward the hill called the King’s Altar. His mouth fell open. Every man in the valley saw it—a blazing column of light shooting up from the hilltop, showering golden fire. High above the armies it burst into a blinding flash that for a moment outshone the sun.  

“That’s not Xaltotun’s signal!” roared the baron.  

“No!” shouted Tarascus. “It’s a signal to the Aquilonians! Look!”  

Above them, the motionless ranks were finally moving, and a deep roar rolled across the valley.  

“Xaltotun has failed us!” bellowed Amalric furiously. “Valerius has failed us! We’ve been led into a trap! Mitra’s curse on Xaltotun for bringing us here! Sound the retreat!”  

“Too late!” yelled Tarascus. “Look!”  

Up on the slopes, the forest of lances dipped and leveled. The ranks of the Gundermen rolled back to the right and left like a curtain being drawn aside. And with a thunder like a rising storm, the knights of Aquilonia charged down the slopes.  

The force of that charge was unstoppable. Bolts from the panicked crossbowmen glanced off their shields and dented helmets. With plumes and pennants streaming behind them, lances lowered, they swept over the wavering lines of pikemen and roared down the hill like a crashing wave.  

Amalric shouted an order to charge, and the Nemedians, desperate and brave, spurred their weary horses uphill. They still outnumbered the attackers.  

But they were exhausted men on tired mounts, charging upward. The oncoming knights hadn’t fought yet that day. Their horses were fresh. They were coming downhill, and they came like a thunderbolt. And like a thunderbolt they struck the struggling Nemedian ranks—smashed them, split them apart, tore them to pieces, and hurled the survivors headlong down the slopes.  

After them came the Gundermen on foot, blood-crazed, and the Bossonians were swarming down the hills, shooting as they ran at every enemy still moving.  

Down the slopes the tide of battle rolled, the stunned Nemedians carried along on its crest. Their archers threw down their crossbows and fled. The few pikemen who survived the knights’ devastating charge were cut down by the relentless Gundermen.  

In wild confusion the battle poured through the wide mouth of the valley and out onto the plain beyond. All over the plain the warriors swarmed—fleeing and pursuing, breaking into single duels and clusters of clashing, hacking knights on rearing, wheeling horses. But the Nemedians were shattered, broken, unable to regroup or make a stand. Hundreds broke away, spurring for the river. Many reached it, crossed, and rode eastward. But the countryside had risen behind them; the people hunted them like wolves. Few ever made it to Tarantia.  

The final collapse came with Amalric’s fall. The baron, trying in vain to rally his men, rode straight at the group of knights following the giant in black armor whose surcoat bore the royal lion, and above whose head flew the golden lion banner beside the scarlet leopard of Poitain. A tall warrior in shining armor lowered his lance and charged to meet the lord of Tor. They met with a crash like thunder. The Nemedian’s lance struck his opponent’s helmet, snapping bolts and rivets and tearing it off, revealing the face of Pallantides. But the Aquilonian’s lance drove through shield and breastplate, piercing the baron’s heart.  

A roar went up as Amalric was thrown from his saddle, breaking the lance that impaled him, and the Nemedians broke apart like a dam bursting under a tidal wave. They fled for the river in a blind panic that swept across the plain like a storm. The Hour of the Dragon was over.

Tarascus did not run. Amalric was dead, the standard-bearer slain, and the royal Nemedian banner trampled into the blood and dust. Most of his knights were fleeing, and the Aquilonians were cutting them down; Tarascus knew the battle was lost, but with a handful of loyal followers he raged through the chaos, driven by one desire—to face Conan the Cimmerian. And at last, he found him.

All formations had completely broken apart, tight ranks shattered and scattered. Trocero’s crest gleamed in one part of the plain, those of Prospero and Pallantides in others. Conan stood alone. The household troops of Tarascus had fallen one by one. The two kings met face to face.

As they charged each other, Tarascus’s horse gave a choking sound and collapsed beneath him. Conan leaped from his own mount and ran toward him as the king of Nemedia freed himself and rose. Steel flashed blindingly in the sunlight, clashed loudly, and blue sparks flew; then came the crash of armor as Tarascus fell full length to the ground beneath a thunderous blow from Conan’s broadsword.

The Cimmerian planted a mail-clad foot on his enemy’s chest and raised his sword. His helmet was gone; he shook back his black hair, and his blue eyes blazed with their old fire.

“Do you surrender?”

“Will you spare my life?” demanded the Nemedian.

“Yes. Better than you would have spared mine, you dog. Life for you and all your men who throw down their weapons. Though I should split your skull for a damned thief,” the Cimmerian added.

Tarascus twisted his neck to look over the plain. The remnants of the Nemedian army were fleeing across the stone bridge, with swarms of victorious Aquilonians at their heels, striking with the fury of vengeance finally satisfied. Bossonians and Gundermen were flooding through the enemy camp, tearing down tents in search of loot, seizing prisoners, ripping open baggage, and overturning wagons.

Tarascus cursed bitterly, then shrugged as best he could under the circumstances.

“Very well. I have no choice. What are your terms?”

“You’ll surrender to me all your current holdings in Aquilonia. Order your garrisons to march out of the castles and towns they occupy, unarmed, and get your damned armies out of Aquilonia as fast as possible. You’ll also return all Aquilonians sold into slavery and pay compensation to be decided later, once the damage your occupation caused has been properly assessed. You’ll stay as a hostage until these terms are fulfilled.”

“Very well,” said Tarascus. “I’ll surrender all the castles and towns my garrisons hold without resistance, and everything else will be done. What ransom for my body?”

Conan laughed, lifted his foot from his enemy’s armored chest, grabbed his shoulder, and hauled him to his feet. He started to speak, then turned as he saw Hadrathus approaching. The priest was calm and composed as ever, stepping carefully between rows of dead men and horses.

Conan wiped the sweat-streaked dust from his face with a blood-smeared hand. He had fought all day—first on foot with the pikemen, then in the saddle, leading the charge. His surcoat was gone, his armor splattered with blood and dented by sword, mace, and axe blows. He loomed huge against a backdrop of blood and slaughter, like some grim pagan hero out of legend.

“Well done, Hadrathus!” he said heartily. “By Crom, I’m glad to see your signal! My knights were nearly mad with impatience, burning to fight. I couldn’t have held them back much longer. What of the wizard?”

“He’s gone down the dark road to Acheron,” answered Hadrathus. “And I—I’m bound for Tarantia. My work here is finished, and I have a duty to perform at the temple of Mitra. All our work here is done. On this field we’ve saved Aquilonia—and more than Aquilonia. Your ride to your capital will be a triumphal procession through a kingdom wild with joy. All Aquilonia will be cheering the return of their king. And so, until we meet again in the great royal hall—farewell!”

Conan stood silently watching the priest depart. From different parts of the field, knights were hurrying toward him. He saw Pallantides, Trocero, Prospero, Servius Galannus, their armor splashed with crimson. The thunder of battle was fading into a roar of triumph and celebration. All eyes, fierce from combat and shining with victory, turned toward the great black figure of the king; armored arms raised blood-stained swords. A deep, thunderous roar rose like the crashing of the sea: “Hail, Conan, king of Aquilonia!”

Tarascus spoke.

“You haven’t yet named my ransom.”

Conan laughed and slid his sword back into its scabbard. He flexed his powerful arms and ran his blood-streaked fingers through his thick black hair, as if feeling his regained crown there.

“There’s a girl in your harem named Zenobia.”

“Why, yes, there is.”

“Good.” The king smiled as if recalling a very pleasant memory. “She’ll be your ransom, and nothing else. I’ll come to Belverus for her, as I promised. She was a slave in Nemedia, but I’ll make her queen of Aquilonia!”
