The knuckles of the tall traveler tightened on his staff, but, like the others, he stepped aside to let the men in armor pass. Among the motley crowd, he drew little notice in his drab, dusty garments. Yet once, as he passed a sword-seller’s stall and the light streaming from its wide doorway fell full upon him, he thought he felt an intense stare. Turning quickly, he saw a man in the brown jerkin of a free workman watching him fixedly. The man turned away with undue haste and vanished into the shifting throng. Conan turned into a narrow by-street and quickened his pace. It might have been idle curiosity—but he could take no chances.  

The grim Iron Tower stood apart from the citadel, amid a maze of narrow streets and crowding houses, where meaner structures, appropriating space from which the more fastidious shrank, had invaded a portion of the city ordinarily alien to them. The Tower was in truth a castle—an ancient, formidable pile of heavy stone and black iron, which had once served as the citadel in an earlier, rougher age.  

Not far from it, lost among a tangle of half-deserted tenements and warehouses, stood an ancient watchtower so old and forgotten that it had not appeared on city maps for a hundred years. Its original purpose was long lost to memory, and none who noticed it at all observed that the seemingly ancient lock barring its door—preventing beggars and thieves from claiming it as shelter—was in fact comparatively new and extremely strong, cunningly disguised to appear rusted and antique. Fewer than half a dozen men in the kingdom had ever known the secret of that tower.  

No keyhole showed in the massive, green-crusted lock. But Conan’s practiced fingers, moving over it, pressed here and there on knobs invisible to the casual eye. The door opened inward without a sound, and he stepped into solid blackness, closing it behind him. A light would have revealed the tower empty—a bare, cylindrical shaft of massive stone.  

Groping in a corner with the sureness of familiarity, he found the projections he sought on a slab of the stone floor. Quickly he lifted it and, without hesitation, lowered himself into the aperture beneath. His feet found stone steps leading downward into a narrow tunnel that ran straight toward the foundations of the Iron Tower, three streets away.  

The bell on the citadel—tolled only at midnight or for the death of a king—boomed suddenly. In a dimly lit chamber of the Iron Tower, a door opened and a figure stepped into a corridor. The interior of the Tower was as forbidding as its exterior. Its massive stone walls were rough and unadorned. The flagstones of the floor were worn deep by generations of faltering feet, and the vaulted ceiling loomed dark in the dim torchlight.  

The man who trudged down that grim corridor matched his surroundings. He was tall and powerfully built, clad in close-fitting black silk. Over his head was drawn a black hood that fell about his shoulders, with two holes for his eyes. A loose black cloak hung from his shoulders, and over one he bore a heavy axe—neither tool nor weapon in shape.  

As he went down the corridor, a figure came hobbling toward him—a bent, surly old man stooping under the weight of his pike and the lantern he carried.  

“You are not as prompt as your predecessor, master headsman,” he grumbled. “Midnight has just struck, and masked men have gone to milady’s cell. They await you.”  

“The tones of the bell still echo among the towers,” answered the executioner. “If I am not so quick to leap and run at the beck of Aquilonians as was the dog who held this office before me, they shall find my arm no less ready. Get to your duties, old watchman, and leave me to mine. I think mine the sweeter trade, by Mitra, for you tramp cold corridors and peer at rusty dungeon doors, while I lop off the fairest head in Tarantia this night.”  

The watchman limped on, still grumbling, and the headsman resumed his leisurely pace. A few strides brought him around a turn in the corridor, where he absently noted a door standing partly open. Had he thought, he would have known it had been opened since the watchman passed—but thinking was not his trade. He was already passing the unlocked door before he sensed anything amiss, and then it was too late.  

A soft, tigerish step and the rustle of a cloak warned him, but before he could turn, a heavy arm hooked around his throat from behind, crushing the cry before it could escape his lips. In the brief instant allowed him, he realized with a surge of panic the strength of his attacker, against which his own brawny thews were helpless. He sensed, without seeing, the poised dagger.  

“Nemedian dog!” muttered a voice thick with passion in his ear. “You’ve cut off your last Aquilonian head!”  

And that was the last thing he ever heard.

In a dank dungeon, lit only by a guttering torch, three men stood over a young woman who knelt on the rush-strewn floor, staring wildly up at them. She was clad only in a scanty shift; her golden hair fell in lustrous ripples over her white shoulders, and her wrists were bound behind her. Even in the uncertain torchlight, despite her disheveled state and pallor of fear, her beauty was striking. She knelt mutely, staring with wide eyes at her tormentors. The men were masked and cloaked—such a deed as this required masks, even in a conquered land. She knew them all nevertheless; but what she knew would harm no one—after that night.  

“Our merciful sovereign offers you one more chance, Countess,” said the tallest of the three, speaking Aquilonian without an accent. “He bids me say that if you soften your proud, rebellious spirit, he will still open his arms to you. If not—” He gestured toward a grim wooden block in the center of the cell. It was blackly stained and deeply notched, as if a keen edge, cutting through some yielding substance, had sunk into the wood.  

Albiona shuddered and turned pale, shrinking back. Every fiber of her vigorous young body quivered with the urge to live. Valerius was young too, and handsome. Many women loved him, she told herself, struggling inwardly for life. But she could not speak the word that would ransom her soft young body from the block and the dripping axe. She could not reason it out; she only knew that when she thought of the clasp of Valerius’ arms, her flesh crawled with an abhorrence greater than the fear of death. She shook her head helplessly, driven by an impulse more irresistible than the instinct to live.
