Part of the city was dedicated to the temples. The great structures loomed, their black masses rearing against the dim stars—grim, indescribably menacing in the flare of a few torches. Suddenly, he heard a low scream from a woman on the other side of the street, somewhat ahead of him—a naked courtesan wearing the tall plumed headdress of her class. She was shrinking back against the wall, staring across at something he could not yet see. At her cry, the few people on the street halted as if frozen. At that instant, Conan became aware of a sinister slithering ahead of him. Then, around the dark corner of the building he was approaching, a hideous wedge-shaped head emerged, followed by coil after coil of rippling, darkly glistening trunk.  

The Cimmerian recoiled, remembering tales he had heard—serpents were sacred to Set, god of Stygia, who men said was himself a serpent. Monsters such as this were kept in the temples of Set, and when they hungered, they were allowed to crawl forth into the streets to take whatever prey they wished. Their ghastly feasts were considered sacrifices to the scaly god.  

The Stygians within Conan’s sight fell to their knees, men and women alike, passively awaiting their fate. One the great serpent would select, enfold in scaly coils, crush to a red pulp, and swallow as a rat-snake swallows a mouse. The others would live. That was the will of the gods.  

But it was not Conan’s will. The python glided toward him, its attention likely drawn by the fact that he was the only human still standing. Gripping his great knife beneath his mantle, Conan hoped the slimy brute would pass him by. But it halted before him and reared up horrifically in the flickering torchlight, its forked tongue darting in and out, its cold eyes glittering with the ancient cruelty of the serpent-folk. Its neck arched—but before it could strike, Conan whipped his knife from beneath his mantle and struck like lightning. The broad blade split the wedge-shaped head and sheared deep into the thick neck.  

Conan wrenched his knife free and sprang clear as the great body knotted, looped, and whipped terrifically in its death throes. For a moment he stood staring in morbid fascination; the only sound was the thud and swish of the snake’s tail against the stones.  

Then from the shocked votaries burst a terrible cry: “Blasphemer! He has slain the sacred son of Set! Slay him! Slay! Slay!”  

Stones whizzed about him as the crazed Stygians rushed forward, shrieking hysterically. From all sides others emerged from their houses and took up the cry. With a curse, Conan wheeled and darted into the black mouth of an alley. He heard the patter of bare feet on the flags behind him as he ran more by feel than by sight, the walls echoing with the vengeful yells of his pursuers. Then his left hand found a break in the wall, and he turned sharply into another, narrower alley. On both sides rose sheer black stone walls. High above, he could see a thin line of stars. These giant walls, he knew, were the walls of temples. He heard behind him the pack sweep past the dark mouth in full cry. Their shouts grew distant, fading away—they had missed the smaller alley and run straight on into the blackness. He too kept straight ahead, though the thought of encountering another of Set’s “sons” in the darkness sent a shudder through him.  

Then, somewhere ahead, he caught a moving glow, like that of a crawling glowworm. He halted, flattened himself against the wall, and gripped his knife. He knew what it was: a man approaching with a torch. Soon it was close enough for him to make out the dark hand that held it and the dim oval of a dark face. A few more steps and the man would certainly see him. Conan sank into a tigerish crouch—the torch halted. A door was briefly outlined in the glow as the torchbearer fumbled with it. Then it opened, the tall figure vanished through it, and darkness closed again over the alley. There was a sinister suggestion of furtiveness about that slinking figure entering the alley door in darkness—a priest, perhaps, returning from some dark errand.  

Conan groped toward the door. If one man came up that alley with a torch, others might come at any time. To retreat the way he had come might mean running straight into the mob from which he fled. At any moment they might return, find the narrower alley, and come howling down it. Hemmed in by those sheer, unscalable walls, he longed for escape—even if it meant invading some unknown building.  

The heavy bronze door was not locked. It opened under his fingers, and he peered through the crack. He looked into a great square chamber of massive black stone. A torch smoldered in a niche in the wall. The chamber was empty. He glided through the lacquered door and closed it behind him.  

His sandaled feet made no sound as he crossed the black marble floor. A teak door stood partly open, and gliding through it, knife in hand, he entered a vast, dim, shadowy place whose lofty ceiling was only a hint of darkness high above, where the black walls swept upward. On all sides, black-arched doorways opened into the great still hall. It was lit by curious bronze lamps that cast a dim, eerie glow. On the far side of the hall, a broad black marble stairway without railing ascended to lose itself in gloom, and above him, on all sides, dim galleries hung like black stone ledges.  

Conan shivered; he was in a temple of some Stygian god—if not Set himself, then one scarcely less grim. And the shrine was not unoccupied. In the midst of the great hall stood a black stone altar, massive and somber, without carvings or ornament, and upon it coiled one of the great sacred serpents, its iridescent scales shimmering in the lamplight. It did not move, and Conan remembered tales that the priests kept these creatures drugged part of the time. The Cimmerian took an uncertain step out from the door, then shrank back suddenly—not into the room he had just left, but into a velvet-curtained recess. He had heard a soft step somewhere nearby.
