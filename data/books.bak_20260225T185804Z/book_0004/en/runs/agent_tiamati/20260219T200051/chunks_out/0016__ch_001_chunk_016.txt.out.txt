Kissing his hands, she sprang lithely up and ran down the corridor, vanishing through a heavy double door.  

He glanced after her, wondering if he was a fool to trust her; then he shrugged his mighty shoulders and drew the satin hangings together, concealing his refuge. It was not strange that a passionate young beauty should risk her life to aid him; such things had happened often enough before. Many women had looked upon him with favor, both in the days of his wanderings and during his kingship.  

Yet he did not remain motionless in the alcove, waiting for her return. Following his instincts, he explored the niche for another exit and soon found one—the opening of a narrow passage, hidden by the tapestries, leading to an ornately carved door barely visible in the dim light filtering in from the outer corridor. As he stared into it, he heard, somewhere beyond that carved door, the sound of another door opening and closing, followed by a low murmur of voices. The familiar tone of one of those voices brought a sinister expression to his dark face. Without hesitation, he glided down the passage and crouched like a stalking panther beside the door. It was not locked, and manipulating it delicately, he pushed it open a crack—with a reckless disregard for possible consequences that only he could have explained or defended.  

It was masked on the other side by tapestries, but through a thin slit in the velvet he looked into a chamber lit by a candle on an ebony table. Two men were there. One was a scarred, sinister-looking ruffian in leather breeks and a ragged cloak; the other was Tarascus, king of Nemedia.  

Tarascus seemed ill at ease. He was slightly pale and kept starting and glancing about him, as if expecting and fearing to hear some sound or footstep.  

“Go swiftly and at once,” he was saying. “He is deep in drugged slumber, but I know not when he may awaken.”  

“Strange to hear words of fear issuing from the lips of Tarascus,” rumbled the other in a harsh, deep voice.  

The king frowned.  

“I fear no common man, as you well know. But when I saw the cliffs fall at Valkia, I knew that this devil we had resurrected was no charlatan. I fear his powers because I do not know their full extent. Yet I know they are somehow connected with this accursed thing I have stolen from him. It brought him back to life; therefore it must be the source of his sorcery.  

“He had it well hidden, but by my secret order a slave spied on him and saw him place it in a golden chest, and saw where he hid the chest. Even so, I would not have dared steal it had Xaltotun himself not been sunk in lotus slumber.  

“I believe it is the secret of his power. With it, Orastes brought him back to life. With it, he will make us all slaves if we are not wary. So take it and cast it into the sea as I have bidden you. Be sure you are so far from land that neither tide nor storm can wash it upon the beach. You have been paid.”  

“So I have,” grunted the ruffian. “And I owe more than gold to you, king; I owe you a debt of gratitude. Even thieves can be grateful.”  

“Whatever debt you feel you owe me,” answered Tarascus, “will be paid when you have hurled this thing into the sea.”  

“I’ll ride for Zingara and take ship from Kordava,” promised the other. “I dare not show my head in Argos because of a murder or two—”  

“I care not, so it is done. Here it is; a horse awaits you in the court. Go, and go swiftly!”  

Something passed between them—something that flamed like living fire. Conan caught only a brief glimpse of it before the ruffian pulled a slouch hat over his eyes, drew his cloak about his shoulders, and hurried from the chamber. As the door closed behind him, Conan moved with the devastating fury of unchained bloodlust. He had held himself in check as long as he could. The sight of his enemy so near set his wild blood seething and swept away all caution and restraint.  

Tarascus was turning toward an inner door when Conan tore aside the hangings and leaped like a blood-mad panther into the room. Tarascus wheeled, but even before he could recognize his attacker, Conan’s poniard ripped into him.  

But the blow was not mortal, as Conan knew the instant he struck. His foot had caught in a fold of the curtains and tripped him as he leaped. The point sank into Tarascus’s shoulder and plowed down along his ribs, and the king of Nemedia screamed.  

The impact of the blow and Conan’s lunging body hurled him back against the table; it toppled, and the candle went out. Both men were carried to the floor by the violence of Conan’s rush, and the foot of the tapestry entangled them in its folds. Conan stabbed blindly in the dark, Tarascus screaming in a frenzy of panicked terror. As if fear lent him superhuman strength, Tarascus tore free and blundered away in the darkness, shrieking: “Help! Guards! Arideus! Orastes! Orastes!”  

Conan rose, kicking himself free of the tangling tapestries and the broken table, cursing with the bitterness of his bloodthirsty disappointment. He was confused and ignorant of the palace’s layout. The yells of Tarascus still echoed in the distance, and a wild outcry was rising in answer. The Nemedian had escaped him in the darkness, and Conan did not know which way he had gone. His rash stroke for vengeance had failed, and now only the task of saving his own hide remained.  

Swearing luridly, Conan ran back down the passage into the alcove, glaring out into the lighted corridor just as Zenobia came running up, her dark eyes wide with terror.  

“Oh, what has happened?” she cried. “The palace is roused! I swear I have not betrayed you—”  

“No, it was I who stirred up this hornet’s nest,” he grunted. “I tried to pay off a score. What’s the shortest way out of this?”  

She caught his wrist and ran swiftly down the corridor. But before they reached the heavy door at the other end, muffled shouts arose from behind it, and the portals began to shake under an assault from the other side. Zenobia wrung her hands and whimpered.
