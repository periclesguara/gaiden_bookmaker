## Introduction

Conan the Conqueror: The Hour of the Dragon is not merely a novel. It is a monument of modern mythmaking.

Published in 1935, at a time when the world trembled between collapse and transformation, this work stands as Robert E. Howard’s only full-length Conan novel — and arguably the most politically and psychologically mature expression of his heroic vision.

Howard was born in 1906, into a world already accelerating beyond recognition. By the time he reached adulthood, the old order had fractured. Empires had fallen. The First World War had mechanized death on an unprecedented scale. The Russian Revolution had shattered monarchies. Electricity illuminated cities that rose vertically into steel and concrete. Telegraph wires stretched across continents. Radios carried voices into private homes. Automobiles replaced horses. Airplanes conquered the sky.

Within a single generation, humanity had leapt from oil lamps to global communication.

The New York Stock Exchange collapsed in 1929. Political extremism surged. Nazism and fascism emerged from the wreckage of economic despair. The world no longer felt stable. It felt volatile — dangerous — transitional.

Howard absorbed this atmosphere deeply.

Conan is not simply a barbarian. He is the archetype of the solitary man standing against decay. He embodies strength in a collapsing world. He represents willpower against corruption, clarity against conspiracy, vitality against decadence.

In The Hour of the Dragon, Howard goes further than in his earlier Conan tales. This is not just a story of swordplay — it is a meditation on power, legitimacy, kingship, betrayal, and survival. Dark sorcery operates behind political intrigue. Hidden forces manipulate visible power. Kingdoms rot from within.

The novel captures the anxiety of a civilization sensing its own fragility.

In retrospect, it is impossible not to see how these themes resonated with the coming global conflict. Within a few short years, millions of men would be called to arms in the Second World War. The archetype of the lone soldier, the man forced to stand against overwhelming forces, would cease to be myth and become reality.

Howard’s Hyborian Age is fictional — but its emotional climate was not.

This edition presents the novel in a carefully modernized English form, preserving Howard’s intensity while refining archaic constructions for clarity and flow. The goal is not alteration, but accessibility — to allow contemporary readers to experience the full force of this epic without linguistic barriers.

More than a fantasy novel, this work stands as a cultural artifact of a world on the brink — and as a testament to the enduring power of heroic myth.
